The Catalogue

Every schedule the research names, in five families. Each entry gives the notation, the definition in the words of the source that defined it, how the schedule is programmed, what it does to behavior, where the applied literature has used it, what goes wrong, and a school example. The last line of each entry is a notes box for this program’s own use of the schedule; the notes save with the form. Open an entry by clicking its name; print opens them all.

What the Records Look Like

Stylized cumulative records: time runs left to right, each response steps the line up, and each short diagonal mark is a reinforcer. They are drawn by rule from the descriptions in Ferster and Skinner (1957) and the sources in the catalogue, not traced from data, and they show the shape to expect from each schedule once it has taken hold. Human performances with verbal rules can look flatter than these (Catania, Matthews & Shimoff, 1982).

Reading a Record

SlopeRate. A steep line is a high rate; a flat line is a pause. Compare slopes, not heights.
Pause after the markThe post-reinforcement pause of fixed schedules: the flat stretch right after each reinforcer, longer as the requirement grows.
Curve into the markThe scallop of fixed-interval schedules: a gradual steepening as the time approaches, then the reinforcer.
Straight line through the marksVariable schedules: no feature of the schedule predicts the next reinforcer, so nothing pauses.
Steps of equal height, widely spacedSpaced-responding DRL: single responses separated by at least the criterion time, each one paid.
Runs with growing gaps, then a flat lineA progressive ratio approaching its breakpoint, or a fixed ratio under strain.
Steep, then flattening to nothingExtinction: the burst, then the decline; the flattening is slower after an intermittent history.

Reading the Notation

LettersF fixed, V variable, R ratio (responses), I interval (time), T time (no response required). FR 10: the tenth response pays. VI 60 s: the first response after an average of 60 seconds pays. FT 30 s: delivery every 30 seconds whatever happens.
Numbers and unitsRatios count responses; intervals and times are in seconds or minutes, always stated. Ferster and Skinner’s convention is minutes unless noted; the applied literature usually writes the unit.
DR-Differential reinforcement of: O other (absence), L low rates, H high rates, P paced responding, A alternative, I incompatible, D diminishing rates, C communication (FCT), V variability (lag). The letter after DR names what is paid, and what is paid is what grows.
Combined schedulesmult (signaled alternation), mix (unsignaled alternation), chain (sequence with a stimulus change), tand (sequence without one), conc (two responses at once), conjt (two schedules on one response), alt (either requirement), conj (both requirements), interlock (a requirement that changes with time), second order written as outer(inner), e.g., FI 5 (FR 10).
ModifiersLH limited hold (a window after set-up); resetting or non-resetting (DRO); whole-interval or momentary (DRO); signaled or unsignaled (delays); progressive (a parameter that changes across reinforcers).

Where the Catalogue Comes From

The definitions of the basic and combined schedules are Ferster and Skinner’s (1957), quoted from the B. F. Skinner Foundation e-book of the text, and Catania’s (2007) two tables of basic schedules and schedule combinations as reproduced in DeLeon, Bullock and Catania (2013). Zeiler (1977) is the classic analysis of what controls behavior under schedules; Lattal and Neef (1996) survey the schedule research that bears on applied work. The differential-reinforcement entries follow Vollmer and Iwata (1992) for the family and the Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis studies named in each entry for the variants. The applied entries draw on the thinning literature after functional communication training (Hanley et al., 2001; Hagopian et al., 2011; Greer et al., 2016), the token literature (Hackenberg, 2009, 2018; Ivy et al., 2017), and the two chapters on arranging reinforcement by DeLeon and colleagues (2013, 2014). Where an entry cites a study through one of those reviews rather than directly, it says so.

What the Chooser and the Designer Assume

DRO starting intervalThe mean inter-response time from baseline (minutes observed ÷ occurrences), times the share chosen (75% by default): a value the student already meets most of the time (Poling & Ryan, 1982; Vollmer & Iwata, 1992). Steps increase the interval by the chosen percentage until the terminal value; the criterion and the step-back rule are written by the user.
DRL limitsThe first full-session limit is the baseline count scaled to the session length, rounded down to a whole number; the limits then fall in equal steps to the target share of baseline. Spaced-responding criteria start at the baseline mean IRT and lengthen by the same factor each step.
Variable seriesFleshler and Hoffman (1962): the nth of N intervals with mean T is T[1 + ln N + (N − n) ln(N − n) − (N − n + 1) ln(N − n + 1)], which keeps the probability of reinforcement nearly constant through the interval (Catania & Reynolds, 1968). The arithmetic series is 2T/(N + 1), 4T/(N + 1) … 2NT/(N + 1), shuffled. Both average T. Random schedules state the probability per cycle (RI) or per response (RR) instead.
NCRThe first interval is the baseline mean IRT, from the last three baseline sessions (the start of Kahng et al., 2000; Vollmer et al., 1993, began with near-continuous attention), or the time to the first problem behavior in baseline (Lalli et al., 1997). Four ways to thin. Two are published (Kahng et al., 2000): fixed increments (FT 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30 s, then 1 to 5 min, a step after any session at or under the criterion) and the adjusting IRT (before each session, the mean IRT of the last three sessions, never more than double the interval in force); the adjusting IRT reached FT 5 min in 6, 11 and 15 sessions, fixed increments in 10, 15 and 25. Two are the form’s own conventions: doubling the interval each day the criterion holds, and performance-based doubling with a step back. A session with no problem behavior counts as the session length, and the adjusting interval never falls below the one in force: both conventions. The omission rule delays a delivery (the form’s convention) or cancels it, as in the momentary DRO of Vollmer et al. (1997). Variable time was as effective as fixed time (Van Camp et al., 2000; Carr et al., 2001) and held up better under missed deliveries in a human-operant study (Jones et al., 2025).
Multiple-schedule thinningS+ held constant while S− is multiplied by the factor until the terminal value, after Hanley et al. (2001) and Greer et al. (2016), or set so that the share of the session the request works drops by at most a set percentage per step. Each step shows that share (S+ ÷ (S+ + S−)) and the drop from the step before; a drop above 33% is flagged, because the size of the drop went with how large resurgence was (Laureano et al., 2024) and steps of 20% or less made large resurgence less likely (Greer et al., 2024). The evidence is divided: a jump to the terminal value after a one-session probe gave less resurgence in a two-group comparison (Strohmeier et al., 2024), so the form shows and flags large steps and does not forbid them. The terminal S− comes from the longest wait in the classroom or home when it is written (Saini et al., 2016). The default criterion asks for at least 80% of requests made while the S+ is up (Greer et al., 2016, used at least 70% correct requests with a 90% reduction); the signal check asks whether the student can match and name the two signals (Pizarro et al., 2021). A natural signal (the teacher busy or free) is reached through arranged signals, which were learned faster and generalized better for 2 of 3 participants (Shamlian et al., 2016; Kuhn et al., 2010). The criteria are the user’s, with the chooser’s defaults as working conventions.
The extinction stepFor NCR, DRO, multiple-schedule thinning, chained schedules, waits and FCT or DRA without extinction, the Design page asks what follows problem behavior, with no default: an extinction step agreed by the team and parent (with the date), extinction only partly possible, or no extinction. The card prints the line under its own heading, and “+ EXT” in the notation only when the step is chosen. It is a real choice with costs on both sides: FCT without extinction reached a 90% reduction in none of 11 applications, and with extinction in 11 of 25 (Hagopian et al., 1998); extinction alone brought bursts in 62% and new aggression in some cases of self-injury (Lerman et al., 1999). With the step chosen the card says what to expect: bursts in about 1 in 4 treatments (Lerman & Iwata, 1995; Muething et al., 2024), defined as any of the first three sessions higher than every one of the last five baseline sessions (Lerman & Iwata, 1995), usually back to baseline by about the fifth session (Muething et al., 2024).
FCT or DRA without extinctionFive rows compare what problem behavior gets now with what the request gets: how often, how soon, how much, how good and how hard. Seconds on both sides decide how soon and how much; the user says who wins the other rows. The form warns when the request loses or ties on a row and when it wins on fewer than three: without extinction, change was largest and most consistent when several dimensions favored the alternative (Athens & Vollmer, 2010), and during thinning 3 of 4 participants needed a larger and a better reinforcer (Briggs et al., 2019). The same rows, filled from baseline, check that a request starts at least as good as what problem behavior got: a model prediction (Fisher et al., 2023). The matching worksheet uses strict matching (Herrnstein, 1961); in classrooms matching was partial (Mace et al., 1994).
WaitsA wait ends when the time is up and the student is doing the tolerance response, the activity or the tasks; a time-only wait is marked “not recommended alone”. Waits that ended on what the student did gave lower problem behavior for all 4 participants (Ghaemmaghami et al., 2016), and requiring an alternative response gave the best tolerance (Drifke et al., 2020). Multiple and chained schedules come first (Hanley et al., 2001; Rooker et al., 2013). The wait multiplies by the factor each step until the longest wait, a working convention.
When thinning stallsEven with good thinning, many programs need an added component. FCT reached a 90% reduction in 59% of 58 applications, and the effect held through thinning in 11 of 23 (48%); FCT with alternative reinforcement reached it in 10 of 14 (Rooker et al., 2013). With signaled thinning, 73% held without additions (Greer et al., 2016). So the card writes the next step before it is needed: after two step-backs at the same step, add an alternative reinforcer or competing items, check the reinforcer’s size and quality, and check integrity. Punishment is not that step: it stays a separate restrictive option that needs approval.
Step rulesThis form’s placeholders step after 2 consecutive sessions and step back after 2 sessions above 20% of baseline; Form TD-1 steps after 3 consecutive sessions and back after two above criterion. Both are working conventions, not findings. Hagopian et al. (2004) stepped after two consecutive sessions at an 80% to 90% reduction; Rooker et al. (2013) thinned once a reduction of more than 80% held; Kahng et al. (2000) stepped after one session at or below 0.5 per minute; Saini et al. (2016) recommend a fallback criterion and a reduction criterion set to the case, with 100% for severe aggression toward peers. The BCBA sets the rule on each card.
Data sheetsThe card lists the data to keep; the sheets are the workstation’s: Form DD-1 for counts, intervals and sessions (DRO intervals run and earned, DRL counts against the limit, NCR deliveries, reinforcers per session, PR breakpoints, tokens and exchanges), Form SM-1 for the interlocking session sheet, and Form MT-1 for interval samples (momentary and variable-momentary DRO checks, the S+ / S− periods of a multiple schedule). The card’s Data sheet used line names them when it is left blank.
Token pricesResponses per back-up = tokens per back-up × responses per token. The designer warns when an exchange costs more than 50 responses (tokens per exchange × responses per token), a working convention after Bullock and Hackenberg (2006) as reported in DeLeon et al. (2013).
