Why contrast is expected

Contrast is larger in a setting close in time to the changed one, especially the one just before it. It has been obtained with components as much as 23 hours apart (Lerman & Iwata, 1996). With adults across three components, positive contrast was more likely, or larger, in the component just before the changed one than in the one just after it (Boyle et al., 2016, as reviewed in Boyle, Hoffmann, & Lambert, 2018).

Interaction, and Whether It Is Contrast

Does It Persist Across Sessions?

Nevin and Shettleworth (1966) called rate changes within a component transient contrast and rate changes across successive schedule cycles sustained contrast; one rate per session can show only the second, so this table follows its course across the sessions of the changed phase. The blocks are the first and last third of that phase (at least one session each), each compared with the constant component’s baseline mean, and a change of 10% or more counts as present. Persisting: present in both blocks, the same way. Declining: present in both, the last block’s change under 60% of the first’s. Faded: present in the first block only. Late onset: in the last block only. Reversed: present in both, in opposite directions. Terrace (1966) found that contrast and the peak shift both disappeared during extended discrimination training, and Bloomfield (1966) found a peak at an intermediate stage of discrimination training that declined, later in training, to a stable level still above the baseline rate.

An increase in the untreated setting is not automatically contrast. Work through these before reporting it as such.

The Definitions and Their Sources

Reynolds (1961a) named two things. An interaction is a change in behavior during one stimulus brought about by changing the schedule associated with a different stimulus. The interaction is a contrast when the change in the rate of responding during one stimulus is in a direction away from the rate of responding during the other stimulus. A change in the same direction as the change during the other stimulus is now called induction, not contrast, although Pavlov (1927, as cited in Reynolds, 1961a) had used “induction” for a contrast effect.

This form tests direction the way the rows below define it: the change in the constant component is compared with the change in reinforcement in the changed component, recorded on the setup sheet as less, about the same or more, because contrast follows the change in reinforcement there rather than the change in its rate (Reynolds, 1961a; see “It follows reinforcement, not the rate there” below). What the plan did there (extinction, DRA or FCT, thinning, punishment, NCR, DRO) is recorded beside it and does not set the direction: extinction with a break card honored on FR 1 can leave the reinforcer as frequent as before. A change of 10% or more against that change (a rise when the reinforcement there became less, a fall when it became more) is read as consistent with contrast, one the other way as induction, and a smaller one as no appreciable interaction. From a baseline mean of zero the change is read on the rate itself: any rate above zero after the change is a rise. Before any of these is said, the constant component’s data are checked the way visual analysis checks them (Kratochwill et al., 2010): the baseline’s trend (a least-squares line over the baseline sessions) is carried over the sessions after the change, and if it already reaches their mean the change is not called an interaction; and if fewer than half of the sessions after the change lie beyond the whole baseline range in the direction of the change, it is not called one either. Whether the change was immediate (the last three baseline sessions against the first three after) is reported beside it. A row whose phase disagrees with its date and the date of the change is listed under the verdict. The changed component’s own rate is a check that the plan took hold, and stands in for the record only when it is left blank and the rate moved 10% or more. The comparison needs at least three sessions in each phase, since a phase of fewer than three data points cannot show an effect, and it is a two-phase (AB) comparison, from which causal inferences are difficult to draw (Kratochwill et al., 2010). It is the difference between a plan producing spillover benefit and a plan producing a problem somewhere else, and the two are distinguished by sign rather than by impression.

Positive contrastThe constant component rises as the changed component delivers less reinforcement — reinforcement thinned or withheld with nothing in its place, or punishment added.
Negative contrastThe constant component falls as the changed component delivers more reinforcement.
InductionThe constant component moves in the same direction as the change in reinforcement in the changed component: it falls as reinforcement there becomes less and rises as it becomes more. Reported here rather than discarded, because a plan that reduces behavior in both settings is worth documenting.
Transient contrastNevin and Shettleworth (1966) used this for rate changes within a constant component, following the preceding component: rate in the constant component was high and fell with time when preceded by extinction, low and rose with time when preceded by more frequent reinforcement. Williams (2002) calls contrast after a component transition local contrast and concludes that it contributes to molar (overall) contrast under some conditions, but not generally.
Sustained contrastRate changes across successive schedule cycles (Nevin & Shettleworth, 1966): the change in a component’s overall rate. It need not last. Terrace (1966) showed both contrast and the peak shift disappearing during extended discrimination training, which he suggested could be attributed to the dissipation of emotional responses that resulted from response suppression. Bloomfield (1966) found a peak at an intermediate stage of discrimination training that declined, later in training, to a stable level still above the baseline rate; the peak correlated with the number of responses to S− and the final rate did not.

What Matters for a School Case

In a schoolRecord the untreated setting on the same days as the treated one, with the same definition and period (the setup sheet prints a tally sheet for its staff, who should change nothing about how they respond while it is counted). With three or more sessions in each phase the analysis reads the verdict; a verdict consistent with positive contrast is a reason to extend the plan to that setting and to run this form again (Re-check due), not proof of what caused the rise. Practitioners surveyed by Boyle, Uribe-Zarain, and Powell (2023) reported that contrast led to conversations with stakeholders and affected buy-in, so tell the staff of both settings what is being counted and why. Do it before the plan starts: record who was told on the setup sheet, and print the notice for parents and teachers with the tally sheet.
What a component can beA component can be a type of task within a session (for example, new tasks and review tasks), or a person, as well as a setting. Applied contrast studies have measured behavior to increase as well as behavior to decrease: work rate across three teachers (Kistner et al., 1982); learning new tasks interspersed with review tasks, where all five children learned the new tasks when food was given only for them, which the authors suggest may have been a contrast effect (Charlop et al., 1992); and opposition at home and at school, with a parent and a teacher as the components (Wahler et al., 2004). Say on the setup sheet whether the target is a behavior to decrease or to increase, and the verdicts are worded to match.
It does not need extinctionContrast occurs when the other component merely programs fewer reinforcers rather than none (Findley, 1958, and Schuster, 1960, as cited in Reynolds, 1961a). A plan that thins reinforcement in one setting can produce contrast in another without any extinction component.
It follows reinforcement, not the rate thereWhen reinforcement for not responding produced very low rates of responding in one component but maintained a high frequency of food presentations, the increase in the other component was eliminated (Reynolds, 1961a). For this form: a fall in the changed component’s rate is not by itself less reinforcement there, which is why the setup sheet asks how much reinforcement the setting delivers after the change. When DRA or FCT keeps the reinforcer coming as often as before, positive contrast is not expected on this account, and a rise in the untreated setting points first to the behavior still working there.
Resurgence is close kinLattal and Miles (2024) conclude that, with certain caveats, resurgence appears to be an instance of behavioral contrast measured against an extinction baseline (see the resurgence row on the analysis sheet).
Target responding need not be near zeroResurgence is assessed against an extinction baseline of the target response, whereas contrast has been assessed under a variety of reinforcement schedules (Lattal & Miles, 2024), so an increase in the constant component does not need the behavior there to have been reduced or extinguished first.
What the applied studies foundContrast is possible in a school but not to be expected. In a classroom token economy brought in across three teachers, half of the six children showed no contrast and the other half only mild, short-lived effects; none showed it consistently (Kistner et al., 1982). In one child, opposition at school rose as opposition at home fell, and it was not correlated with the teacher’s inappropriate attention (r = .03); the authors still did not claim contrast, because their single record of the teacher’s attention to both cooperation and opposition could not show that reinforcement at school stayed the same (Wahler et al., 2004). In a multiple-baseline FCT study, the settings not yet treated showed no contrast (Fisher et al., 2015, as read by Boyle, Hoffmann, & Lambert, 2018). Among practitioners, 84% had had a stakeholder report contrast, and 51% called it rare or very rare (Boyle, Uribe-Zarain, & Powell, 2023). Contrast has remained largely unstudied by applied researchers (Boyle, Hoffmann, & Lambert, 2018), and researchers had not identified common methods of mitigating it in applied settings (Boyle, Uribe-Zarain, & Powell, 2023).
Reinforcement there must be held constantContrast can be read only when reinforcement in the setting that did not change stays the same. If the student earns more reinforcers there as the rate rises, a sustained increase may be “nothing more than the direct effect of reinforcement” (Boyle, Hoffmann, & Lambert, 2018). That is why the tally sheet records what happened right after each occurrence, and the Data sheet takes the share followed by the reinforcer named on Form FS-1. When that share is 10 points or more higher after the change than in baseline, the analysis does not call a rise there contrast (this form’s convention).
A gain that spreads may not lastA gain that spreads to another setting often fades there unless something maintains it. Responding generalized for two of three children and then extinguished outside therapy; thinner schedules in treatment and occasional free reinforcers in the other setting kept it going (Koegel & Rincover, 1977). Classroom gains were kept by making the regular classroom resemble the treatment classroom or by reprogramming peers; teacher training alone did no better than no maintenance strategy (Walker & Buckley, 1972). When the verdict is a spread of improvement, the analysis sets the re-check 4 weeks after the last session, if none is set; re-check in 4 to 6 weeks.
Contrast across successive phases (context only)A milder consequence or a smaller reinforcer can work worse after a stronger one than it would have alone. Change one step at a time, and read the next phase against its own baseline. A 1-minute timeout suppressed behavior when it came first, but less reliably after 15- and 30-minute timeouts (White et al., 1972), which Burchard and Barrera (1972) cite as a contrast effect; the same pattern with reinforcer quality is called incentive contrast (Boyle, Hoffmann, & Lambert, 2018). Timeout is punishment: this is context for reading data, not a recommendation to use it.
Laboratory background (not needed for a school case)
Interresponse-time accounts are in doubtReynolds and Catania (1961) obtained contrast with fixed-interval and with DRL schedules, which they argued casts doubt on accounts depending on selective reinforcement or punishment of different interresponse times.
Schedule type mattersBloomfield (1967) found fixed-ratio components susceptible to contrast while low-rate (DRL) components were not. What is running in the constant component affects whether contrast shows up there at all.
Not only multiple schedulesCatania (1961) obtained contrast in concurrent as well as multiple schedules.
