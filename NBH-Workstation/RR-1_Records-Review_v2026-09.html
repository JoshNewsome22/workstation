Records Review for Functional Behavior Assessment

A structured review of the existing record before any new assessment begins: which documents were examined, what the IEP and eligibility history show, what has already been tried and with what result, what the discipline, incident, attendance, and health records reveal about when and where behavior occurs, and what the records suggest about function. The sheet ends with a records-based hypothesis for each target behavior and a list of the gaps that interviews, observation, and analysis must fill. It also documents the existing evaluation data for the IEP team's review (34 CFR §300.305(a)).

Guide: What a Records Review Contributes and What It Cannot

The record is the cheapest source of information about a behavior and the one most often skipped. It already contains dates, places, times, activities, and administrative consequences for every referral; the interventions that have been tried and how they were documented; the eligibility and communication profile that constrains what a replacement behavior can be; and the health events that act as setting events. Read first, it sharpens every interview question and tells the observer where and when to look. Read alone, it proves nothing about function, because referrals record what an adult did afterward, not what the behavior produced. Evidence boxes report what the cited sources say; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses.

1. The Records Review as Review of Existing Evaluation Data

EvidenceUnder 34 CFR §300.305(a), as part of an initial evaluation (if appropriate) and any reevaluation, the IEP team and other qualified professionals, as appropriate, must review existing evaluation data on the child, including evaluations and information provided by the parents, current classroom-based, local, or State assessments and classroom-based observations, and observations by teachers and related services providers, and, on the basis of that review and input from the child's parents, identify what additional data, if any, are needed. Under §300.306(c)(1)(i) eligibility and educational-need decisions must draw on a variety of sources, including aptitude and achievement tests, parent input, teacher recommendations, and physical condition, social or cultural background, and adaptive behavior. Florida Rule 6A-6.0331(8) states the same requirement for an initial evaluation (if appropriate) and any reevaluation. Under FERPA (34 CFR §99.10) the parent, or the eligible student, must be given the opportunity to inspect and review the student's education records, within a reasonable period and no more than 45 days after a request; under IDEA (34 CFR §300.613(a)) the school must permit the parent to inspect and review the education records it collects, maintains, or uses for the child, without unnecessary delay and before any meeting regarding an IEP.
Applied guidanceThis form is completed before IA-1 interviews and ABC-1 observation and is dated so the file shows the review preceded new data collection. The Sources sheet is the inventory: every document examined, its date, where it was found, and what it contributed. A record that could not be located is listed as not available so the gap is visible. The Synthesis sheet closes with the §300.305 question: are the existing data sufficient, and if not, which additional assessments are proposed? That determination belongs to the IEP team; the BCBA's recommendation is recorded here.

2. Records as a Functional-Assessment Method

EvidenceFunctional assessment texts treat record review as the first of the indirect methods. The Functional Assessment Interview of O'Neill et al. (2015) begins with a description of the behaviors and goes on to the setting events that predict them, the primary ways the person communicates, and the history of the behaviors, the programs tried to decrease them, and their effects; Steege et al. (2019) describe the review of records as an early step in school-based FBA that identifies previously documented antecedents, consequences, and intervention outcomes. Hanley (2012) cautions that indirect methods, including records and interviews, are best treated as sources of hypotheses to be tested rather than as conclusions. Office discipline referrals have documented value as a measure of school-wide behavioral status (Irvin et al., 2004), and for individual students the number of referrals early in the year moderately predicts the year's total (McIntosh et al., 2010), but a referral captures the adult's decision to write it, not every occurrence of the behavior, and it lists a perceived "motivation" only as the referring adult's guess.
Applied guidanceReferral and incident data are entered by date, behavior, location, time block, activity, and administrative action so the form can tally where and when incidents cluster and what typically followed them. A cluster in academic blocks with removal as the typical consequence is a hypothesis about escape, not a finding; a cluster in unstructured settings with adult conference as the consequence is a hypothesis about attention. Each hypothesis is carried to the Synthesis sheet with its source named, and to IA-1 and ABC-1 as questions.

3. Intervention History

EvidenceA treatment that failed tells the analyst something only if its function match and integrity are known. For two middle school students, function-based plans reduced problem behavior more than non-function-based plans (Ingram, Lewis-Palmer, & Sugai, 2005). On integrity, the compliance of two preschool children varied with the integrity of a three-step prompting procedure (Wilder, Atwell, & Wine, 2006), and three participants who had received differential reinforcement of alternative behavior at full integrity kept a bias toward appropriate behavior when treatment errors were then introduced (Vollmer, Roane, Ringdahl, & Marcus, 1999). Prior FBAs and BIPs in the file are therefore reviewed for the hypothesized function, the components, whether integrity was measured, and what the outcome data showed, not merely whether the plan "worked."
Applied guidanceThe Intervention history sheet records each prior FBA and each documented intervention with the function it addressed, its dates, whether integrity data exist, and the outcome as the record shows it. "Ineffective" without integrity data is entered as "unknown" with the reason. Prior plans that relied on removal, time-out, or office referral are flagged, since for escape-maintained behavior those consequences can function as negative reinforcement.

4. Health, Medication, and Setting Events

EvidenceBiological conditions alter the value of reinforcers and the probability of problem behavior. For three students whose problem behavior was maintained by escape from instruction, Kennedy and Meyer (1996) found sleep deprivation and allergy symptoms associated with higher rates of it; O'Reilly (1995) documented escape-maintained aggression correlated with sleep loss; O'Reilly (1997) found self-injury only during periods of otitis media, which may have served as an establishing operation related to escape from ambient noise, and Carr et al. (2003) found the aggression, self-injury, and tantrums of three participants linked both to menstrual discomfort and to task demands. Medication changes alter behavior directly and through side effects (see Form MS-1). Attendance records identify absences and tardies that may coincide with these conditions.
Applied guidanceThe Health and setting events sheet records only what the school record contains: diagnoses as listed, medications as listed, nurse-visit counts, and dated events. It is not a medical history and nothing on it is a clinical judgment. Dates of medication starts and changes are entered so they can be laid against the incident dates on the Synthesis sheet; a temporal correspondence is noted as a setting-event hypothesis for the interview.

5. Recording Conventions

  • Quote the record, then interpret. Each entry states what the document says (with its date) before any inference; inferences are labeled as such on the Synthesis sheet.
  • Dates on everything. An undated finding cannot be aligned with incidents, medication changes, or plan changes.
  • Not available is a finding. A missing prior FBA, an unmeasured integrity, or an incomplete transfer record is listed, not left blank.
  • Minimum necessary. Family and health details are recorded only where they bear on a setting event, antecedent, or reinforcer. Details the FBA does not need stay in the source document.
  • Carry forward. Every hypothesis and every gap on the Synthesis sheet names the form that will test or fill it.

6. Counting Removal Days

Evidence34 CFR §300.530(b)(2): “After a child with a disability has been removed from his or her current placement for 10 school days in the same school year, during any subsequent days of removal the public agency must provide services to the extent required under paragraph (d) of this section.” Under §300.536(a), “a change of placement occurs if— (1) The removal is for more than 10 consecutive school days; or (2) The child has been subjected to a series of removals that constitute a pattern— (i) Because the series of removals total more than 10 school days in a school year; (ii) Because the child's behavior is substantially similar to the child's behavior in previous incidents that resulted in the series of removals; and (iii) Because of such additional factors as the length of each removal, the total amount of time the child has been removed, and the proximity of the removals to one another.” §300.536(b)(1): “The public agency determines on a case-by-case basis whether a pattern of removals constitutes a change of placement.” §300.11(c)(1): “School day means any day, including a partial day that children are in attendance at school for instructional purposes.” Whether an in-school suspension, a bus suspension, or an administratively shortened school day counts is addressed in the Department's July 19, 2022 discipline guidance (Questions C-7, C-8, and C-6); the Removal days sheet quotes each answer beside the removal it concerns.
Applied guidanceThe Removal days sheet is the form's only count of removal days. Enter one row per removal, with the dates the record gives; the form counts Monday to Friday less the no-school days listed there, and it knows no district calendar. For each row, record whether it counts toward the cumulative total (yes, no, or team to decide) and why; the form never decides it. Days not counted or not yet decided are totaled separately, so the team can see what its decision would change. Each flag quotes the provision it rests on. The pattern question, the change-of-placement decision, and the manifestation determination belong to the team; the sheet records them and makes none.
Regulatory statements paraphrase 34 CFR §§300.305, 300.306, and 300.613, FERPA 34 CFR §99.10, and Florida Rule 6A-6.0331(8). Section 6 quotes 34 CFR §§300.11(c)(1), 300.530(b)(2), and 300.536 from the eCFR. Method statements after O'Neill et al. (2015), Steege et al. (2019), Hanley (2012), Irvin et al. (2004), and McIntosh et al. (2010). Full citations on the References sheet.
