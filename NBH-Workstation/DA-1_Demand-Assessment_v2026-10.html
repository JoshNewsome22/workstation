Demand Assessment

For behavior maintained by escape: which demands evoke it, and why. The team lists the student’s demands from the schedule, each task is presented in brief sessions with the same prompting sequence while compliance, latency to the first problem behavior, its rate and the student’s affect are recorded, and the results rank the tasks, name the ones for the demand condition of Form EA-1, and say where demand fading and task modification should start on Form TD-1.

Student, Behavior, and the Working Conventions

Physical guidance: the stop rule. The physical step comes only after the vocal instruction and the model. It is a light touch that helps the student move, never force and never a hold: your hands never move the student against their pull.

  • Let go at once if the student pulls away, pushes against your hand, or goes stiff, or cries, screams, or shakes.
  • Keep the task in place. After a short calm pause (about 10 seconds with no pulling, pushing, crying, or shouting), give the instruction again from the vocal step. The instruction counts as not complied with. If the target behavior occurs, the consequence chosen above applies as usual.
  • After 2 stops on the same task in a session, do not guide it again that session: give its instructions with the vocal step and the model only, score each one as usual (by “Compliance counted as” above), and tell the BCBA the same day.
  • If the student says no in one of the ways the assent plan (Form SI-1) describes, or is still upset after the calm pause, stop the session, and enter the minutes actually run.
  • Staff never hold the student still, or hold the student’s body to make them do the task: that is restraint, and restraint is never used to make a student comply. Use the crisis plan (Form CR-1) only for imminent danger.
  • If a hold happens anyway, or the student is hurt: stop the session, make sure the student is safe, and tell the BCBA and the school administrator (at home, the parent or guardian) at once. A hold is a restraint: follow the crisis plan’s steps for reporting it, which start the same day (Form CR-1). Anyone who suspects abuse reports it right away to the Florida Abuse Hotline.

Based on the rule Form TD-1 sets for escape extinction with physical guidance.

Why an inventory first. A demand condition only tests escape when its tasks are ones the student actually escapes from. Roscoe, Rooker, Pence and Longworth (2009) found that functional analyses run with demands a brief assessment had identified as evoking the most problem behavior were differentiated where the same analyses with low-ranked demands were not, and Call, Pabico and Lomas (2009) used the latency to the first problem behavior across candidate tasks to pick the ones to include. This sheet lists the candidates and the features of each that the research says matter: difficulty relative to the student’s skills (Weeks & Gaylord-Ross, 1981; Carr & Durand, 1985), novelty, duration and pace (Smith, Iwata, Goh & Shore, 1995), response effort, the mode of prompting and how the task is presented.

The Demand Inventory

Walk the student’s schedule with the people who present the work and list the demands, from the ones they expect to be worst to the ones the student does without trouble. Include at least two tasks the team thinks the student does readily; the assessment needs high-probability demands to compare against and the fading plan needs somewhere to start (working convention). The rating in the last column is the informant’s guess at how aversive the task is, 1 (not at all) to 5 (very); the Results sheet shows where the guesses and the data disagree. Up to 12 tasks.

Before the First Session

The prompting ends in physical guidance, or problem behavior is followed by a brief escape, so these sessions work like the demand condition of an experimental analysis (Form EA-1). Check each line before the first session.

The Trial Record

Procedure, as the form assumes it.

  1. Present each task in brief sessions of the length on the Inventory sheet (2 to 5 minutes as a working convention; Roscoe et al. (2009) and Call et al. (2009) used sessions of this order), with the same materials and the same person presenting as in the classroom when that is possible.
  2. Deliver instructions on the pacing chosen and use the prompting sequence chosen, vocal then model then physical guidance about 5 seconds apart (the sequence of the demand condition in Iwata, Dorsey, Slifer, Bauman & Richman, 1982/1994). Praise compliance briefly. Physical guidance is a light touch, never force: let go at once if the student resists or shows distress, keep the task in place, and give the instruction again from the vocal step after a short calm pause; after 2 stops on one task, do not guide it again that session (the stop rule on the Inventory sheet).
  3. Record, per session: the instructions delivered and the ones complied with, the latency in seconds from the first instruction to the first problem behavior (leave blank when none occurred), the count of problem behaviors, and the student’s affect on the 1 to 5 scale (1 distressed or avoiding, 3 neutral, 5 calm and engaged).
  4. Run the tasks in alternating order so that one task never follows the same task, and give each task the number of sessions set on the Inventory sheet (3 as a working convention). The button below writes the order; sessions can also be added one at a time.
  5. Optional: between sessions, offer two tasks side by side and record which the student picks. The choice probes are a preference measure for the same tasks and feed the Results sheet.

What follows problem behavior inside a session is a decision made on the Inventory sheet and held constant across tasks. If the demand-condition contingency is used, the count and the latency are read as they would be in Form EA-1; if nothing is programmed, the latency is the cleaner measure and the count may rise across the session.

Second Observer and Agreement (optional)

For some sessions, spread across the tasks (a third of them as a working convention), a second observer records the same session independently. Agreement per session: the problem-behavior count and the instructions complied with as total-count agreement (the smaller count divided by the larger; both zero is 100%), the latency as agreeing when the two are within 10 seconds or both record none, and the affect rating as exact agreement (Cooper, Heron & Heward, 2020). Ranked Tasks

Ranked from the task most likely to evoke problem behavior to the least: by mean rate of problem behavior, then by mean latency (shorter first); when the sessions are latency-based (no programmed consequence, or the session ends at the first problem behavior, on the Inventory sheet), by mean latency first, then rate (Call et al., 2009). Two separate questions: a task evokes problem behavior when it occurred in at least half of its sessions, and those are the tasks for the demand condition; by compliance, a task is a high-probability demand when mean compliance was 80% or above and problem behavior occurred in no more than a quarter of its sessions, a low-probability demand when mean compliance was 40% or below (the lines Mace et al. (1988) used to sort requests), and otherwise mixed, which places it in the fading plan. Low compliance alone says the student does not do the task, not that the task evokes the behavior. All the cutoffs are working conventions.

What This Form Builds

When the indirect and descriptive assessments point to escape, the next two decisions both depend on knowing which demands matter: the demand condition of the functional analysis (Form EA-1) has to present a task the student escapes from, or it tests nothing, and the treatment (Form TD-1) has to start demand fading and task modification from tasks the student can already manage. A demand assessment answers both. Each candidate task is presented in brief sessions under the same prompting sequence, the problem behavior each one evokes is measured, and the tasks are ranked. The ones at the top go into the demand condition; the ones at the bottom are the high-probability demands the fading plan and the interspersal procedures start from; the features that separate the two (difficulty, length, novelty, effort, how the task is presented) are the first antecedent modifications to try.

The form keeps the measures the research used. Roscoe, Rooker, Pence and Longworth (2009) ranked tasks by the problem behavior they evoked in brief sessions and showed that the functional analysis was differentiated with the high-ranked tasks and not with the low-ranked ones. Call, Pabico and Lomas (2009) ranked them by the latency to the first problem behavior, a measure that is quick, needs no counting, and is less affected by what happens after the first response. Thomason-Sassi, Iwata, Neidert and Roscoe (2011) showed across many functional analyses that latency to the first response tracks response rate well enough to stand in for it. Compliance is recorded because it is what the demand is for, and because the high- and low-probability sets of the interspersal literature are defined by it (Mace et al., 1988). Affect is a secondary measure that catches the task a student endures without responding. The choice probes are a preference measure for the same tasks: the task a student picks when offered a pair is the one to use as a choice option (Dyer, Dunlap & Winterling, 1990).

What the Research Supports

The demand condition needs the right demandsRoscoe, Rooker, Pence and Longworth (2009) ran a demand assessment before the functional analysis for participants whose problem behavior was thought to be escape-maintained, exposing each to a set of tasks and ranking them by the problem behavior they evoked. Functional analyses that used the high-ranked tasks in the demand condition were differentiated; analyses that used low-ranked tasks were not. The tasks named by this form’s first recommendation are chosen by the same logic.
Latency is enough to rank tasksCall, Pabico and Lomas (2009) measured the latency to the first problem behavior across candidate demands, selected the demands with the shortest latencies for the functional analysis, and obtained clear escape functions. Thomason-Sassi, Iwata, Neidert and Roscoe (2011) compared latency-based and rate-based functional analyses and found that latency to the first response gave the same conclusion as rate in most cases. Both measures are kept here; the ranking uses rate first and latency to break ties (latency first when the sessions are latency-based), and the chart can show either.
Difficulty is an establishing operationWeeks and Gaylord-Ross (1981) showed that difficult tasks produced more aberrant behavior than easy ones in students with severe disabilities, and that an errorless teaching procedure on the difficult task reduced it. Carr and Durand (1985) built their assessment on easy versus difficult tasks and found behavior problems rose under the difficult ones; the treatment, functional communication training, taught a request for help. The Inventory sheet’s difficulty and “can the student do it” columns drive the easier-materials recommendation.
Novelty, length and pace matter tooSmith, Iwata, Goh and Shore (1995) analyzed establishing operations for escape-maintained self-injury and found that, for different participants, task novelty, session duration and the rate of task presentation changed the amount of self-injury. The inventory records novelty and usual length so that these can be compared across tasks; the instruction pacing is chosen once on the Inventory sheet and held constant across tasks, and the shorten-the-task recommendation applies when problem behavior arrives late in a long session.
Where fading startsPace, Iwata, Cowdery, Andree and McIntyre (1993) treated escape-maintained self-injury by removing demands altogether and then reintroducing them gradually with escape extinction; Zarcone, Iwata, Smith, Mazaleski and Lerman (1994) replicated the fading and showed that self-injury re-emerged when the demand rate rose too fast and was extinguished when the step was held. A fading plan needs to start with demands that evoke little or nothing; the high-probability demands this form identifies are those.
Interspersal and high-probability sequencesHorner, Day, Sprague, O’Brien and Heathfield (1991) reduced aggression and self-injury during instruction by interspersing easy, already-mastered requests among the difficult ones. Mace et al. (1988) preceded a low-probability request with a run of brief, single-step high-probability requests (compliance of 80% or more; the low-probability requests, 40% or less), and compliance with the low-probability request rose. This form’s tasks are whole activities: the high-probability ones are where fading starts and a source of easy items to intersperse, not a high-probability request sequence (its single-step requests are chosen on Form TD-1’s planner).
Curricular revision and choiceDunlap, Kern-Dunlap, Clarke and Robbins (1991) and Kern, Childs, Dunlap, Clarke and Falk (1994) used assessment of the features of academic tasks that set the occasion for problem behavior, then revised the curriculum (shorter tasks, a choice of tasks, functional materials, fine-motor demands removed) and reduced the behavior. Dyer, Dunlap and Winterling (1990) showed that a choice of tasks and reinforcers reduced serious problem behavior; Kern, Vorndran, Hilt, Ringdahl, Adelman and Dunlap (1998) reviewed the choice literature. The antecedent modifications this form proposes are those.
The form of the instruction can be the problemFisher, Adelinis, Thompson, Worsdell and Zarcone (1998) found destructive behavior maintained by the termination of “don’t” requests, and McComas, Hoch, Paone and El-Roy (2000) found idiosyncratic establishing operations for escape during academic tasks (the type of prompt, the presence of a particular material). The prompting and presentation columns are on the inventory for this reason, and the recommendations flag a task that evokes behavior despite low effort and adequate skill as one whose presentation should be changed.
Escape without extinctionVollmer, Marcus and Ringdahl (1995) reduced escape-maintained self-injury with noncontingent escape, breaks delivered on a time schedule, and thinned the schedule. A break schedule set below the mean latency to problem behavior (as observed, not floored) is one of the modifications proposed here. Geiger, Carr and LeBlanc (2010) give the treatment-selection model for escape-maintained behavior that Form TD-1 follows; this form supplies its antecedent branch.
The functional analysis itselfIwata, Dorsey, Slifer, Bauman and Richman (1982/1994) set out the demand condition that Form EA-1 runs: instructions presented about every 30 seconds with a vocal, model, physical prompting sequence, brief escape following problem behavior. Hanley, Iwata and McCord (2003) review the method and the conditions. Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020) give the textbook account of establishing operations, negative reinforcement and antecedent interventions used in the Guide.

How the Figures Are Calculated

ComplianceInstructions complied with divided by instructions given, per session, as a percent; the per-task figure is the mean of the session percents. What counts as complied is the definition on the Inventory sheet.
Rate of problem behaviorProblem behaviors counted divided by the session’s minutes; the per-task figure is the mean of the session rates.
LatencySeconds from the first instruction to the first problem behavior. A session with no problem behavior is entered as the session’s full length in seconds (a floor, not an observation); the per-task figure is the mean, used for the ranking. “Sessions with PB” shows how often the floor was used. The modification rules use the observed latency, the mean over only the sessions with problem behavior.
AffectMean of the 1 to 5 session ratings.
Chosen when offeredAcross the choice probes, the number of times the task was picked divided by the number of probes in which it was offered.
RankingTasks are sorted by mean rate, highest first; ties are broken by mean latency, shortest first, then by mean compliance, lowest first. With latency-based sessions (Inventory sheet), by mean latency first, then rate. A task with no sessions is unranked.
Evokes problem behavior; compliance classA task evokes problem behavior when it occurred in at least 50% of its sessions. Separately, by compliance: high-probability demand, mean compliance at or above 80% with problem behavior in at most 25% of sessions; low-probability demand, mean compliance at or below 40%; otherwise mixed. Working conventions; the 80 and 40 are from Mace et al. (1988).
Differentiation verdictDifferentiated when a task evokes problem behavior and the top-ranked task’s mean rate is at least twice the bottom-ranked task’s (or the bottom is zero and the top is not; with latency-based sessions, the bottom’s mean latency at least twice the top’s); weak when the spread is smaller; undifferentiated when no task evokes it. Working convention.
Tasks for the demand conditionUp to three tasks that evoke problem behavior, in rank order; compliance alone never names one. When there are none, the form says so and points to longer sessions, a different pacing or presentation, or the possibility that the function is not escape from these demands.
Starting tasks for demand fadingThe high-probability demands in reverse rank order (least evocative first), followed by the mixed tasks that do not evoke problem behavior; the tasks that evoke it, and the low-probability demands, are the terminal steps. When no task qualifies as high-probability, the lowest-ranked task is offered with a note that fading should start from no demands at all (Pace et al., 1993).
Antecedent modificationsProposed per low-probability or mixed task from its inventory features and its session data: easier materials or prerequisite teaching when difficulty is above skill or the student cannot do the task; shorten the task or schedule breaks when the observed latency is at least half the session (behavior arrives late); choice and interspersal when the observed latency is short (the demand’s onset is the problem); pre-teaching when the task is novel; reduce the response effort when the task is high effort; shorten the usual block when the task normally runs 15 minutes or more; change the presentation or prompt when the task is low effort, within the student’s skills and still evokes problem behavior; use the task as a choice option when it was chosen in at least half the probes in which it was offered. The cutoffs are working conventions.
Informant disagreementFlagged when an informant rated a task 4 or 5 and it came out high-probability, or rated it 1 or 2 and it evoked problem behavior.
Agreement (optional)Per session: count and complied as total-count agreement, latency within 10 s, affect exact; then per task and overall (Cooper et al., 2020).

Cautions

It is not a functional analysisThe assessment shows which demands evoke the behavior; it does not show that escape maintains it. That is Form EA-1’s job, with these tasks in the demand condition and the other conditions for comparison.
Few sessions, floored latenciesThree sessions a task is a working convention chosen to keep the assessment to a morning. Means from three sessions move a lot with one session; read the sessions-with-PB column alongside them, and add sessions for tasks that are close in rank when the decision depends on the order.
The consequence inside a session shapes the countWith brief escape after each problem behavior, the count is a measure under the escape contingency; with nothing programmed, the count can climb within a session. Either is fine when it is the same for every task. The latency is unaffected by what follows the first response.
SafetyStop a session when the behavior reaches the stopping rule in the crisis plan (Form CR-1); enter the minutes actually run so the rate is right, and note the stop.
Physical guidanceThe physical step of the prompting sequence is a light touch that helps the student move, never force and never a hold. Staff let go at once when the student pulls away, pushes against the adult’s hand, or goes stiff, or at any sign of distress; the task stays in place, and after a short calm pause the instruction is given again from the vocal step. After 2 stops on one task in a session, the task is not guided again that session, and the BCBA is told the same day. A withdrawal of assent, as the student’s assent plan (Form SI-1) describes it, or distress that outlasts the calm pause, ends the session. Holding a student to make them do the task is restraint, and in a Florida public school restraint may not be used to correct noncompliance (§ 1003.573(3), F.S.; Form CR-1). If a hold happens anyway, or the student is hurt, the session stops, the BCBA and the school administrator are told at once, the hold is reported as the crisis plan sets out, and anyone who suspects abuse reports it right away to the Florida Abuse Hotline. The rule is based on the one Form TD-1 sets for escape extinction with physical guidance; the Inventory sheet shows it whenever the prompting ends in physical guidance.

Writing help

Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. Rewrite with Claude works only in the forms opened from the website: a single-use passcode from your BCBA unlocks the tab (in the workstation, every form in that tab) until the session’s time is up or the tab is closed; press Lock when you finish on a shared iPad. Use it for real cases only with your district’s or agency’s approval. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated assessment is six tasks from a third-grader’s schedule, three 5-minute sessions each in alternating order over two mornings, ten choice probes, and the draft plan written from the results.
