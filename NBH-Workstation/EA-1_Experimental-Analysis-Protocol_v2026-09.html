Guide: Experimental Analysis and the Designs That Carry It

An experimental analysis demonstrates a functional relation by manipulating antecedents or consequences and observing the behavior under repeated test and control conditions; it is the only assessment method that identifies function rather than correlation. Evidence boxes report what the cited studies found; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses. Full references are on the last sheet.

1. The Standard Analysis and What Has Been Learned About It

EvidenceIwata, Dorsey, Slifer, Bauman, and Richman (1982/1994) exposed nine participants with developmental disabilities to alone, social-disapproval (attention), academic-demand, and unstructured-play conditions in a multielement design with 15-min sessions (the pilot participant received three of the four conditions) and found self-injury consistently higher in a specific condition for six of the nine. Hanley, Iwata, and McCord (2003) and Beavers, Iwata, and Lerman (2013) reviewed the subsequent literature (435 studies by 2012) and reported differentiated outcomes in the large majority of published analyses. Wallace and Iwata (1999) re-scored 46 analyses at 10 and 5 min: every 10-min data set produced the same interpretation as the 15-min original, and the 5-min sets differed in 3 of 46 cases (93.5% correspondence). Thomason-Sassi, Iwata, and Fritz (2013) found that therapist and setting rarely changed the outcome. Rooker, Iwata, Harper, Fahmie, and Camp (2011) examined how prone the tangible condition is to false-positive outcomes (whether a new response was acquired more readily under tangible reinforcement, and whether behavior known to have no social function emerged under it) and concluded that tangible items should be included with care and selected from items typically found in the individual's environment. Kahng et al. (2015) reviewed the records of 99 people admitted to an inpatient unit for self-injury, found injury rates relatively low both during and outside functional analyses and injuries usually not severe, and concluded that the analysis is relatively safe when appropriate precautions are taken.
Applied guidanceThe default analysis is the four-condition multielement analysis with 10-min sessions, a play control, and an alone or no-interaction condition (ignore when the target is aggression). Add a tangible condition only when the indirect and descriptive assessments (Forms IA-1, ABC-1) indicate tangible delivery follows the behavior. Continue until the structured criteria on the interpretation sheet are met or until a variant is indicated by the selector sheet. Every analysis carries written session-termination criteria, a protective plan, and medical clearance for behavior that produces injury.

2. Variants for Safety, Time, Setting, and Low-Rate Behavior

EvidenceBrief. Northup et al. (1991) completed analyses in a single 90-min outpatient visit using one 5- to 10-min exposure to each condition followed by a contingency reversal; Derby et al. (1992) summarized 79 such cases, and Kahng and Iwata (1999) found that a brief analysis made of the first session of each condition matched the full analysis in 66% of 50 cases. Latency. Thomason-Sassi, Iwata, Neidert, and Roscoe (2011) terminated sessions at the first response and found an inverse relation between rate and latency; latency-based and 10-min analyses corresponded in 9 of 10 paired cases with far fewer occurrences of the behavior. Trial-based. Bloom, Iwata, Fritz, Roscoe, and Carreau (2011) embedded 2-min control and test segments as trials in classroom activities, terminating a segment at the first response; outcomes corresponded with standard analyses in 6 of 10 cases (partially in a 7th), and Bloom, Lambert, Dayton, and Samaha (2013) had teachers run them. Precursor. Smith and Churchill (2002) delivered consequences for precursors instead of the severe behavior and inferred the severe behavior's maintaining variables from the precursor analysis for all 4 participants; Fritz, Iwata, Hammond, and Bloom (2013) found that separate analyses of precursors and of severe behavior corresponded in 7 of 8 cases, and noted that analyzing precursors can reduce the severe behavior observed during assessment. Extended alone. Querim et al. (2013) ran five consecutive 5-min alone or no-interaction sessions as a screen and predicted the automatic-versus-social outcome of the full analysis in 28 of 30 cases. Pairwise. Iwata, Duncan, Zarcone, Lerman, and Shore (1994) alternated one test with its control; Iwata and Dozier (2008) proposed the single-function test when a hypothesis is already strong. Antecedent-only. Carr and Durand (1985) varied task difficulty and attention level without programmed consequences; Anderson and Long (2002) manipulated antecedents while allowing natural consequences (structured descriptive assessment). Synthesized (IISCA). Hanley, Jin, Vanselow, and Hanratty (2014) built one test condition from all interview-reported establishing operations and reinforcers against a matched control; Jessel, Hanley, and Ghaemmaghami (2016) reported differentiation in 30 of 30 replications; Slaton, Hanley, and Raftery (2017) found the IISCA differentiated for all 9 children while the standard analysis was differentiated for 4 (6 when precursors were counted); Fisher, Greer, Romani, Zangrillo, and Owen (2016) found behavior sensitive to isolated contingencies for 4 of 5 participants and none showing the response pattern the synthesis assumes, and Holehan et al. (2020) found that isolated contingencies produced differentiated responding for all 5 participants and little to no difference between treatments informed by isolated and synthesized analyses (Slaton et al., 2017, found IISCA-based treatment effective for all 4 children treated and standard-analysis-based treatment for 2); as Slaton et al. note, the synthesis precludes a precise understanding of the individual contingencies.
Applied guidanceChoose the variant on the selector sheet from four questions: how dangerous the behavior is, how much time and which setting are available, whether a reliable precursor exists, and how strong the prior hypothesis is. Dangerous behavior is analyzed by latency, precursor, or trial-based methods before any repeated-occurrence method. A synthesized analysis is an acceptable route to a treatment baseline; when the plan needs to know which contingency is operative (for example, to choose which reinforcer to withhold), follow it with isolated tests. A brief analysis that evokes no behavior is a null result, not evidence of no social function.

2b. Yoked Controls: Separating the Contingency from the Delivery

EvidenceA yoked control asks whether the contingency matters or only the reinforcer arriving. In the master session the consequence follows the target behaviour and every delivery time is recorded; in the yoked session the same reinforcer is delivered at those recorded times, response-independently. If responding is maintained in the master session and falls in the yoked session, the response–reinforcer dependency is doing the work. Rescorla and Skucy (1969) examined response-independent reinforcer delivery during extinction; Halliday and Boakes (1971) found behavioural contrast in pigeons exposed to extinction but in none for whom a variable-time schedule replaced the variable-interval schedule at the same rate and distribution, so removing the dependency without changing reinforcement rate was not sufficient to produce contrast; Lieving and Lattal (2003, Experiment 3) found resurgence failed to develop when a variable-interval alternative schedule was changed to an otherwise identical variable-time schedule, but occurred in every pigeon once the reinforcer was removed altogether. Killeen (1972) used a yoked-chamber arrangement to compare concurrent and multiple schedules directly.
Known limitation. Yoked designs are not a clean control. Church (1964) showed that a yoked group can differ from its master group for reasons unrelated to the contingency: random error, that is, individual differences in how subjects respond to the same events, produces systematic differences between the master and yoked conditions. In addition, the master animal’s behaviour determines when reinforcers arrive, so in the yoked session those deliveries land on whatever the yoked subject happens to be doing, and can adventitiously reinforce something. Treat a yoked difference as consistent with a contingency effect rather than as proof of one, and record which behaviours the response-independent deliveries actually followed.
Applied use. The practical question this answers is whether a procedure works because of what it makes contingent or because of what it delivers. It bears directly on noncontingent reinforcement, on whether a reward chart is doing anything beyond providing the item, and on the response-independent option in the relapse literature (Form RM-1).

3. Undifferentiated Results

EvidenceVollmer, Marcus, Ringdahl, and Roane (1995) described a progression when a brief multielement analysis is unclear: an extended alone series, then extended test-control comparisons, which resolved most of their cases. Vollmer, Iwata, Duncan, and Lerman (1993) resolved undifferentiated multielement outcomes by re-running test and control conditions in reversal-type designs. Iwata and Dozier (2008) listed further modifications: single-function and pairwise tests, longer sessions, individualized establishing operations, and within-session analysis. Hanley (2012) recommended starting with an open-ended interview and brief observation and then alternating a single test condition designed from that information with an intimately matched control condition.
Applied guidanceAn undifferentiated multielement analysis is followed, in order, by the extended-alone screen, a pairwise test of the strongest candidate, modification of the establishing operation from interview data, and only then a synthesized analysis. Each step is logged on the sessions sheet as a new phase so the whole sequence prints as one record.

4. Interpretation

EvidenceHagopian et al. (1997) found low agreement among three predoctoral interns interpreting multielement analyses (M = .46), developed structured criteria that reproduced an expert panel's interpretations (exact agreement .94), and after training in the criteria the interns' mean agreement rose to .81. The criteria draw an upper and a lower criterion line one standard deviation above and below the mean of the control condition and count test-condition points outside them. Roane, Fisher, Kelley, Mevers, and Bouxsein (2013) generalized the rule to analyses of any length: a test condition is differentiated when the number of points above the upper line minus the number below the lower line is at least half the points in that condition, with additional rules for trends, low magnitude, and multiple control; the lower line is set at zero when the standard deviation exceeds the control mean. Saini, Fisher, and Retzlaff (2018) applied the criteria on an ongoing basis, series by series, to existing analyses: the interim interpretations predicted the final post hoc interpretations with a very high degree of accuracy and identified function after 19 sessions on average instead of 32.
Applied guidanceThe interpretation sheet computes the criterion lines and the Roane rule from the sessions log and flags trend and low-magnitude patterns for visual review. For latency measures the rule is inverted (points below the lower line count as evidence), an adaptation of this form; Sunde, Briggs, and Mitteer (2022) published structured criteria adapted for latency-based analyses, and those should be applied on visual review. A function is reported only when the rule is met and the pattern survives visual inspection for trend and consistency; otherwise the analysis continues or a variant is chosen.

5. Single-Subject Designs for the Treatment That Follows

EvidenceBaer, Wolf, and Risley (1968) described the reversal and multiple-baseline designs as two designs commonly used to demonstrate reliable control of a behavior change; Sidman (1960) set the steady-state logic: change conditions only after responding is stable. Barlow and Hayes (1979) described the alternating-treatments design; Hartmann and Hall (1976) the changing-criterion design; Horner and Baer (1978) the multiple-probe technique. Thompson and Iwata (2005) reviewed the control conditions used to demonstrate reinforcement effects (extinction, noncontingent reinforcement, DRO, DRA), noting that NCR and DRO controls have often been used because conventional extinction is difficult to implement. Kratochwill et al. (2010, 2013) set the What Works Clearinghouse standards: at least three demonstrations of the effect at three points in time, at least five data points per phase (three with reservations), interobserver agreement on at least 20% of sessions in each condition at 80% or higher, and a visual analysis of level, trend, variability, immediacy, overlap, and consistency; Lane and Gast (2014) give a step-by-step guide to that analysis. Horner et al. (2005) had earlier proposed quality indicators for participants, setting, dependent and independent variables, fidelity, and social validity (Wolf, 1978).
Applied guidanceThe treatment-design sheet builds the phase plan for the chosen design, sets the phase-change rule, counts demonstrations of effect, and computes per-phase level, trend, and percentage of non-overlapping data (Scruggs, Mastropieri, & Casto, 1987) from the logged data. A withdrawal is not used when the behavior is dangerous or the treatment cannot ethically be removed; use a multiple baseline across settings or behaviors, or a DRO or NCR reversal, instead.

6. Running Sessions at the Table

Applied guidanceThe Sessions sheet has a session runner above the log. Pick the condition and the runner reads its card: the session length, the consequence period after each target (for example, the 30-s break in Demand), the attention schedule in the control condition and how long attention is held back after a target, and the prompt-step interval. All of them can be edited before you start. During the session, tap Target for every response (keys: Space or T) and Precursor when a precursor is being analyzed (P). Consequence follows says which response the consequence period follows: the target, the precursor (a precursor analysis), or either (a synthesized analysis whose card says target or precursor). When it follows the precursor, the precursor is the response the log records, and the target count goes in the notes; Undo (Z) removes the last target. The runner times each consequence period and cues its end, cues each noncontingent attention delivery, and can end the session at a termination count. Latency sessions end at the first target and record its latency. Trial-based sessions run the control segment and then the test segment, each ending at the first target, and write the 1 or 0 into the trial-based log. When the session is over, check the summary and press Save to the log. The date, condition, phase, minutes, the value the primary measure needs (count, latency, or percentage of intervals), the terminated box and a short note go into the next empty row, and the log and the graph update. Sound, flash and vibration can each be turned off. The runner is for the data collector; the therapist runs the session.
Variant summaries follow the primary reports cited above and the reviews by Hanley et al. (2003) and Beavers et al. (2013). Numerical correspondence figures are from the studies named; the sequencing rules and the latency adaptation of the structured criteria are practice rules of this form.

Interpret each phase on its own when the design changed (e.g., a pairwise test after an undifferentiated multielement).

S. Before the First Session

Setup & safety · Select variant · Conditions · the session runner on Sessions & graph

Everything the sessions depend on is decided and written down before the first one: the safety plan, the room and materials for each condition, each condition’s signal, who does what, the session length, and the order the conditions rotate in. The example student’s plan puts a helmet on the student and mats on the floor in every session.

    TherapistStudentData collectorSecond observerThe session runner’s screen, drawn largePlacemat: the condition’s signal

    What You Say and Do

    • Agree the roles before the first session: the therapist runs the session; the data collector runs the session runner and taps every target; the second observer records independently and says nothing.
    • Set the room for each condition before its session: the placemat, the materials on its card, and nothing else within reach.
    • Keep each condition’s signal and materials the same in every session unless the plan changes them.

    What Goes on the Sheet

    • Setup & safety, Safety Plan: Medical clearance, Session termination criteria, Protective equipment and blocking, Staffing and ratio, Consent and notification, Analysis termination criteria.
    • Measurement and Agreement: Primary measure, Session length (min), Interval size (s), IOA plan (% of sessions), IOA index, Procedural fidelity checks.
    • Select variant: the answers and the Variant chosen, then Load condition templates for the chosen variant; the cards on Conditions, edited to the student.
    Decision. Every analysis carries written session-termination criteria, a protective plan, and medical clearance for behavior that produces injury. The analysis termination criteria are written down too: for example, the structured criteria met, or five series without differentiation.

    A. The Attention Condition

    Conditions: the Attention card · the red placemat · the session runner from Start session to Save to the log

    Attention tests whether attention maintains the behavior. Attention is diverted, which is the establishing operation, and brief attention follows each target. This story follows the data collector through the session runner from start to finish.

      What You Say and Do

      • Therapist: sit nearby and attend to the magazine or the work; no eye contact and no talk except the consequence.
      • After each target: 5 to 10 s of concern or reprimand with a light touch, then straight back to work.
      • Data collector: pick Attention, check the timings, Start session; tap Target (Space or T) for every response. Undo (Z) removes a mistaken tap.

      What Goes on the Sheet

      • Save to the log fills the next empty row: Date, Condition, Phase, Min and Count; the Rate is computed.
      • Term. is checked and the Notes filled when a session ends early.
      • IOA % when a second observer recorded the session (section I).
      Why. Attention is withheld until a target so that it is valuable, and then follows the target at once. Play (control) delivers attention on a schedule whatever the student does, so a difference between the two is the evidence that attention maintains the behavior.

      B. The Demand Condition

      Conditions: the Demand card · the blue placemat · the runner times the break and cues the prompt steps

      Demand tests whether escape from tasks maintains the behavior. Tasks are presented continuously with three-step prompting, and each target produces a break from them.

        What You Say and Do

        • Present tasks continuously: vocal, then model, then physical, each after the step interval (the card says every 5–10 s). Praise each compliance and present the next task.
        • After a target: remove the task, turn away, and say nothing for the 30 s; re-present the task when the runner cues the end of the break.
        • No consequence for other behavior; block only as the safety plan allows.

        What Goes on the Sheet

        • In the runner, Consequence period (s) 30 and Prompt step every (s) 5 are read from the card; change them before Start session if the card says otherwise.
        • The row, as in Attention: Date, Condition, Phase, Min, Count; Term. and Notes if the session ended early.
        Why. The tasks are the establishing operation and the break is the consequence under test, so the break follows only the target and always lasts the card’s 30 s. The runner holds the prompt cues while the break runs and cues its end.

        C. The Play (Control) Condition

        Conditions: the Play (control) card · the green placemat · attention on the runner’s schedule

        Play is the control: preferred items, attention on a schedule whatever the student does, and no demands, so none of the test conditions’ establishing operations is present. The Interpretation view draws its criterion lines from this condition.

          What You Say and Do

          • Attention on the 30-s schedule, whatever the student is doing, and attention for appropriate play. No demands.
          • After a target: no reaction. The next scheduled attention waits until 5 s after the target.
          • Data collector: the runner counts down to each delivery (Attention in) and moves it when a target comes too close to it.

          What Goes on the Sheet

          • On Conditions, exactly one card is marked Control; Interpretation draws the criterion lines from it.
          • The row, as in Attention.
          Why. The control condition has none of the establishing operations and delivers attention on a schedule, so responding here is the reference level every test condition is compared with. The hold keeps a scheduled delivery from landing just after a target.

          D. Alone, No Interaction, and Ignore

          Conditions: the Alone / No interaction card · the extended-alone template

          Alone tests whether the behavior persists without any social consequence: no materials, no interaction, and no programmed consequence. When the target is aggression the ignore arrangement is used instead, with the therapist present but not interacting.

            What You Say and Do

            • Alone: no materials and no interaction; watch without going in, as the safety plan arranges.
            • Ignore, for aggression: the therapist present, facing away, not interacting; blocking only by the plan’s blocking rules.
            • Apply the termination criteria exactly as written, in this condition as in every other; End early marks the session terminated.

            What Goes on the Sheet

            • A terminated session: Term. checked and the runner’s note in Notes. Interpretation counts terminated sessions.
            • The extended-alone screen: five 5-min sessions, each with the phase label Ext. alone.
            Decision. Persistence across the five alone sessions indicates automatic reinforcement; extinction across them indicates a social function to be tested next (Querim et al., 2013). When sessions keep ending on the termination criteria, the Interpretation view notes that latency or precursor measures may be more appropriate.

            E. A Tangible Condition

            The trial-based template’s Tangible card · added only with an indication from the assessment history

            EA-1’s standard analysis has no tangible condition; Tangible appears among the trial-based conditions, where each trial pairs a control segment with a test segment. It is added only when the indirect and descriptive assessments point to it.

              What You Say and Do

              • Before adding it: check Forms IA-1 and ABC-1 for tangible delivery following the behavior.
              • Control segment: the item available. Test segment: the item removed; a target returns it at once and ends the segment.

              What Goes on the Sheet

              • The Trial-Based Log: 1 if the target occurred in the segment, 0 if not, blank if the trial was not run.
              • The % trials column for each segment is computed.
              Caution. Rooker, Iwata, Harper, Fahmie, and Camp (2011) examined how prone the tangible condition is to false-positive outcomes and concluded that tangible items should be included with care and chosen from items typically found in the student’s environment. An elevated tangible condition with no history of access-related problem behavior is read with that in mind.

              F. Reading the Graph

              Sessions & graph · Interpretation · every number is from a worked example you can enter yourself

              The Interpretation view applies structured criteria to the session log: criterion lines from the control condition, a count of the points outside them, and a magnitude floor. The numbers are what the form computes for a worked example of sixteen 10-min sessions, four of each condition.

                What You Say and Do

                • Check that exactly one card is marked Control before reading anything.
                • When the design changed, choose the Phase to interpret, for example the pairwise test after an undifferentiated multielement.
                • Read the flags for visual review before the outcome, and interpret provisionally until each condition has three sessions.

                What Goes on the Sheet

                • The Decision on Interpretation, then Function(s) identified, Treatment implication and Interpretation notes.
                • The signatures, with the second analyst’s independent interpretation.

                G. Latency and Trial-Based Sessions

                Select variant: Latency-based FA, Trial-based FA · the runner’s Session type

                Two variants shorten exposure to the behavior. A latency-based session ends at the first response. A trial-based analysis runs short control and test segments inside the ongoing class activity, and each segment ends at the first response.

                  What You Say and Do

                  • Latency: set the runner’s Session type before Start session; the session ends at the first target.
                  • Trial-based: the control segment, then the test segment; each ends at the first response, and the consequence is delivered in the test segment only.

                  What Goes on the Sheet

                  • Latency: seconds in the Latency s column; a session with no response is scored at the ceiling.
                  • Trial-based: the Trial-Based Log, 1 or 0 per segment. Interpretation reads trial-based data from that summary; its criterion-line rule applies to session-based rates.
                  Decision. Dangerous behavior is analyzed by latency, precursor, or trial-based methods before any repeated-occurrence method. In Thomason-Sassi et al. (2011), latency-based and 10-min analyses corresponded in 9 of 10 paired cases with far fewer occurrences of the behavior.

                  H. IISCA and Precursor Analyses

                  Select variant: Interview-informed synthesized contingency analysis, Precursor FA

                  Two variants built on what the earlier assessments found. The IISCA combines every interview-reported establishing operation into one test and compares it with a matched control. The precursor analysis delivers the programmed consequences for a precursor instead of the severe behavior.

                    What You Say and Do

                    • IISCA: the test holds every establishing operation the interview reported; the matched control holds the same reinforcers, freely available, and no establishing operations.
                    • In the test, after a target or a precursor: all the reinforcers together for 30 s, then the establishing operations again. The runner’s Consequence follows reads Target or precursor for this card.
                    • Precursor: the programmed consequence follows the precursor; the severe behavior is recorded. The data collector taps Precursor (key P) for the precursor and Target for the severe behavior; Consequence follows reads Precursor.

                    What Goes on the Sheet

                    • IISCA: two cards, Synthesized test and Matched control, 5-min sessions, alternated.
                    • Precursor: Precursor (if analyzed) on Setup & safety; the precursor and the severe behavior counted separately, with Precursor and Target; Term. if a criterion ended the session.
                    Decision. A synthesized analysis is an acceptable route to a treatment baseline. When the plan needs to know which contingency is operative, for example to choose which reinforcer to withhold, it is followed with isolated tests.

                    I. Agreement and Fidelity

                    Setup & safety: IOA plan, IOA index, Procedural fidelity checks · the IOA % column · Interpretation

                    Two checks on the data before anyone reads the graph: agreement between two independent observers, and fidelity, whether each condition was run as its card says.

                      What You Say and Do

                      • Second observer: the same definition, a separate record, no talk, and no look at the other record.
                      • Compare the two records with the IOA index set on Setup & safety, in intervals of the Interval size (s).
                      • Fidelity: score the session against the card’s Fidelity checklist items.

                      What Goes on the Sheet

                      • The agreement in the session’s IOA % column.
                      • Interpretation, Agreement and Fidelity Obtained: Sessions with IOA, Mean IOA, and By condition.
                      Standard. Interobserver agreement on at least 20% of sessions in each condition, at 80% or higher (Kratochwill et al., 2010), and procedural fidelity measured and reported.

                      Terms, as the Form Uses Them

                      For the therapist, the data collector and the analyst
                      Establishing operation / antecedent
                      What is in place during a condition that makes the consequence under test valuable: attention diverted in Attention, tasks presented continuously in Demand. Each card names its own.
                      Session signal (SD)
                      What goes with one condition in every session, such as a colored placemat or shirt, a room, or the therapist facing away, so the conditions are discriminable.
                      Test and control conditions
                      A test condition arranges an establishing operation and delivers the consequence after the target. The control condition has none of the establishing operations; exactly one card is marked Control, and the criterion lines come from it.
                      Multielement design and series
                      The conditions alternate session by session. One session of each condition is a series; the analysis termination criteria can count series without differentiation.
                      Consequence period
                      The session runner’s timer for the programmed consequence in a test condition, read from the card: 30 s for the Demand break, 10 s for Attention’s “5–10 s”. The runner’s Consequence follows sets what starts it: Target, Precursor, or Target or precursor.
                      Attention on a schedule
                      In Play (control), attention every 30 s independent of behavior, withheld for 5 s after a target, then the schedule resumes. The runner cues each delivery.
                      Three-step prompting
                      Vocal, then model, then physical, each after the step interval on the Demand card (every 5–10 s). Compliance at any step is praised.
                      Session termination criteria
                      Written before the first session and applied in every condition, for example any tissue damage or three head hits within 10 s. A session that meets one ends at once and is marked Term.
                      Analysis termination criteria
                      When the analysis as a whole stops: for example, the structured criteria met, five series without differentiation, an injury, or a time limit.
                      Criterion lines (UCL, LCL)
                      One standard deviation above and below the control condition’s mean. The lower line is set at zero when the standard deviation exceeds the mean (Hagopian et al., 1997; Roane et al., 2013).
                      u, l and n
                      For one test condition: the points above the upper line, the points below the lower line, and all its points. The rule compares u − l with n / 2.
                      Differentiated
                      u − l is at least n / 2 and the condition’s mean is at least 1.5 times the control mean, the magnitude floor this form applies. For latency the rule is inverted.
                      Undifferentiated
                      No test condition meets the rule. EA-1’s next steps, in order: the extended-alone screen, a pairwise test, establishing operations modified from interview data, then a synthesized analysis.
                      Extended-alone screen
                      Five consecutive 5-min alone or no-interaction sessions (Querim et al., 2013). Persistence indicates automatic reinforcement; extinction, a social function to test next.
                      Pairwise test
                      One test condition alternated with its own control (Iwata et al., 1994), used for the strongest candidate or when a hypothesis is already strong.
                      Latency and ceiling
                      The seconds from the start of a session to the first target. A session with no response is scored at the ceiling, 10 min (600 s) in the latency template.
                      Trial (control segment, test segment)
                      A trial-based analysis runs a control segment, then a test segment, embedded in the class activity; each ends at the first response and is scored 1 or 0.
                      Precursor
                      A response that reliably precedes the severe behavior, defined on Setup & safety. In a precursor analysis the programmed consequence follows it instead of the severe behavior: the data collector taps Precursor for it, and Target for the severe behavior.
                      Synthesized test and matched control
                      The IISCA’s two conditions: all interview-reported establishing operations together, with all the reinforcers after a target or precursor; and the same reinforcers freely available with no establishing operations.
                      IOA index
                      How two observers’ records are compared: Interval-by-interval, Exact count per interval, Proportional (smaller / larger per interval), or Total count, which compares only the session totals.
                      Procedural fidelity
                      Whether a session was run as its card says, scored from the card’s Fidelity checklist items on a planned share of sessions.
                      Phase label
                      The label in each row of the log, such as FA, Ext. alone or Pairwise. The graph draws a phase line wherever it changes, and Interpretation can read one phase at a time.
