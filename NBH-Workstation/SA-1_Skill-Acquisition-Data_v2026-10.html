Mastery Criterion

The workstation’s standard (the BCBA, 11/2): for the whole set, 100% independent across 3 consecutive teaching sessions with 2 instructors; applied target by target, each target independent on every trial of it in 2 consecutive teaching sessions in which it was presented. The evidence (Richling, Williams & Carr, 2019): 100% across three sessions kept the highest level a week later in 11 of 12 evaluations (a mean of 86% correct across the 12). In the one direct test of 90% across three sessions (4 children), maintenance one week later averaged 10%. 100% took about one more session than 90% (a mean of 14 against 13). The samples are small. A level below 100% is the team’s choice: write the reason here; its first maintenance probe is at 1 week and decides whether it held (the Probes page).

What This Form Builds

A skill-acquisition program is the teaching side of the plan. Form GB-1 writes the goal, Form TD-1 names the replacement skill the plan depends on, and this form records how the skill is taught and whether it is being learned. Its first page states what every instructor needs in order to run the same trial: the discriminative stimulus, the definition of a correct response, the time allowed, the prompt hierarchy and its codes, the error-correction procedure, the reinforcement schedule (Form SR-1), and the mastery criterion the data will be judged against. The pages after it are the data: a trial-by-trial grid for discrete-trial or natural-environment teaching, a step-by-step grid for a task analysis, a graph of percent independent per session with the mastery line and the phase changes, and the maintenance and generalization probes that decide when the program is finished.

Discrete-trial teaching presents learning opportunities as a series of trials, each with a clear instruction, a response, a consequence and a short pause before the next trial; it is well suited to establishing new responses and discriminations that the student does not pick up from the environment, and it must be joined to teaching in the natural setting if the skill is to be used there (Smith, 2001). Green (2001) sets out the stimulus-control side: a skill is learned when the student’s response comes under the control of the instruction or the natural cue rather than the prompt, which is why every prompt hierarchy on this form ends with a plan to fade. The task-analysis page follows the chaining literature summarized in Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020): forward, backward and total-task presentation, with the training step moving as steps are mastered.

The decision rules are the form’s working conventions and they are printed beside the graph so they can be changed: at criterion for the set number of consecutive sessions and instructors, the skill is mastered and moves to maintenance and generalization probes; no change over five teaching sessions (read from the line of progress through them) means the procedure is changed rather than continued, after the checklist on the Graph page has been worked through in order (fidelity, prerequisites, the reinforcer, the procedure, prompt dependence; v25.65); a maintenance probe below criterion calls for booster sessions. None of these numbers is a published threshold.

What the Research Supports

Discrete-trial teaching: what it is for and what it is notSmith (2001) describes the trial as a unit (instruction, response, consequence, inter-trial interval) and the method’s strengths: a high rate of learning opportunities, clear data, and the ability to teach discriminations and new response forms that a student does not acquire from the environment. He also lists its limits: skills taught only at the table may not be used elsewhere, students may wait for prompts, and the method must be combined with teaching in natural settings. The format field, the generalization plan and the probes page follow that advice.
Transfer of stimulus control and delayed promptingTouchette and Howard (1984) taught discriminations with a prompt that was delayed a little more over trials, so that the response could occur before the prompt. Control moved from the prompt to the task stimulus, and the transfer was quicker when the reinforcement contingencies favored responses made before the prompt over responses made after it. The form’s separate reinforcement lines for independent and prompted responses, and the time-delay field, come from this.
Most-to-least against least-to-mostLibby, Weiss, Bancroft and Ahearn (2008) compared the two hierarchies while teaching play chains to children with autism. Least-to-most produced acquisition in fewer sessions for some learners at the cost of many more errors; most-to-least produced few errors but was slower for some; most-to-least with a short delay before the prompt combined accuracy with speed. The hierarchy field, the delay field and the fading rule let the team write the arrangement they chose and change it when the error data say so.
No single prompt hierarchy is bestNo one hierarchy was the most efficient across learners. Teaching chains to learners with autism, Seaver and Bourret (2014) found delay fading the most efficient fading method for 4 learners and least-to-most for 3; most-to-least was never the most efficient. The model prompt was the most efficient prompt type for 6 of their 10 learners, and the physical or the verbal-and-gestural prompt for the others. In listener (conditional discrimination) training, least-to-most was the most efficient for every participant (Schnell et al., 2020). A randomized trial of constant time delay, most-to-least and flexible prompt fading with 27 children found minimal differences (Cihon et al., 2020). Delayed prompting gave few errors, and transfer was faster when reinforcement favored the response made before the prompt (Touchette & Howard, 1984); a 5-s constant delay taught cooking chains with under 9% errors, and the skills held at 85% or more over 3 months (Schuster et al., 1988). So the form does not default to most-to-least. When errors are many, it suggests a progressive delay with the prompt type that has worked, or a short comparison of two fading methods with this student, and lists most-to-least third.
Physical guidance only while the student allows itThe research this form’s prompts come from is hands-on: schedules and chains were taught with manual guidance from behind, faded as fast as possible to a light touch, shadowing and distance (MacDuff et al., 1993). So the limit is written down. A physical prompt is given only while the student allows it; when the student pulls away, stiffens or pushes the adult’s hand, the adult lets go, waits, and offers a lighter prompt or a short break. For 3 adults with profound multiple disabilities, a teaching program that gave a brief break and a preferred activity after resistance reduced resistance and signs of unhappiness, and the teaching steps did not suffer (Green et al., 2005). Assent is rarely reported in the research, and its withdrawal can happen at any time (Morris et al., 2021). Giving up guidance often costs nothing: the model prompt was more efficient than the physical prompt for 6 of 10 learners (Seaver & Bourret, 2014). Each session counts the times the student pulled away; when that happens in two of the last five sessions, the rules say the guidance is not accepted and the prompt type, the session length or the breaks should change. Holding a hand, an arm or any body part to stop a response, or as a consequence, is restraint and is never written as a prompt. Guided compliance (physical guidance after a refusal) is escape extinction, not a teaching prompt: if a plan uses it, it is written on Form TD-1, marked as extinction, and it is never the default.
Forward and backward chainingSlocum and Tiger (2011) taught chains with forward and backward chaining. Neither method was consistently more efficient, and a short task did not predict how a long one would go. In the choice test, three of the four children showed no preference between the two methods, and one preferred forward chaining. The chaining type is therefore a choice the team records, not a rule; total-task presentation is included because it is the common classroom arrangement for a short chain. What happens to the steps after the training step is a choice too: with forward chaining, 5 of 7 children learned fastest when they were guided through the remaining steps and 2 of 7 when the teacher completed them, and not completing them gave the shortest sessions (Bancroft et al., 2011). Guiding through the steps is subject to the rule above. A constant time delay worked well for chains (Schuster et al., 1988), so the Steps page has the Delay/level row and the time-delay rule too.
Teaching a visual scheduleA schedule handed to a student without teaching is decoration. When Form VS-1 has made a schedule, the Setup page offers a program for it: a task analysis of using it (go to the schedule; look at or touch the picture; get the materials; do the activity; put them away; mark it Done; go to the next one), taught total-task, from behind the student with as little talk as possible, guidance faded to a light touch, then shadowing, then distance (MacDuff et al., 1993). The schedule is the prompt that stays: fade the adult, not the schedule. When the schedules were removed, responding fell to baseline levels (Brodhead et al., 2014, 2018).
Stimulus prompts and fadingA stimulus prompt changes the material rather than adding the adult’s help. Children with autism always failed to learn with an extra-stimulus prompt (a cue added beside the stimulus) and usually learned with a within-stimulus prompt (the relevant feature exaggerated, then faded; Schreibman, 1975). Words alone were learned fastest by 5 of 6 students, and pictures beside the words slowed sight-word reading (Didden et al., 2000); fading the picture in only as needed reduced that interference (Richardson et al., 2017). An identity-matching (picture) prompt in a least-to-most sequence sped up listener learning, for all 4 children in Carp et al. (2012), after Fisher et al. (2007).
Video model and video promptVideo modeling taught conversation to 3 boys with autism; it generalized to new topics, people and settings and held over 15 months (Charlop & Milstein, 1989). Video prompting (each step shown, then done) taught job tasks to all 8 young adults in a transition program; the skills held over 3 months and in a new setting with new materials, and an error led to rewatching the step, then a model (Schroeder et al., 2023). The clips show a peer or an adult, not the student, unless the family’s written consent covers it.
Trial-by-trial against first-trial dataLerman, Dittlinger, Fentress and Lanagan (2011) compared data collected on every trial with data collected on the first trial only, and found that first-trial data gave a rough estimate of session performance for most children and skills, but frequently indicated mastery prematurely and were insensitive to early changes in performance. Cummings and Carr (2009) compared continuous and discontinuous (first-trial) measurement in a program for children with autism: skills measured by first trial reached the mastery decision in fewer sessions, and skills measured on every trial were somewhat better maintained. The grid records every trial and flags the first, so a team that must sample can see what the sample would have said.
The mastery criterion predicts maintenance(v25.65) In Richling et al.’s one direct test of 90% across three sessions, with 4 children, maintenance a week later averaged 10%, against a mean of 86% for 100% across the 12 evaluations; the samples are small. At 10 trials a session, 90% allows one error, the margin they found to matter. They ran six to nine sessions a day in that experiment and name the time between sessions as the next question, so the option that the sessions fall on at least 2 different days is a caution, not a tested rule. With a level below 100%, the first maintenance probe is due 1 week after mastery, and it decides whether that level held. Richling, Williams and Carr (2019) surveyed practitioners, who most often reported 80% across three consecutive sessions, and then compared criteria held across three sessions in four experiments: 60%, 80% and 100% in the first, and 80%, 90% and 100% in the last. Across the 12 evaluations, 100% across three sessions gave the highest level at the follow-up a week later in all but one, and 80% across three sessions maintained in only about half; in the last experiment 90% across three sessions also maintained poorly, which the authors call preliminary evidence against it. 100% took a mean of 14 sessions to reach and 90% a mean of 13. They describe Fuller and Fienup (2018) as finding that 90% in a single 20-trial session maintained better than 80% or 50%. The workstation’s standard is therefore 100% across 3 sessions with 2 instructors (the BCBA, 11/2), for every skill including a safety skill; a lower level is recorded with its reason on the reason line.
Mastery judged target by targetApplying the criterion to each target, and replacing a target as soon as it meets it, against applying it to the whole set: learners acquired sight words faster with the target-by-target criterion, and maintenance was similar, improving when the criterion was 100% across two sessions rather than one (Wong & Fienup, 2022); Kim, Fienup, Draus and Wong (2023) replicated the faster learning. With tacts, intraverbals and listener (match-to-sample) responses, the target criterion took fewer sessions for 3 of 5 learners in tact training and for both learners in listener training, but 20% of the listener targets it called mastered showed overselection, a false positive (Cordeiro et al., 2022). This form’s option uses 100% across two consecutive teaching sessions in which the target was presented, and for a listener program asks for a mixed-array check (every comparison in every position) before a target counts as mastered.
Error correction is chosen per learnerCarroll, Joachim, St. Peter and Robinson (2015) compared four error-correction procedures during discrete-trial instruction with 5 children (2 with ADHD, 3 with autism): single-response repetition, remove and re-present, re-present until independent, and multiple-response repetition, against a control. Re-present until independent was the most efficient for 3 of the 5 children and the second most efficient for the other 2; single-response repetition was the most efficient for one child, and remove and re-present for another. They did not test a model alone. Across the later comparisons reviewed by Cariveau et al. (2019), demonstration (a model, no response required) was the most efficient procedure for 8 of 13 learners, an active student response for 5 of 14, remove and re-present for 1 of 5, and re-present until correct for 3 of 14. Multiple-response repetition beat single-response repetition for all 6 adults reading sight words (Worsdell et al., 2005), but it was the most efficient for no child in the later comparisons. Demonstration was the most efficient, or one of the two most efficient, for 4 of 5 children, who echoed the model; the child who rarely echoed it did not learn that way (Kodak et al., 2016). A short test predicts well: a brief assessment matched later teaching for 2 of 4 learners and partly for the other 2 (Carroll et al., 2018), and McGhan and Lerman (2013) found a match for 4 of 5. The menu lists the procedures under their research names, and the Setup page has a brief comparison: a small set with each of two or three procedures for up to 5 sessions, the one with the most correct responses kept and then confirmed on a new set. Inserting mastered trials between the prompt and the re-presentation gave no benefit for most of 4 children (Plaisance et al., 2016), so it is not offered.
Generalization is programmed, not hoped forStokes and Baer (1977) reviewed the generalization literature and named the tactics that worked: train sufficient exemplars, train loosely, program common stimuli, use indiscriminable contingencies, introduce the behavior to natural maintaining contingencies, mediate generalization, and train to generalize, against the common practice of “train and hope”. The generalization plan on the Setup page and the dimension column on the Probes page are the record of which tactics were used and which dimensions were tested.
Probes instead of a continuous baselineHorner and Baer (1978) describe the multiple-probe technique: baseline measured by intermittent probes rather than every session, for skills where a long untreated baseline would be pointless or aversive. The phase code B on the grids marks a probe or baseline session; the phase line on the graph separates it from teaching.
Receptive and discrimination programs need their own careGrow and LeBlanc (2013) give recommendations for receptive-language instruction: balanced arrays with the target in each position equally, rotation of the comparison stimuli, a prompt-fading procedure chosen in advance, interspersal of mastered targets, and a data system that catches position and other faulty stimulus control. The materials, interspersal and error-correction lines are there so these choices are written down.
The prompting procedures by nameThe system of least prompts, constant and progressive time delay, graduated guidance and most-to-least prompting are defined and compared in Wolery, Ault and Doyle (1992), and the response prompts (verbal, model, physical), stimulus control transfer and the three chaining formats are defined in Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020). The code table on the Setup page uses those names and lets the team change the codes to the ones their sheets already use. A new program codes verbal (V) and model (M) prompts apart (v25.65): the model prompt was the most efficient type for 6 of 10 learners, the physical prompt for 2 and the vocal-plus-gestural prompt for 1 (Seaver & Bourret, 2014), so one combined code would hide the difference. The prompt type a child had been taught with most recently was the more efficient one (Coon & Miguel, 2012), so the Setup page asks which prompt type is tried first, and why. A file saved before keeps its own codes.
When progress stalls: check before changing the teachingProgrammed fidelity errors slowed or prevented learning in discrete-trial instruction (Carroll et al., 2013), so the first check is a second adult scoring a session (Form TI-1). A skills assessment (matching, imitation, scanning, and telling sounds and pictures apart) was related to the outcome of listener training for 7 of 9 students (Kodak et al., 2015), and Kodak et al. (2022) replicated the assessment, so a listener program checks those skills next. Then the reinforcer (Form PA-1), then the procedure (a brief error-correction or prompt comparison). For learners who answer correctly only after the prompt, differential reinforcement, prompt fading and a longer response interval each worked for someone, and the best one differed by learner (Gorgan & Kodak, 2019); a moderate item after prompted responses and the best item after independent ones worked best for 3 of 4 (Cividini-Motta & Ahearn, 2013). The checklist on the Graph page is ticked as each check is done, and prints with the decision.
Problem behavior during teachingProblem behavior was higher with demands and with harder tasks, and errorless teaching reduced both errors and problem behavior in visual discrimination tasks, though not in perceptual-motor tasks (Weeks & Gaylord-Ross, 1981). Easy requests mixed in among the instructional trials reduced aggression and self-injury during instruction (Horner et al., 1991). The evidence is mixed: in Knutson et al. (2019), mixing in easy tasks did not reduce problem behavior more than teaching only new targets. Each session therefore records its count of problem behavior, so the team can see which applies; when the count and the errors rise together, the rules say so.
Interspersal and the inter-trial intervalMixing mastered targets in costs speed. Massed trials gave the fastest acquisition for 5 of 6 children (Majdalany et al., 2014), and teaching only new targets (0 mastered to 1 new) was the most efficient ratio for all 4 children (Knutson et al., 2019). Interspersal is used for a reason, such as maintenance or tolerance of demands, and the reason is written on the Setup page. Short inter-trial intervals in a varied format were the most efficient (Cariveau et al., 2016), so 3 to 5 s is a sound default. For practice spread over a week, sessions spread across the week beat one long session for typically developing children (Haq & Kodak, 2015).
Instructive feedbackExtra information given after the consequence, which the student is not asked to repeat, can teach more than the target. Progressive delay with instructive feedback made teaching more efficient, and 58% to 92% of the skills acquired were maintained 8 to 9 weeks later (Reichow & Wolery, 2011). The Setup page has a line for it, and the Probes page a probe type to test it.
Natural environment teaching: who starts the trialIncidental teaching produced more generalization and more spontaneous use than structured training (McGee et al., 1985), and unprompted use rose in free play after incidental teaching (Hart & Risley, 1975). Play-based teaching with a brief period of teacher-led delay trials was the most effective and was preferred by most children; teaching only in play was the least effective (Heal et al., 2009), which is why the form keeps both formats. In natural environment teaching, each trial records who started it, and the Probes page has a probe for spontaneous use in free play.

How the Figures Are Calculated

Percent independent (trials)Trials coded with an independent code ÷ trials scored in the session × 100. Empty cells are not scored. The number of trials per session sets how many rows the grid reads.
Percent correct (prompted included)Trials coded independent or prompted ÷ trials scored × 100. The gap between the two lines on the graph is the prompted share; a wide gap that does not close is a fading problem.
First trialThe code in trial 1 of each session. The metric reports how many of the last sessions began with an independent response; with the box ticked on the Setup page, the mastery rule also requires it.
Percent of steps independent (task analysis)Steps coded independent ÷ steps scored in the session × 100, over the steps listed.
Step status and current training stepA step is mastered when it was independent on the last two sessions in which it was scored (working convention). Forward chaining: the current training step is the first step not mastered; backward chaining: the last; total-task: every step. An entry in the override field replaces the computed step.
Mastery ruleThe last N teaching-phase sessions (N from the criterion) all at or above the criterion percent, with at least the set number of different instructor initials among them (and, if ticked, an independent first trial in each). Maintenance-phase and baseline-phase sessions are not counted. With at least 2 different days ticked (v25.65), the sessions in the window must carry at least two different dates. With the criterion applied to each target (v25.64): each trial is tagged with its target in the row Target per trial under the grid (the tags listed on the Setup page), and a target is mastered when it was independent on every trial of it in 2 consecutive teaching sessions in which it was presented; in a listener program a target counts only once its mixed-array check is marked Passed. The program is mastered when every listed target is, with at least the set number of instructors across the sessions that mastered them. Replace each mastered target with a new one and add it to the list.
No-change ruleAt least five teaching sessions, none of the last five at criterion, and the least-squares line through the last five (the line of progress, drawn on the graph) rising fewer than 10 percentage points from the first of the five to the last: the form says to change the procedure. The rule also prints the mean of the last 3 of the five against the first 3, and says so when the two readings differ. Two single sessions decided the rule before v25.64, so two bad days at the ends could hide a rise (50, 20, 30, 40, 55 read as no change; its line rises 12 points). Lines of progress raised agreement between raters on changes in trend from 66% to 84% and in level from 73% to 85% (Bailey, 1984), and least squares was the most accurate of five trend methods (Aydin, 2026). The five-session window and the 10-point margin are working conventions. Under most-to-least prompting or graduated guidance, or when the five sessions all ran at a 0-s delay, independent responding is near 0 by design until the prompt is faded, so the rule reads the Delay/level row instead (a delay in seconds, or the prompt level by its code or a word of its meaning; without it, the prompt codes in the session’s cells): a level that falls or a delay that grows is progress, and no change in it over the five sessions means the prompt is not being faded (v23.21). When the rule fires it does not name one change: it points to the checklist on the Graph page (fidelity on Form TI-1, prerequisites, the reinforcer, the procedure, prompt dependence), in that order (v25.65), and opens the brief error-correction comparison on the Setup page. When the last 5 teaching sessions average 80% or more correct with prompts but under 50% independent (not under most-to-least, graduated guidance or a 0-s delay, where that is by design), the rules name the three options for prompt dependence: stronger differential reinforcement, faster fading, or a longer wait before the prompt (working conventions).
Error ruleUnder least-to-most a trial is coded at the level that finally worked, so the errors made at the lighter levels go in the row Trials with an error before the prompt under the grid. When the last three teaching sessions average 30% or more such trials, or under 50% correct even with prompts, the form suggests, in this order: a progressive delay (0 s, then 1, 2, 4 s) with the prompt type that has worked; or a comparison of two fading methods on two small sets for up to 5 sessions; or, third, most-to-least (v25.65; Seaver & Bourret, 2014; Touchette & Howard, 1984; Libby et al., 2008). Both figures are working conventions.
Guidance ruleEach session’s row Student pulled away from guidance (times) counts the times the student pulled away, stiffened or pushed the adult’s hand during a physical prompt. When it is above 0 in two of the last five sessions of the graphed grid, the rules say: guidance is not accepted; change the prompt type (model, gesture, video), shorten the session or add breaks (Form PA-1 reinforcer check). Two of five is a working convention.
Problem-behavior ruleEach session’s row Problem behavior this session (count). When the count rose across the last three sessions of the graphed grid (each above the one before), and the errors rose with it (the errors before the prompt, or else the trials in error), the rules say the errors may be making the task aversive and name what to try. With the count rising alone, they say so. These are working conventions.
Time-delay ruleUnder constant or progressive time delay the Delay row under the trial grid says the delay each session ran, and the form reads the latest teaching session: a 0-s session with every trial correct moves to the delay, a delayed session with more than 20% of trials in error (counted from Trials with an error before the prompt when that row is filled in: an error made before the prompt is the one the delay is about) returns to 0 s for a session, and under progressive delay a session at or above the criterion percent correct with no error lengthens the delay by its next step (working conventions after Wolery, Ault, & Doyle, 1992).
Maintenance ruleA maintenance-phase session or a maintenance probe below the criterion percent calls for booster sessions.
Probe resultIndependent ÷ trials × 100, passed at or above the criterion percent (working convention); a probe with more independent responses than trials is not scored, and its % cell says to check it. The metrics count maintenance probes passed, the generalization dimensions probed and passed, and the settings or material sets passed against the number on the Setup page: a passed generalization probe whose dimension is Setting or Materials counts once per distinct condition.
Instructor countDistinct non-blank initials in the instructor row of the sessions in the mastery window, read without capitals, spaces or points (jn, JN and J.N. are one instructor; v23.21). Blank initials count as no instructor, so the mastery verdict says when initials are missing. The initials in brackets in “Who teaches” are offered in the instructor row.
Agreement (IOA)For a session a second observer coded independently (their codes typed in the row Observer 2 under the grid, in trial or step order), the trials or steps both coded with the same code ÷ the trials or steps both coded × 100 (trial-by-trial agreement; Cooper, Heron & Heward, 2020). The metrics give the sessions with agreement and the mean; the rules say when none of the last 10 sessions had one (working convention). The second observer’s codes are not graphed.
Criterion from the objectiveWhen the case carries the Form GB-1 objective, its percentage and its count fill the Setup criterion when it is empty. When the Setup criterion is below the objective, the Setup page and the Graph say so, and the case calls the program mastered (for GB-1’s progress report) only when the objective’s numbers are met too.
Maintenance probes dueFrom the mastery date (the session that first completed the mastery window) and the schedule on the Probes page (“1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month”, the default), the next maintenance probe is on the workstation’s due list; a maintenance probe dated within 3 days before a due date, or after it, counts for it.

Running the Sheet

Score the response, not the correctionThe code in the cell is what happened after the SD, before any correction. A trial that is corrected keeps its original code; the correction is procedure, not data.
Baseline before teachingTwo probe sessions under the probe rule (working convention) before any prompting, so the graph shows what teaching added, and a third when the two differ by more than 20 percentage points or the second is higher than the first: the dual-criterion checks kept false positives low only with at least 3 points in the first phase (Lanovaz et al., 2017). A baseline already at criterion means a harder target. Every trial is recorded, not a sample: sampling only the first trials underestimated the sessions to mastery, and sampling sessions overestimated them (Carey & Bourret, 2014).
Two instructors earlyThe criterion asks for two instructors; the second one should start running sessions before the end, not after it, so that generalization across people is being taught rather than tested.
Fade on the dataThe fading rule on the Setup page is applied after each session; the session note records the level used. A prompt level that stays for many sessions is itself a reason to look at the reinforcer.
Keep the sheet with the programSave the data file with the program name in it; the CSV export carries every session, step and probe into a spreadsheet; the PDF print is the record for the file. A student taught two or more skills keeps them in one file: Add a program in the toolbar, and choose the one shown beside it. Each program has its own setup, sessions, steps and probes; the student details are shared; the case holds every program and Form GB-1 reads each. The print and the blank sheets are of the program shown (v23.21).

Writing help

A multi-line text box on this form shows an Improve wording button at its corner when you are in it and whenever it holds text; from a keyboard, Alt+Enter in the box (Option+Return on a Mac or iPad) opens the same panel. Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. Rewrite with Claude works only in the forms opened from the website: a single-use passcode from your BCBA unlocks the tab (in the workstation, every form in that tab) until the session’s time is up or the tab is closed; press Lock when you finish on a shared iPad. Before anything is sent, the student’s name and ID become placeholders such as [Student], other names can be added under Also hide, and the panel shows exactly what will be sent; the names are put back in the answer. Use it for real cases only with your district’s or agency’s approval. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated program is a break-request (FCT) program taught by discrete trials with most-to-least prompting, two baseline probes, nine teaching sessions rising to criterion with two instructors, one maintenance session, an eight-step hand-washing task analysis in forward chaining, and four probes, one of them below criterion.
