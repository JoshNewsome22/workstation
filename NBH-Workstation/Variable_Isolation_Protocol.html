How to Use This Protocol

Scatter plots and ABC data show when and around what a behavior occurs. A functional analysis (FA) shows what maintains it by manipulating variables directly. This protocol fills the gap: it applies formal rules of eliminative inference to descriptive data so that candidate variables are systematically ruled out, not just noticed.

When This Form Is the Right Choice

The Logic in One Paragraph

Mill (1843) described four methods of experimental inquiry for inferring cause; this form applies them to observational data. Agreement: if a behavior occurs under otherwise different circumstances, the one condition they share is a candidate cause. Difference: if two situations are alike in every respect except one, and the behavior occurs in only one of them, the differing condition is a candidate cause. Concomitant variation: if the behavior changes in magnitude as a condition changes in magnitude, the two are causally linked or share a common cause. Residues: subtract what is already explained; what remains is attributable to what remains. Mill's methods are eliminative: their power comes from ruling variables out. The Agreement, Difference and Concomitant variation views apply them. The Contingency view adds the quantitative tools developed for descriptive analysis in behavior analysis (conditional probability and contingency space analysis). Synthesis & decision forces every surviving hypothesis to compete against the full evidence set and rates confidence with criteria adapted from Bradford Hill (1965).

What this form cannot do. Descriptive methods identify correlation, not function. Consequences that reliably follow behavior are not necessarily reinforcing it, and consequences delivered at high background rates (attention in a busy classroom, for example) can look contingent when they are not. In Thompson and Iwata (2007), attention was the most common consequence in the descriptive analyses of 8 of 12 participants but maintained problem behavior for only 2 of them, and the consequence observed most often was the maintaining one in only 3 of the 12 cases; Lerman and Iwata (1993) reported agreement between descriptive and experimental analyses in a minority of cases. Treat the output of this protocol as a ranked, evidence-weighted hypothesis set that specifies exactly which conditions an FA (or a treatment-as-test) should compare.

Steps

  1. Setup. Define the behavior, inventory the data you already have, and record why an FA is being deferred.
  2. Candidates. Build the widest reasonable list of setting events/MOs, antecedents, and consequences. Nothing is eliminated yet.
  3. Agreement. Log 5–10 occurrences and code every candidate as present, absent, or unknown. Any candidate absent during an occurrence cannot be a necessary condition.
  4. Difference. Match each occurrence with a non-occurrence as similar as possible (same period, staff, setting). Identify what differed. Non-occurrence data are the most under-collected data in school assessment and the most informative.
  5. Concomitant variation. For candidates that come in degrees (sleep, task difficulty, time since attention), test whether behavior tracks the level of the variable.
  6. Contingency. Compute whether consequences are actually more likely after behavior than at other times, and whether behavior is more likely after specific antecedents.
  7. Synthesis. Score competing hypotheses, account for residues, rate causal confidence, and choose the next step.

Method of Agreement: Occurrence Ledger

Log occurrences of the behavior across as many different circumstances as possible. Code every candidate as present, absent, or unknown for each occurrence.

Rule of inference. If a candidate was absent during any occurrence, it is not a necessary condition for the behavior. Candidates present in every coded occurrence survive. Agreement cannot confirm a cause: several survivors may remain, and a survivor may be a constant of the setting ("in classroom") rather than a controlling variable. Diversity of occurrences is what gives this method its power; ten occurrences during math with the same teacher eliminate almost nothing.

Multiply controlled behavior. If a candidate is absent in some occurrences but present in a clear subset (all morning episodes, for example), do not discard it. Split the occurrences into subsets and run the ledger separately. The behavior may have more than one function, which Hagopian et al. (2013) found in a substantial share of inpatient FAs.

Method of Difference: Matched Non-Occurrence Pairs

For each occurrence, find a comparison period when the behavior did not occur under conditions as close as possible to the occurrence. Then identify what differed.

Rule of inference. If an occurrence and a non-occurrence share every candidate except one, that one candidate is implicated. The fewer the differences, the stronger the inference; a pair that differs on four variables tells you only that one of the four matters. Combined with Agreement, this is Mill's joint method, which Mill judged a great advantage over agreement alone: a variable present across all occurrences and absent across all matched non-occurrences is the strongest evidence this protocol can give short of an experiment. That holds for an antecedent. A consequence follows the behavior by definition, so it cannot be present in a period without the behavior and the joint method says nothing about it: test it by its contingency in the Contingency view (Martens et al., 2008). A setting event or motivating operation raises the odds on the days it is present, so it is not expected in every occurrence: absence in some occurrences does not set it aside; test it in Concomitant variation, by the days with it and the days without it. This is also the logic of a structured descriptive assessment (Anderson & Long, 2002) with the antecedent arranged by circumstance rather than by the analyst.

How to Sample Non-Occurrences

  1. Use the scatter plot to find periods with the same activity, staff, and time of day where behavior was absent.
  2. Prefer the same day where possible; setting events are then held constant automatically.
  3. For low-rate behavior, schedule 3–5 brief observations per week in the highest-probability period and log them whether or not behavior occurs. A non-occurrence in the high-risk period is informative.
  4. Code the matched non-occurrence of each pair in the ledger below, for the same candidates as the Agreement ledger; the occurrence’s codes are read from Agreement by the row number entered as Occ. #.

Method of Concomitant Variation: Dose–Response Check

Some candidates are not simply present or absent; they vary in degree. If the behavior rises and falls with the level of the candidate, the two are linked, either directly or through a common cause.

Rule of inference. A monotonic relation between the level of a candidate and the level of behavior supports the candidate; a flat relation across a meaningful range weakens it. This is Bradford Hill's "biological gradient" criterion. Correlation can arise from a third variable (both task difficulty and problem behavior rise after lunch because of a schedule effect, for example), so check survivors against the matched pairs in Difference.

Setting Events: Days With and Without

A setting event or motivating operation is not tested by agreement or by matched pairs: it raises the odds on the days it is present. Compare the behavior on the days with it and the days without it, from the daily record (Form DD-1) and the log that records the setting event (home note, nurse’s log, attendance). The reading is this form’s convention, not an empirical cut-off: with at least 5 days on each side, a rate on the days with it at least 1.5 times the rate without it supports the setting event, two thirds of it or less suggests the opposite, and anything between shows no clear difference.

Contingency Analysis from Descriptive Data

A consequence is only a candidate reinforcer if it is more likely after the behavior than at other times. A consequence that is equally likely at all times is noncontingent, whatever the ABC narrative looks like.

Rule of inference. Compare the probability of the consequence given the behavior, P(C | B), with the probability of the consequence in the absence of the behavior, P(C | no B). A positive contingency exists when P(C | B) exceeds P(C | no B) (Martens et al., 2008; Vollmer et al., 2001, compared P(C | B) with the probability of the consequence at randomly selected points instead). Neither source sets how large the difference must be: Martens et al. knew of no decision rules for the degree of contingency. The same comparison run in the antecedent direction, P(B | A) versus P(B | no A), tests whether an antecedent actually raises the likelihood of behavior. In Pence et al. (2009), three descriptive methods, including a comparison of conditional with background probabilities, gave similar outcomes for all but 1 of 6 participants, and for all but 1 the descriptive outcome differed substantially from the FA. Samaha et al. (2009) found that consequences identified as reinforcers by FA followed problem behavior in natural observation after a range of delays and frequencies.

Data Requirements

These calculations need counts, not narrative ABC. Consequence side: Form ABC-1 supplies the four figures for each consequence: the incidents (nB) and how many were followed by it, and the sampled no-behavior windows (n¬B) and how many were followed by it, with its p(C | B) and p(C | no B); enter them as they are. Antecedent side: P(B | A) needs the antecedent coded in every interval, with and without the behavior, which ABC-1 does not record; with ABC-1’s incidents and samples, set “How the counts were taken” below, and the classification uses the odds ratio and Yule’s Q, which do not move with how many windows were sampled (ABC-1 reports the same figures; a risk ratio across its two sampling frames does). For interval recording, record the observation in consecutive intervals (10 s is standard). Mark each interval for occurrence of the behavior and each candidate consequence within the analysis window (usually the same interval or the next). "Non-behavior intervals" are all intervals in which the behavior did not occur. Count C for them in the same window, on the same terms: a wider window after behavior than after other intervals raises P(C | B) by itself. Sample sessions across the periods where behavior is most and least likely so that P(C | no B) reflects true background rates.

With Form ABC-1’s counts, an antecedent row takes: with A, the incidents and the sampled windows with the antecedent in place, and of them the incidents (…with behavior); without A, the same with it not in place. The odds ratio and Yule’s Q then match ABC-1’s antecedent table.

Contingency space: one point per consequence row, P(C | ¬B) across and P(C | B) up. The diagonal is no contingency; a point above it is a positive contingency, below it a negative one, and the farther from the diagonal the stronger (Martens et al., 2008).

Classification thresholds are set here as a working convention, judged on the difference as shown, to two decimals: +.20 or more "positive contingency," +.05 to +.19 "weak positive," −.04 to +.04 "no contingency (noncontingent)," −.05 or lower "negative contingency." Martens et al. (2008) plot P(C | B) against P(C | ¬B) in a contingency space; points near the diagonal indicate no contingency regardless of how high P(C | B) is. A high P(C | ¬B) means the environment delivers that consequence freely, a warning that the ABC data may be misleading; the table flags P(C | ¬B) of .30 or more.

The ratio P(B | A) ÷ P(B | ¬A) is a relative-risk analogue, for interval counts; each row also shows the odds ratio and Yule’s Q, and with incident-plus-sample counts (Form ABC-1) the classification uses Q: |Q| of .20, .43 and .60 weak, moderate and strong, positive evocative and negative abative (ABC-1’s bands). A ratio near 1 means the antecedent does not change the likelihood of behavior; the working convention here labels ≥ 2.0 "evocative," 1.3–1.99 "weakly evocative," 0.77–1.29 "no effect," and < 0.77 "abative," judged on the ratio as shown, to two decimals. Base rates matter: P(B | A) cannot exceed 1, so once behavior occurs in half or more of the intervals without the antecedent (P(B | ¬A) of .50 or more, flagged in the table) the ratio cannot rise much above 2.0 however reliably the antecedent evokes behavior, and interval size should be reduced.

Cause precedes effect (Hill's temporality). Short, consistent antecedent-to-behavior latencies support antecedent control; long or variable latencies suggest a setting event or an antecedent you have not yet identified. Consequences delivered after long delays are weaker candidates for reinforcement.

Synthesis and Decision

Every surviving hypothesis now competes against every piece of evidence. The goal is not to find the hypothesis with the most support but the one with the least disconfirming evidence (Heuer, 1999).

Competing Hypotheses Matrix

Edit the hypothesis headers to be specific ("Escape from independent written work" rather than "Escape"). List each item of evidence from Agreement to Contingency as a row. For each cell, code whether the evidence is C consistent with the hypothesis, I inconsistent with it, or N neutral. Evidence that is consistent with every hypothesis may have no diagnostic value; evidence that is inconsistent with all but one is the most diagnostic, and ACH asks how sensitive the conclusion is to such critical items (Heuer, 1999). Standing: the hypothesis with the fewest inconsistent findings is "least disconfirmed" (tied hypotheses share it; with only one hypothesis coded there is no comparison), any other with 3 or more is "disconfirmed," and the rest are "retained." The cut-off of 3 is this form's convention.

Method of Residues

Rule of inference. Subtract the portion of the behavior accounted for by the leading hypothesis. If a meaningful residue remains (occurrences the hypothesis cannot explain), that residue is the effect of some other variable: a second function, an unidentified setting event, or a measurement problem.

Causal Confidence Rating (Adapted from Hill, 1965)

Rate each criterion 0 (not met / no data), 1 (partially met), 2 (clearly met). Hill's "experiment" criterion is omitted because it is what the FA supplies. His "analogy" is replaced by replication on a second sample, which descriptive data can supply: an analogous relation is in the literature for every common function, so it separates none of them. Bands: high, a total of 12 or more with temporality rated 2; moderate, 7 or more otherwise; low, 6 or less. Hill scored none of his viewpoints and held that none is required as a sine qua non; the scoring, the bands and the temporality requirement are clinical decision heuristics for this protocol, not validated cut scores.

Evidence Guide and References

Sources underlying each view. Empirical findings are kept separate from the applied conventions adopted in this form.

What the Literature Supports

  • Mill's methods are eliminative rules of inductive inference. Mill held that the possibility of more than one cause undermines a conclusion from agreement but not one from difference, judged the joint method a great advantage over agreement alone, and noted that the pair of instances the method of difference requires is seldom found without artificial experiment (Mill, 1843).
  • Descriptive analyses frequently disagree with FA results and over-implicate attention (Lerman & Iwata, 1993; Thompson & Iwata, 2007).
  • Comparing the probability of a consequence after behavior with its background probability can identify possible contingencies (Vollmer et al., 2001) but does not establish function: it gave outcomes similar to simple ABC tallies, and different from the FA, for all but 1 of 6 participants (Pence et al., 2009); reinforcers identified by FA followed behavior after a range of delays (Samaha et al., 2009); and contiguity, contingency and dependency must be kept distinct (McComas et al., 2009).
  • Contingency space analysis graphs P(C | B) against P(C | no B) to evaluate whether, and how strongly, a consequence is contingent on behavior; its authors knew of no decision rules for the degree of contingency (Martens et al., 2008).
  • Structured descriptive assessment, in which antecedents are arranged but consequences are left natural, made targeted environmental events occur more often than unstructured observation did for two children (Freeman et al., 2000) and suggested hypotheses similar to those of an analogue functional assessment for 3 of 4 children (Anderson & Long, 2002).
  • Precursor behaviors can share the function of severe behavior and can be assessed in its place (Smith & Churchill, 2002).
  • A scatter plot can make temporal patterns of responding identifiable, as illustrated in three cases (Touchette et al., 1985), but visual inspection of 15 scatter plots found no reliable temporal pattern in any of them, while control charts of the same data showed one or more periods in which the behavior was more likely in 12 (Kahng et al., 1998).
  • Biological setting events such as menstrual discomfort can be assessed in applied settings, with interview, record review, direct observation and functional analysis (Carr et al., 2003).
  • Naturally occurring response allocation can be described by the matching law with reinforcers identified by functional analysis (Borrero & Vollmer, 2002), but matching was also obtained with attention that did not function as a reinforcer (St. Peter et al., 2005).
  • Trial-based FA conducted in the classroom, with trials interspersed among classroom activities, corresponded with standard FA in 6 of 10 cases and partially in a 7th; graduate students, not teachers, served as therapists (Bloom et al., 2011).

Conventions Adopted in This Form (Not Empirically Derived)

  • Contingency-difference and probability-ratio classification bands, and the flags at P(C | ¬B) ≥ .30 and P(B | ¬A) ≥ .50, in Contingency.
  • Correlation magnitude and direction bands in Concomitant variation.
  • The 0–16 confidence score, its decision bands and the temporality requirement in Synthesis & decision.
  • The standing of hypotheses in Synthesis & decision (3 or more inconsistent findings: disconfirmed).
  • Requirement of ≥ 3 coded occurrences, all present, before a candidate is labeled as surviving agreement in Agreement.
  • The computed joint-method summary in Difference: Yes when every coded cell agrees and Mostly at two thirds or more; strongly implicated needs Yes on both sides and two or more implicating pairs, one implicating pair implicates by difference, and a No on either side with no implicating pair eliminates.
