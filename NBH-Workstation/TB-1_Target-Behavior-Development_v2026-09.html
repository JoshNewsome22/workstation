Guide: What Makes a Quality Target Behavior

Work the sheets in order: select and prioritize, test any proposed cluster, define each target, then run the quality check before baseline. On this sheet, Evidence boxes report what the cited studies found; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses. Full references are on the last sheet.

1. What Counts as a Target Behavior

EvidenceBaer, Wolf, and Risley (1968) described the "behavioral" dimension of applied work: it usually studies what subjects can be brought to do rather than what they can be brought to say, and because behavior is composed of physical events, its study requires precise measurement and raises the problem of reliable quantification. Skinner (1953) argued that explaining behavior by an inner cause, as when a man is said to eat because he is hungry or to fight because of the instinct of pugnacity, gives on analysis "merely redundant descriptions," not causes.
Applied guidanceApply the Dead Man's Test (Lindsley, 1991): if a dead man can do it, it is not behavior. "Sitting quietly," "not hitting," and "staying in seat" fail; write the active response instead. Labels such as "frustrated," "defiant," or "attention-seeking" name a hypothesized cause, not a response that can be counted. Do not embed inferred motivating conditions in the definition ("hits when frustrated"); motivating operations (establishing operations in Michael, 1993) are analyzed in the FBA, not written into the definition.

2. Topographical Versus Functional Definitions

EvidenceSkinner (1935) treated a response as a class, defined by the properties relevant to its relation with the environment rather than by a fixed muscular form (the many different acts that move a lever form one class, "pressing the lever"); he later called such a unit an operant (Skinner, 1938). Johnston and Pennypacker (2009) distinguish a topographical definition (the class is specified by its form: movements, force, body parts, physical outcome) from a functional definition (the class is specified by its effect on the environment). Iwata et al. (1982/1994) defined self-injury topographies by form plus physical outcome (for example, audible or forceful contact), which let observers record without inferring function.
Applied guidanceWrite school BIP definitions topographically: staff can see form; they cannot see function, and a function-based definition ("escape-maintained aggression") invites observers to score their hypothesis rather than the response. Add a physical-outcome criterion where form alone is ambiguous (contact forceful enough to be audible from 2 m, to displace the object, or to leave a mark). Record the hypothesized function in its own field. Use a functional definition only when the outcome is the target and form legitimately varies ("task completion": worksheet handed in with every item attempted).

3. Cluster (Umbrella) Titles Versus Specific Topographies

A cluster ("Aggression toward others") is the team's label for a set of topographies (hitting, kicking, biting) that will be recorded together and consequated the same way. A topography is one specific form. Sheet 3 tests whether a given set of topographies may share a cluster.

EvidenceDerby et al. (1994) found that aggregate functional analyses of "aberrant behavior" were undifferentiated for clients whose separated topographies showed distinct functions (self-injury versus stereotypy, for example). Beavers and Iwata (2011) reviewed 521 functional-analysis cases published in JABA: 88 (16.9%) showed multiple control, and 77 of those 88 (87.5%) came from datasets in which topographies had been collapsed, which, the authors suggested, means that when multiple control is observed it often may be a by-product of response aggregation during assessment. Melanson and Fahmie (2023) reported the same pattern across 40 years of studies: 306 of 473 multiple-control outcomes (64.7%) came from aggregated topographies. Response-class-hierarchy studies (Lalli et al., 1995; Richman et al., 1999; Harding et al., 2001) showed that members of one functional class can occur in a predictable order, milder members usually before more severe ones, and that withholding reinforcement for milder members can bring out more severe members. Precursor studies found that mild precursor topographies commonly share the function of the severe behavior: analyses of precursors allowed the maintaining variables of the problem behavior to be inferred for all 4 participants in Smith and Churchill (2002), separate analyses of precursor and severe behavior corresponded for 7 of 8 subjects in Fritz et al. (2013), and Fahmie and Iwata (2011) reviewed the topographies and functions of precursors reported for 34 subjects. In Fritz et al. (2013), a treatment aimed at the precursors also kept severe behavior at low rates (3 treatment analyses, 2 subjects).
Applied guidance: the clustering ruleTopographies may share one cluster only when all of the following hold. Sheet 3 checks each one.
  • Same urgency level. Every member requires the same staff response and the same crisis or safety procedure. A topography that causes injury (level 4) never shares a cluster with one that merely disrupts (level 2).
  • Same hypothesized function, or an explicit decision to record members separately until function is known.
  • Same measurement dimension or sampling method. Count/rate members are not mixed with duration or latency members (Johnston & Pennypacker, 2009), or with members recorded by interval or time sampling.
  • Same plan consequence. If staff would respond differently to two forms, they are two targets.
  • Comparable rates. A low-rate dangerous form must not be buried under a high-rate mild form in the graph. When rates differ widely (sheet 3 flags estimated rates five or more times apart, a working convention of this form), keep the cluster label for the plan but give each topography its own column on the data sheet.
A precursor or milder member of a response-class hierarchy is its own target, defined separately, even when it shares function with the severe form: the plan responds to it earlier and differently. A replacement behavior is always its own target.

4. Anatomy of a Quality Definition

EvidenceHawkins and Dobes (1977) set three criteria: objectivity (refers only to observable characteristics), clarity (readable and unambiguous, so that an experienced observer could read it and readily paraphrase it accurately), and completeness (boundary conditions specify what is and is not an instance). Kazdin (2011) adds that definitions should carry examples, non-examples, and decisions for borderline cases, and should be tested for interobserver agreement before baseline; agreement of at least 80% is the conventional minimum (Hartmann, 1977; Kazdin, 2011). Johnston and Pennypacker (2009) identify the measurable dimensions: repeatability (count, rate), temporal extent (duration), and temporal locus (latency, interresponse time).
Applied guidanceEvery target on sheet 4 carries: a label; a definition statement (form, body part, target, force or outcome threshold, distance or duration threshold where relevant); at least three examples including subtle forms; at least three non-examples that observers would otherwise mis-score; borderline decisions; onset and offset criteria for duration or latency measures; a counting unit or episode rule (a new episode after 10 s without the response, for instance); context limits; exclusions; measurement dimension; hypothesized function; and the paired replacement behavior.

5. Selecting and Prioritizing Targets

EvidenceWolf (1978) argued that goals, procedures, and effects must be judged socially valid by consumers. Van Houten et al. (1988) stated the right to effective behavioral treatment, including programs that teach functional skills, and noted that improved functioning may require reducing behaviors that are dangerous or that are barriers to further independence or social acceptability. Ayllon and Azrin (1968) proposed the relevance-of-behavior rule: target only behaviors that will contact reinforcement in the natural environment after treatment. Rosales-Ruiz and Baer (1997) described behavioral cusps, changes that open access to new contingencies. Goldiamond (1974) framed the constructional approach: build repertoires rather than only eliminate responses. Carr and Durand (1985) showed problem behavior decreased when a functionally equivalent communicative response was taught; Horner and Day (1991) found that a taught alternative less efficient than the problem behavior (in effort, schedule, or delay) did not compete with it until a more efficient alternative was taught.
Applied guidanceRate every candidate on sheet 2 with the urgency scale below, then select targets so that each urgency tier present is covered and each reduction target is paired with a replacement (the fair pair of White & Haring, 1980). For a plan with four or more targets, a typical set is one level-4 cluster, one level-3 cluster or single topography, one precursor, and one replacement behavior. Add targets rather than widening clusters.

6. Worked Example

Acceptable cluster: "Aggression toward others" (level 4)
Definition. Any forceful contact of the student's hand, foot, head, or teeth with another person, forceful enough to be audible, to move the person, or to leave a mark. Members: hitting (open or closed hand), kicking, biting (teeth close on skin or clothing). Each contact counts once; contacts within the same 10 s form one episode.
Examples. Slaps a peer's arm hard enough to be heard across the room; kicks a staff member's shin during a transition; closes teeth on a staff member's sleeve.
Non-examples. Light tap to get attention; brushing against a peer while walking; biting own hand (self-injury, separate target); kicking a chair (property, separate target).
Why it passes. All members are level 4, share the hypothesized function (escape from demands), are counted, and produce the same staff response.
Unacceptable cluster: "Disruption"
Proposed members: screaming (level 2, count), throwing chairs (level 3, count), leaving the classroom (level 3, duration and latency).
Why it fails. Mixed urgency (2 versus 3), mixed dimensions (count versus duration), and different staff responses. Split into three targets: Screaming (level 2); Throwing objects (level 3); Elopement (level 3, latency to exit and duration out of area).
Definition that fails the criteria
"Student becomes aggressive when frustrated." Fails objectivity (frustrated), clarity (aggressive is a label), and completeness (no form, force, or boundary). Rewrite as the cluster definition at left.
Precursor kept as its own target
Stands and pushes chair back from desk with both hands during a task (level 2; count). Recorded separately because the plan prompts the replacement mand at this point, before aggression occurs.

7. Higher-Level Restricted Repetitive Behavior, and Distance Thresholds for Forceful Topographies

EvidenceTurner (1999) divided restricted and repetitive behavior into lower-level forms, the repetition of movement (tics, motor stereotypy, repetitive object manipulation), and higher-level forms, more complex topographies such as rigid routines and lining items up in specific configurations. Fergus, Ahearn, Matthews, and Pandola (2026) assessed and treated arranging and ordering, a higher-level form, in four adolescents and young adults with autism at a residential school. They scored an RRB episode as any instance of touching or rearranging items in a disruptive manner (without permission, unrelated to retrieving or putting away materials, or not using the item as intended), with onset at a move, throw-away, open, close, or other fixing of an item that was repetitive or disruptive, a new episode after a 1-s pause or on contact with a new item, no scoring during pretend play, and a context rule under which a topographically appropriate act (putting trash in a bin) counted when it disrupted the activity. Each participant had an individual example list (lining markers, DVDs, or pillows; closing drawers; straightening chairs or a trash-can lid; capping pens; picking lint; throwing away others’ belongings). Frequency and duration were recorded from video in continuous 1-s bins and reported as episodes per minute; interobserver agreement was taken on at least 33% of sessions per measure, with scorers trained to 80%, and the obtained coefficients ranged from 0% to 100% (the zeros attributed to very low rates). Collateral measures were engagement and functional (manufacturer-intended) item engagement after Hagopian et al. (2020), emotional responses after Verriden and Roscoe (2019), and mands to end the session. Bann and Morris (2026) defined their forceful topographies with an explicit distance threshold: aggression as hitting with an open or closed fist from 15.24 cm or greater, self-injury as hitting a surface with the palm or the head with a fist or against a surface from 15.24 cm or greater, and property destruction as displacing an item from 15.24 cm or greater or ripping, tearing, or crumpling it; elopement was leaving the assigned instructional room, with sessions run across two adjacent rooms so that the behavior could be measured and consequated safely.
Applied guidanceSheet 4 carries a template for arranging and ordering that can be loaded into a target card and then edited: the source recommends a participant-specific list of examples and non-examples, so the template’s lists are a starting point, not the definition. Record on sheet 4 which family the behavior belongs to, the criterion by which it counts as disruptive (a reported high rate, such as multiple times a day, or the clinician’s judgment that it is excessive or interferes with instruction), and the safety flags that bear on whether interruption-based procedures can be used; the source excluded students whose behavior when interrupted required emergency physical management. Plan frequency and duration of episodes, 1-s continuous recording where video is available, agreement on at least a third of sessions, and scorer training to 80% before baseline. When a competing-stimulus treatment will be evaluated (Forms EA-1 and TD-1), define the collateral measures here so that engagement, functional item engagement, and emotional responses are scored from the start. Where the force of a hit, a strike, or a throw is otherwise ambiguous, the 15.24-cm (6-in.) distance threshold is an observable alternative to an audibility or mark criterion; write it into the borderline rules of the target that uses it.
Decision rules synthesize Hawkins and Dobes (1977), Kazdin (2011), Johnston and Pennypacker (2009), Derby et al. (1994), Beavers and Iwata (2011), and the precursor and response-class-hierarchy studies listed on the reference sheet. The urgency scale is a practice tool; its tiers are not taken from any single study.

A failed check means split the cluster. Operational Definitions

One card per target: cluster, single topography, precursor, or replacement. Write examples and non-examples one per line; the counter reaches criterion at three or more. Function is recorded in its own field and never written into the definition.

Definition Template: Higher-Level Restricted Repetitive Behavior (Arranging and Ordering)

Higher-level RRB (Turner, 1999) is the complex form: rigidity in routines, insistence on sameness, lining or arranging items into particular configurations. Lower-level RRB is the repetition of movement: tics, motor stereotypy, repetitive object manipulation. The template below is the episode definition Fergus et al. (2026) measured. Load it into a target card, then replace the example and non-example lists with the student’s own (the source recommends both), and record the fields beneath it for any RRB target.
