Social Validity

Whether a plan’s goals are the right ones, its procedures acceptable, and its effects worth having is a judgement “that only society was qualified to make” (Wolf, 1978). This form asks the people the plan is for — the student, the caregiver, and the staff who run it — before the plan starts and at every review.

Section G: The Social Significance of the Goals

“The social significance of the goals. Are the specific behavioral goals really what society wants?” (Wolf, 1978, p. 207)

Administer pre at plan sign-off, with the Procedures pre round, and again post. G2 to G4 ask about the replacement skill, the amount of change and the settings, which only the plan sets: asked at target selection, they would be answered about a plan that does not exist yet. The open question, G1 and G5 can be asked earlier, at target selection, to inform Form TB-1’s choice of targets; enter those answers in the pre round. Ask the open question first and write the answer down before any rating item: Willner et al. G5 is the guard for the discrepancy Wolf describes on p. 213: a parent says “improved” while the measured rate is flat “because the child has stopped cursing, which was important to the parent, but not measured by us.” A low G5 now, with high satisfaction later, says the consumer is responding to something the plan does not measure.

Section P: The Social Appropriateness of the Procedures

“The social appropriateness of the procedures. Do the ends justify the means? That is, do the participants, caregivers and other consumers consider the treatment procedures acceptable?” (Wolf, 1978, p. 207)

Appropriateness “in terms of ethics, cost, and practicality” (p. 210): P1 and P2 ask about ethics, P3 about cost, P4 about practicality, P5 about acceptability against the alternative, P8 the administrator about cost to the building. Administer pre at plan sign-off, alongside IC-1 consent and round 1 of the contextual fit form (Form CF-1), where implementers answer both forms; the student and the caregiver answer SV-1 only), and post at every review and after any modification of the plan.

Acceptability is not only an ethical check. Ratings of fairness and concern “correlate very highly with the number of offenses” in Wolf’s program (p. 210), and a disliked procedure is one people “avoid, or run away [from], or complain loudly” about (p. 206). Low ratings are a warning to act on. Ratings and later integrity do not always agree, so check one against the other (Form TI-1, the Form CT-1 follow-up, and What People Did on the Summary sheet): a caregiver may report a plan acceptable and still not run it (Auten et al., 2024). P2 mirrors item 10 of the contextual fit form, so an implementer’s two answers can be compared on the Summary sheet; that form’s other “Values and acceptability” items are not repeated here.

Section E: The Social Importance of the Effects

“The social importance of the effects. Are consumers satisfied with the results? All the results, including any unpredicted ones?” (Wolf, 1978, p. 207)

Post only, at each PR-1 review and at exit. The open question comes first and is mandatory: an outcome that was not a goal is still a result. “Whether or not the program is helpful can be evaluated only by the consumer. Behavior analysts may give their opinions, and these opinions may even be supported with empirical objective behavioral data, but it is the participants and other consumers who want to make the final decision about whether a program helped solve their problems” (p. 210). E5 is reverse-scored in the section mean. E7 is the standard satisfaction question; it is kept because it is standard, and the Summary never treats it as evidence of effect.

What Social Validity Is

Wolf (1978, p. 207) set out the three levels on which society would need to validate applied behavior-analytic work:

“The suggestion seemed to be that society would need to validate our work on at least three levels:
1. The social significance of the goals. Are the specific behavioral goals really what society wants?
2. The social appropriateness of the procedures. Do the ends justify the means? That is, do the participants, caregivers and other consumers consider the treatment procedures acceptable?
3. The social importance of the effects. Are consumers satisfied with the results? All the results, including any unpredicted ones?
We have come to refer to these as judgements of social validity.” (Wolf, 1978, p. 207)

The judges are the consumers: “the participants, caregivers and other consumers,” “the specific consumer or representatives of the relevant community.” Experts are not the judges. Wolf recalls that his team thought “we were psychologists and thus the most qualified judges of what was best for people. Somehow, they didn’t seem convinced by that logic” (p. 206), and the first replication community “fired” the program before the team knew of any complaint; after a consumer feedback system was built, “the Achievement Place program has not been summarily ‘fired’ from a community” (p. 211). Consumer feedback was designed as ongoing quality control, collected “throughout the year of training and certification, and each year thereafter” (p. 211), not once.

The boundary with the contextual fit form. Contextual fit (Form CF-1) asks the implementers whether they can and will run the plan: knowledge, skills, values, resources, routine, administrative support, expected effectiveness. SV-1 asks the consumers, including the student and the caregiver who never run the plan, whether the plan is aimed at the right thing, done in an acceptable way, and worked for them. The overlap is that form’s “Values and acceptability” items; SV-1 item P2 mirrors its item 10 and the Summary sets an implementer’s contextual fit values mean beside their SV-1 procedures mean rather than repeating the items.

Published acceptability scales. This form’s items are its own, written from Wolf (1978); they have no published norms. Where a district or an IEP team wants a published instrument with its own scoring, school versions exist: the Intervention Rating Profile-15 for teachers (IRP-15; Martens, Witt, Elliott, & Darveaux, 1985), the Treatment Acceptability Rating Form–Revised for parents (TARF-R; Reimers & Wacker, 1988) and the Children’s Intervention Rating Profile for students (CIRP; Witt & Elliott, 1985). Their wording is the authors’: use them from the published source, with permission where it is needed, beside this form rather than in its place, and report each on its own scale.

How the Figures Are Calculated

Item meanThe mean rating across respondents who answered that item. Unanswered and N/A are excluded, not scored as neutral. Items that do not apply to a role (student items for adults; adult items for the student; P8 for anyone but the administrator) are not cells at all.
Section meanThe mean of every answered rating in the section for the round, across items and respondents, so a respondent who answered more items counts for more. E5 (“something has got worse”) is reverse-scored (7 minus the rating) in the section mean and reported raw everywhere else.
Role meansRoles are grouped as student, caregiver, implementer (teacher, paraprofessional), administrator, and other. A role is present when it has at least one rating.
ChangePre to post change per item, computed only for respondents who rated the item in both rounds. When the set of respondents differs between rounds, the row is marked not like for like.
Flagged itemAny item on which at least one respondent rated at or below the threshold (a whole number from 1 to 5, set in the toolbar, default 3), whatever the item mean. E5 is tested on its reversed value, so a high E5 is what gets flagged.
DisagreementItems on which the role means for the latest round are 2 or more points apart.
ConcordanceObjective improvement at or above 25% with an E1 mean at or above 4.5: consumers and the data agree the behavior improved. Improvement at or above 25% with E1 at or below 3: data show change the consumers do not see; check G1 and G5 and whether the change is visible in their setting. Change below 10% with E1 at or above 4.5: consumers report improvement the data do not show, and the decision follows the data. Anything else is reported as neither clearly concordant nor discordant. E2 against PR-1’s “used independently” follows the same pattern. These cut-offs are working conventions; the sources fix no threshold.
Administration conditionsThe number of the four conditions met in a round is printed with every mean. Fewer than three met and the Summary carries the caption that the ratings were collected without the conditions Wolf (1978) names as needed for consumers to be the best evaluators, and are read as reports, not as validation.
Verdict labelsSocially valid: Goals pre-mean at or above 4.5 with no role at or below 3; Procedures post-mean at or above 4.5 with no role at or below 3; Effects post-mean at or above 4.5 with concordance “agree” on this or an earlier review; and at least two post administrations; and no line of What People Did shows the plan being dropped. The procedures are read from ratings given with experience: the post round, else the first-experience round, else the pre ratings of people who had run or seen the procedures. Ratings and practice disagree: the ratings would carry the plan, but a line of What People Did shows it being dropped. Accepted, effect unconfirmed by consumers: Goals and Procedures met, Effects below 4.5 or discordant. Acceptability concern: any role’s Procedures mean at or below 3. Goals contested: any role’s Goals mean at or below 3, or the G5 mean at or below 3. Insufficient: fewer than three roles, no post round, or everything met on a single post administration. Below the working thresholds: none of the above applies and a section mean falls short. The labels are working conventions of this form, not findings in the source; Wolf (1978) gives no cut-off.

Cautions From the Source

Satisfaction without effect“Subjective data are risky data. Subjective data may not have any relationship to actual events. A program that is described by its consumers as well-liked or effective may not necessarily be either pleasant or effective” (Wolf, 1978, p. 212). Berleman, Seaberg and Steinburn (1972, as cited in Wolf, 1978): a one- to two-year treatment, evaluated during treatment and eight months after, showed “no positive impact” on disruptive behavior in school, police contacts, or rate of institutionalization, with the control group doing as well or better; yet the youths believed their school acting-out had decreased, and “89 percent of the parents responded positively, as did 94 percent of the boys” when asked if they would participate again. Patterson, and Conrad and Wincze (as cited in Wolf, 1978), report the same pattern: favorable client reports not substantiated by observers.
Distortion by the situation“The contingencies of the situation create distortion”: verbal behavior “is a manipulable behavior”; “some of those being evaluated might politic their consumers for better ratings”; raters “might fear that they will not remain anonymous and be afraid that those they are rating might retaliate” (p. 212). The remedy is to establish the conditions under which people can be assumed to be the best evaluators, which “will involve education about options, lack of coercion, anonymity, and so on.” Those are the four boxes on the Setup sheet.
Responding to something unmeasured“The consumer is responding to changes in some behavior or condition that we are not recording”: the parent says improved while the tantrum rate is flat “because the child has stopped cursing, which was important to the parent, but not measured by us” (p. 213). The remedy is better measurement, which is why the open item and G5 exist.
Not a reason to dismiss the judgeThe third explanation, that “subjective measurement is impossible because humans cannot judge and report their own situation accurately enough,” Wolf rejects as “unacceptable if our goal is to design a responsive consumer-oriented applied social science”; the alternative is to “develop better ways of teaching people to observe their behavior and their conditions” (p. 213).
What the ratings are“Our use of subjective measures does not relate to internal causal variables. Instead, it is an attempt to assess the dimensions of complex reinforcers in socially acceptable and practical ways” (p. 213).

What This Form Must Not Be Used to Claim

  1. A satisfaction rating is not evidence that the plan changed behavior. Berleman et al. (as cited in Wolf, 1978, p. 212): no measured effect, yet “89 percent of the parents … 94 percent of the boys” would do it again. SV-1 Effects never alters the PR-1 suggestion or the FS-1 tier.
  2. A procedure rated acceptable is not thereby ethical, safe, or effective. Acceptability is one of the three things Wolf asks, and relates to adoption and (possibly) effectiveness, not to proof of either.
  3. Ratings collected by the person being rated, or without anonymity, education about options and freedom to decline, may be “politic[ked]” or distorted by fear of retaliation (p. 212). Report the administration conditions with every mean, and do not describe such ratings as validation.
  4. Do not treat the behavior analyst’s or the team’s own rating as consumer validation: “Behavior analysts may give their opinions … but it is the participants and other consumers who want to make the final decision” (p. 210). Staff rows are implementers’ acceptability, not social validity of effects for the student.
  5. Do not infer internal causes from ratings; the form assesses “the dimensions of complex reinforcers in socially acceptable and practical ways,” “not … internal causal variables” (p. 213).
  6. Do not drop an unpredicted outcome because it was not a goal: effects validation covers “all the results, including any unpredicted ones” (p. 207). The open item is mandatory.
  7. Do not conclude from a low student rating that the student “cannot judge”; Wolf rejects that explanation as “unacceptable” (p. 213) and asks instead for better ways of teaching people to observe and report.
  8. Do not call a plan socially valid on one post administration; consumer feedback was designed as ongoing quality control (p. 211).

About the simulation. Load simulation fills every sheet with a simulated administration that describes no real student, family or staff: a second post round on an escape-maintained desk-tipping plan, rated by the teacher, the paraeducator, the mother and the student, with the pre round from target selection and plan sign-off.
