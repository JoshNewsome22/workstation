A review answers four questions in order, and the order matters: did the plan run, what did the behavior do, is the replacement established, and what does that imply. Reading the behavior data before the integrity data is how a plan that was never implemented gets recorded as ineffective.

1. Was the Plan Actually Run?

Ask this first. Behavior data collected under an unimplemented plan do not tell you whether the plan works. If integrity is unknown or poor, the decision below is about implementation, not about the plan.

Did fidelity drop abruptly in the period? When, and what changed

Performance in the period right after a drop in fidelity is likely to be worse than the new fidelity level alone would predict; conversely, a period at full fidelity immediately before may mask low current fidelity. In Burlison et al. (2026) accuracy fell 35 points when fidelity went from 100% to 33% between blocks and rose 42 points on the way back, and a full-fidelity block scored 9 points lower after a 33% block than after a 67% one; the authors call these sequence effects preliminary. Fidelity also varies between implementers and within one over time, and a global figure can hide the one error that matters (Cook et al., 2015, as cited by Burlison et al., 2026), which is why the figures are recorded by implementer and observation.

Why the Order of the Questions Matters

The four questions on this form are asked in a fixed order because reversing them produces a predictable error. If the behavior data are read first and look poor, the plan gets modified — and if the plan was never run as written, the modification is a change to something that was not happening. The integrity figure has to come first for the behavior data to mean anything, which is the argument Gresham (1989) made for treating integrity as a condition of interpreting outcome at all.

The same applies to the replacement. A target behavior that has fallen while the replacement never became independent is not a finished plan; it is a plan holding a behavior down by arrangement. Fading it risks returning the behavior, and a return at the fading step would be read as failure rather than as a consequence of exiting before the replacement was established.

How the Suggestion and the Checks Are Made

The suggested decision on the Decision sheet, the check under it and the Exit Readiness verdict apply the rules below. They are this form's working convention for organizing a review, not findings.

IntegrityBelow 90%, or critical-step integrity below 90%: the plan is not running as written, and the suggestion is to continue and address implementation. 90% or above on both: the plan is running as written. This is the integrity criterion Forms TI-1 and TD-1 use, so one figure means the same thing on every form. 90% is a convention, not a finding. It is set high because St. Peter Pipkin, Vollmer and Sloman (2010) found, in a human operant model, that errors reinforcing the problem behavior were more detrimental than failing to reinforce the alternative alone; a few such errors can sit inside an overall figure that looks acceptable, which is why critical steps are checked separately against the same percentage. A figure outside 0 to 100% is not used.
Plan-run gateThe review may conclude that the plan is not working (modify, or return to assessment) only when the period passes the gate: a TI-1 observation was completed in the period, the most recent observation is at or above the 90% criterion on both figures, mean integrity across the period’s observations is 80% or above, no consequence step ran below 80% of its opportunities, and reinforcers were delivered after the target behavior or a wrong response on 10% or fewer of consequence-step opportunities. Otherwise the review records that the plan was not run as written, the period’s outcome data are not a test of the plan, and the route is retraining (Form ST-1), a new observation (Form TI-1) and a new review before any plan change. The 80% and 10% figures are this form’s conventions, informed by the parametric studies but not set by them; a period that passes the gate is not thereby proven interpretable, since in earlier DRO work commission errors were harmful for some participants even at 80% (Hronek & Kestner, 2025, as summarized by O’Neill et al., 2026). A figure left blank is not tested.
Fidelity beside outcomeThe table under the graph places each period’s integrity and consequence-step commission figure next to its behavior figures, marks a period below 80% or above 10% commission, and marks a fall of 15 points or more from the period before. The figures by implementer and observation are recorded as text, because a mean across implementers can hide one who is not running the plan.

Reading Fidelity Before Outcome

Before judging the plan, check how it was delivered. If TI-1 shows overall fidelity below 80%, any critical consequence step below 80%, or reinforcers delivered after the target behavior, treat the period’s outcome data as a test of the implementation, not of the plan: in parametric studies a DRO that worked for all participants at full fidelity worked in about a third of sessions at 40% fidelity and almost never at 20% (O’Neill et al., 2026), and a teaching procedure mastered by every learner at full fidelity was mastered by one of six when consequences averaged about 67% fidelity, with all six mastering it once errors were corrected (Burlison et al., 2026). Retrain (ST-1), re-observe (TI-1), then re-review.

The figures behind that rule: in O’Neill et al. (2026), 11 adult volunteers in an analog DRO task, the treatment reduced responding in every participant at 100% fidelity, in 35% of sessions at 40% and in 9% at 20%; even at 60%, 3 of 11 lost the effect. In Burlison et al. (2026), 6 adult volunteers in a computerized matching task, 6 of 6 mastered the set taught at 100% fidelity, 1 of 6 the set taught at about 67% on average, and 0 of 6 the set taught with every consequence wrong; all 6 then mastered the previously error-laden set within 3 to 9 blocks once the errors stopped. Both studies used computer-delivered errors with adult volunteers, not staff with clients, and neither sets a clinical threshold: the gate on this form is a working convention. Recovery after retraining is the direction of the evidence, not a guarantee, and it was neither immediate nor free of prior full-fidelity exposure in that study. Reinforcing the behavior that should be on extinction, or a wrong response, was the more damaging error class (Burlison et al., 2026; St. Peter Pipkin et al., 2010), and omission errors were less damaging but not harmless.

A file saved by any other form is refused, and nothing on this form changes. TI-1 is another file and cannot be called, so its rules are applied here as TI-1 applies them: the deciding observations are the unannounced observations with scored steps, as many as its criterion counts, and the most recent of them gives the integrity, the critical-step integrity, the date and the type above; every observation with scored steps counts toward the period figures, with its integrity the correct steps over the scored steps; the consequence-step figures use TI-1’s opportunity counts where it holds them and otherwise each scored code as one opportunity. The figures by implementer and observation list TI-1’s interventionist with each observation; the largest fall between consecutive observations is written beside the sequence question, which is left for the reviewer to answer. No entry is overwritten without asking. Where the figures came from and the date of the import are recorded under the table and saved with the form.

Importing From Form DD-1

Import From DD-1, above the table under What Did the Behavior Do?, fills the table from Form DD-1’s daily data rather than having it retyped. Inside the workstation it reads the DD-1 open there; anywhere, it opens a data file DD-1 saved with Save data, or a case file saved by the workstation (a .json file, or the one-file edition’s .case.html). A file saved by any other form is refused, and nothing on this form changes.

Choose DD-1’s reduction target and, to fill the Replacement column too, a second behavior. The periods are either DD-1’s conditions, the first on the BL row and each later condition a review period, or date ranges you set, the first again on the BL row. In each period every day on DD-1’s sheet with a date and a value counts, with the value DD-1 graphs for that day: the count, the rate per hour from the count and the minutes observed, the minutes, the percentage or the prompt level. The level written into the table is the mean of those days. Beside the table the form shows each period’s days with data, level, standard deviation and range, and its trend: the least-squares line through the period’s days, DD-1’s default method, with its slope per day on DD-1’s sheet, called flat (zero-celerating) when the slope is under 1% of the period’s mean per day, or under 0.01, as DD-1 does. These are DD-1’s own computations; its Sources for the Indices gives their sources.

Each column’s unit is recorded in the Unit row and titles the graph’s axis; when the two columns are in different units, the replacement is drawn against an axis of its own on the right. A column holds one unit, so figures already in it in another unit, or with no unit recorded, can only be replaced as a whole. No entry on this form is overwritten without asking: the import lists every entry that differs from DD-1’s figures, and you keep them or replace them. Where the figures came from (the workstation or the file’s name, DD-1’s behavior and the date of the import) is recorded under the table and saved with the form. Integrity is not recorded on DD-1; enter it from Form TI-1. The suggested decision still reads only the change in level from the BL row to the most recent period, so read the trend and variability beside it.

Exit Criteria

Writing the exit conditions at the review, rather than at the end, changes what the team is aiming at. The five criteria on the fading sheet are separable on purpose: behavior, replacement, schedule, integrity, and generalization can each be met independently, and a plan that meets four of the five is not ready. The most commonly missed is integrity under conditions that will actually obtain after exit — unannounced, with staff who have not been coached that week.

The transfer question is the one that decides whether exit is possible at all. If nothing outside the plan will maintain the replacement, the plan cannot stop, and recording that is more useful than fading and waiting to see. Stokes and Baer (1977) described this as introducing the behavior to natural maintaining contingencies, including teaching the person to recruit a natural community of reinforcement, rather than training and hoping for generalization.
