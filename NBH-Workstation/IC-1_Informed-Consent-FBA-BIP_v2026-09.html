Guide: When Consent Is Required and What Makes It Informed

An FBA used to evaluate one student, as part of an initial evaluation or a reevaluation, is an evaluation under IDEA, and parental consent is required before it is conducted; a BIP built from it can change the behavioral component of the IEP. Consent is therefore a procedural-safeguards event, not a courtesy. This sheet states what the regulations and the BACB Ethics Code require, and how this form fits with the district's own consent process. Evidence boxes report what the cited sources say; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses. Nothing here is legal advice; the district's ESE procedures and legal counsel govern in any conflict.

1. When an FBA Requires Parental Consent

EvidenceParental consent must be obtained before an initial evaluation (34 CFR §300.300(a)) and before any reevaluation (§300.300(c)). OSEP's Letter to Christiansen (2007) states that an FBA used to evaluate an individual child in accordance with §§300.304–300.311, to assist in determining whether the child is a child with a disability and the nature and extent of the special education and related services the child needs, is an evaluation under §300.15; that parental consent is required for an FBA conducted as an individual evaluation or reevaluation; and that a parent who disagrees with an FBA conducted for individual evaluative purposes to develop or modify a BIP for a particular child may request an independent educational evaluation at public expense (§300.502). An FBA used school-wide, in the context of positive behavior supports, generally would not be an evaluation requiring parental consent, unless consent is required from the parents of all children in the school. Letter to Sarzynski (2008), citing Christiansen, states that an FBA the public agency believes necessary to determine whether the positive behavioral interventions and supports in a child's current IEP would be effective in enabling the child to make progress toward the IEP goals, or whether the behavioral component of the IEP would need to be revised, would be a reevaluation for which parental consent is required under §300.300(c). Letter to Gallo (2013) restates that consent is required for an FBA conducted as part of an initial evaluation or reevaluation, including an FBA following a manifestation determination (§300.530(f)). The Department's 2024 guidance, Using Functional Behavioral Assessments to Create Supportive Learning Environments, states that consent would be required if, for a particular child, an FBA is one of the assessment tools and strategies conducted as part of an initial evaluation or reevaluation, or the FBA, along with a review of additional data, is used as an initial evaluation or reevaluation; that when an FBA is used and the LEA is not evaluating or reevaluating the child, the IDEA does not require parental consent; and that when the conduct that led to a disciplinary change of placement is determined to be a manifestation of the student's disability, parental consent is required prior to conducting the FBA. Florida Rule 6A-6.0331 requires the district to seek parental consent for an evaluation whenever a disability is suspected and to obtain informed parental consent before any reevaluation.
Applied guidanceTreat every individual FBA at Royal Palm School as an evaluation or reevaluation. Before any assessment activity begins (interviews, ABC recording, scatter plot, preference assessment, experimental analysis), the file must show one of three things: a signature on this form; a district Consent for Initial Evaluation or Consent for Reevaluation on which the FBA is a listed procedure; or, for a reevaluation only, the district’s decision to proceed after documented reasonable efforts and no response (34 CFR §300.300(c)(2); see section 4). The "How consent was obtained" selector on the Consent sheet records which one applies and where the district form is filed. Existing data collection that is part of the current BIP (daily target-behavior data, integrity checks) continues under the IEP and does not itself require new consent; a new assessment question does.

2. What "Informed" Means

EvidenceUnder 34 CFR §300.9 (mirrored in Florida Rule 6A-6.03411(1)(g)), consent means the parent has been fully informed of all information relevant to the activity, in the parent's native language or other mode of communication; understands and agrees in writing to the activity, with the consent describing the activity and listing any records to be released and to whom; and understands that consent is voluntary and may be revoked at any time, and that revocation is not retroactive. Prior written notice under §300.503 must be given a reasonable time before the agency proposes or refuses to initiate or change the identification, evaluation, or educational placement of the child or the provision of FAPE, and must describe the action proposed or refused, explain why, describe each evaluation procedure, assessment, record, or report used as a basis, state that the parents have protection under the procedural safeguards and (if the notice is not an initial referral for evaluation) how a copy of them can be obtained, name sources the parents can contact for help in understanding the regulations, and describe the other options considered, why they were rejected, and any other relevant factors; it must be written in language understandable to the general public and provided in the parent's native language or other mode of communication, unless it is clearly not feasible to do so. The BACB Ethics Code (2020) Standard 2.11 makes behavior analysts responsible for knowing and complying with all conditions under which informed consent is required (for example, before initial implementation of assessments or behavior-change interventions, when making substantial changes to interventions, and when exchanging or releasing confidential information or records), for explaining, obtaining, reobtaining, and documenting it, and for obtaining assent from clients when applicable; Standard 2.08 requires understandable language in, and ensured comprehension of, all communications, and an explanation of all assessment and behavior-change intervention procedures before they are implemented; Standard 2.09 requires appropriate efforts to involve clients and relevant stakeholders throughout the service relationship, including selecting goals, selecting and designing assessments and behavior-change interventions, and progress monitoring; Standard 2.13 requires assessments that best meet the diverse needs, context, and resources of the client and stakeholders, selected, designed, and implemented with a focus on maximizing benefits and minimizing risk of harm; Standard 2.15 requires minimizing risk of harm in behavior-change interventions. The Code's glossary describes giving an individual the opportunity to give informed consent for services or research as communicating about, and taking appropriate steps to confirm understanding of, the purpose; the expected time commitment and procedures; the right to decline to participate or withdraw at any time without adverse consequences; potential benefits, risks, discomfort, or adverse effects; any limits to confidentiality or privacy; any incentives for research participation; whom to contact for questions or concerns at any time; and the opportunity to ask questions and receive answers.
Applied guidanceThe Consent sheet is written to the parent, in second person, and is meant to be read with them, not handed over. Every proposed procedure has a checkbox and a short plain-language description; unchecked procedures are not consented to. Higher-intensity or optional elements (experimental analysis, edible items, recording, sharing with outside providers) get their own decision and initials on the Specific procedures sheet so that a parent can agree to the assessment without agreeing to all of it. Record the language of the conversation and any interpreter. Give the procedural-safeguards notice and record its date.

3. Assent from the Student

EvidenceStandard 2.11 directs behavior analysts to obtain assent when applicable. Morris, Detrick, and Peterson (2021) reviewed assent procedures in behavior-analytic research with participants who have autism or developmental disabilities and recommended defining assent and dissent behaviors in advance, honoring dissent, and documenting how assent was sought. Rajaraman et al. (2022) describe trauma-informed practice as including opportunities for the client to refuse and to have refusal honored. Under Florida Rule 6A-6.03311(8), when a student with a disability reaches 18, the rights afforded to parents transfer to the student and the right to notice is shared by the parent and the student, except for a student who has been determined incompetent under State law or who has had a guardian advocate appointed to make educational decisions under s. 393.12, F.S.; the parent may continue to take part in educational decisions through, among other means, a power of attorney or a supported decision-making agreement. An adult student to whom the rights have transferred gives consent in their own right.
Applied guidanceAssent is sought from every student in the mode they use, and the Student assent sheet records how the assessment was explained, what the student indicated, and which behaviors the team will treat as dissent during any session. Dissent pauses the activity; it does not cancel parental consent, but repeated dissent is reported to the parent and can change the procedures selected. For a student who is 18 or older, has not been determined incompetent, and has no guardian advocate for educational decisions, the student signs the Consent sheet as the consenting party, or an agent acting under the student’s power of attorney signs; the Student assent sheet records which.

4. Refusal, No Response, and Revocation

EvidenceUnder §300.300(c)(2), if the parent does not respond to a request for consent to a reevaluation, the agency may proceed without consent if it can demonstrate reasonable efforts to obtain consent and the parent has failed to respond; the agency must document its attempts using the procedures in §300.322(d) (§300.300(d)(5)): detailed records of telephone calls made or attempted and their results, copies of correspondence sent to the parents and any responses received, and detailed records of visits made to the parent's home or place of employment and their results. Refusal of consent for reevaluation may be pursued through mediation or due process, or the agency may decline to pursue it (§300.300(c)(1)(ii); Florida Rule 6A-6.0331). A public agency may not use a parent's refusal to consent to one service or activity to deny the parent or child any other service, benefit, or activity of the public agency, except as required by Part 300 (§300.300(d)(3)). Revocation of consent stops the activity going forward but does not undo what was done while consent was in effect (§300.9(c)).
Applied guidanceEvery consent event goes on the Consent record sheet: the request, the notice, each contact attempt with method and outcome, the decision, any exception, and any revocation, each with a date and the staff member's name. A decision to proceed without consent after no response is the ESE administrator's and the district's, not the BCBA's; this form documents the attempts and the date the decision was made. Assessment stops the day a revocation is received.

5. Using This Form with the District Process

  • Signed here. Use when the FBA request arises between scheduled evaluations, when the parent is present at a meeting, or when the district form has been signed but the team wants the procedure-level detail and assent on file with the FBA. Complete all seven parts of the Consent sheet.
  • Obtained through the reevaluation process. Select this on the Consent sheet, enter the district form's name, the date it was signed, where it is filed, and confirm that the FBA is a listed procedure on it. The signature block becomes optional; the procedure checklist and the Specific procedures sheet are still completed so the file shows what the parent was told.
  • Obtained through the initial-evaluation process. Same as above for a student not yet eligible.
  • Declined or revoked. Record the decision, date, and who received it; stop any assessment that is not part of the current IEP; notify the ESE contact.
  • The plan itself. Consent on this form covers the assessment and the writing of the BIP (part 2, “Behavior intervention plan development”); it is not consent to carry the plan out. Adopting or revising the BIP is an IEP team decision made with the parent, with prior written notice when it changes the provision of FAPE (34 CFR §300.503(a)), and BACB Standard 2.11 lists informed consent before initial implementation of behavior-change interventions and when making substantial changes to them. Record the parent’s consent to implement the plan as its own event on the Consent record sheet.

Informed Consent for Functional Behavior Assessment

1. Why We Are Asking

We are asking for your permission to conduct a functional behavior assessment (FBA) for your child. An FBA is a way of finding out what a behavior does for a student: what tends to happen before it, what tends to happen after it, and in what situations it is more and less likely. The results are used to write or revise a behavior intervention plan (BIP) that teaches a better way to get the same result and changes the situations that set the behavior off. Federal and Florida special-education rules require your informed written consent before the school evaluates or reevaluates your child. An FBA done for one student as part of an evaluation or reevaluation is covered by that rule, so we need your consent before we begin.

4. Records, Confidentiality, Risks, Benefits, and Your Rights

  • Records. Everything collected becomes part of your child's education record and is protected under FERPA and Florida law. It is kept in the student's behavior file and seen by the IEP team and the staff who work with your child. You may ask to review it at any time. The school must let you review it without unnecessary delay, before any IEP meeting, and no more than 45 days after you ask. You may also ask for copies.
  • Risks. Observations, interviews, data collection, analysis of the data, and preference assessments are ordinary school activities. They carry no more risk than an ordinary school day, and food items are used only with your separate approval. During an experimental analysis, and during practice with waiting for a reward, the behavior may briefly occur. Sessions are short, run by trained staff, and stopped by fixed safety rules. The rules for the experimental analysis, and your decision on it, are on the Specific procedures sheet; the stop rule for practice with waiting is written in that plan.
  • Benefits. A plan based on the assessment is more likely to reduce the behavior and teach a replacement than a plan based on a guess. There is no cost to you.
  • Your choice. Consent is voluntary. You may refuse. You may also withdraw consent at any time by telling the BCBA or the ESE contact named above; we will ask you to confirm it in writing for our records. Withdrawing consent stops the assessment from that point on; it does not undo what was already done. Refusing this assessment does not affect any other service your child receives.
  • Your rights. You have received, or will receive with this form, the notice of procedural safeguards. If you disagree with the results you may request an independent educational evaluation at public expense. If you need this form in another language or explained through an interpreter, we will provide one. Questions can be directed to the BCBA or ESE contact named above.

A. Experimental (functional) analysisEA-1

In a functional analysis we run short sessions, usually 5 to 10 minutes each, and each session is set up as one situation: for example, attention is given only after the behavior, or a task is removed only after the behavior, or your child is left with nothing to do. This tells us directly what the behavior produces. The behavior may occur during these sessions; that is expected and is what the test measures. One study looked at the records of 99 people treated in a hospital unit for self-injury. Injury rates were relatively low during functional analysis sessions and at other times, and injuries were usually not severe. The authors suggest that a functional analysis of self-injury is relatively safe when appropriate precautions are taken (Kahng et al., 2015). The safeguards below are the precautions we take.

  • Safeguards. Sessions are run by staff trained in the procedure; the school nurse is informed before the first session; a session is stopped immediately if any injury occurs or if the behavior reaches the stop rule written here; protective materials are used where needed; no session is run when your child is ill or when medication has just changed.
