The Intervals

A cue at a predictable time sets up the fixed-interval pattern Ferster and Skinner (1957) describe: little behavior early in the interval and a burst just before the check. A variable cue (an average of 3 minutes, from 1 to 6, as the teachers’ timer was set in Bulla & Frieder, 2017) keeps the behavior steady across the interval. Start fixed while the student learns to rate; go variable once agreement holds.

Earns and Tiers

The in-a-row earn is a small reinforcer inside the day, so the sheet pays before the end of the day; the tiers make a bigger day worth more without making a bad start pointless. The per-expectation goal prints under each column from the Targets sheet.

The Count and the Graph

The goal is a number the student met on some baseline sessions and the graph top is a little above the best session; the student plots each session’s count, which is the self-graphing that added to the reactive effect in DiGangi, Maag and Rutherford (1991) and that Farrell and McDougall (2008) built fluency with. Points possible are the sessions; the goal percent is the share of sessions at or above the count.

Check-In, Check-Out

The Behavior Education Program card (Crone, Hawken & Horner, 2010): teacher feedback after each period on a 0-1-2 scale, adult contact at both ends of the day, and a home copy. Hawken and colleagues’ reviews report consistent reductions in problem behavior, most reliably when the behavior is maintained by adult attention (Hawken et al., 2014; Maggin et al., 2015); Campbell and Anderson (2011) showed in a component analysis that the periodic teacher feedback carried the effect for the students studied. Points possible are periods × expectations × 2.

The Interlocking Schedule

From the school’s session sheet: a stopwatch starts when the student begins; at the end of each minute the student and the staff member each circle Yes or No for engagement; the number of items required to earn the reinforcer changes with the minutes elapsed; the session ends, with praise and the reinforcer, when the student has completed the items required at that minute and both engagement checks were Yes.

What This Form Builds

A self-monitoring system is a self-management package: the student observes and records their own behavior at set moments, an adult checks the record, and the record earns something. Reviews of classroom self-management interventions report consistent gains in on-task and appropriate behavior and reductions in disruption across grade levels and settings (Briesch & Chafouleas, 2009; Bruhn, McDaniel & Kreigh, 2015), and the daily behavior report card, the teacher-rated cousin of these sheets, has a meta-analysis behind it (Vannest, Davis, Davis, Mason & Burke, 2010). The two mechanisms the research keeps finding are the recording itself, which is reactive, and the consequences attached to the record. Both are arranged on this form. DeLeon, Bullock and Catania (2013) add a caution and a reason the arrangement works: human performances under schedules often look different from the laboratory patterns because people describe their own behavior, and in verbally able children “it is often easier to shape verbal behavior related to performance than to directly shape the performance itself.” A self-monitoring sheet shapes exactly that description, and the match keeps it true.

The matching procedure comes from Rhode, Morgan and Young (1983): students rated their own behavior on a scale each period, the teacher rated it too, a match earned the points plus a bonus, and the teacher’s matching was faded from every period to a random sample to none as agreement held; the gains carried into the regular classroom. Salter and Croce’s Self & Match system (2006) is a published form of the same arrangement with Yes/No ratings and the 2-1-0 match points the sheets here reproduce. The cued-interval sheet is self-monitoring of attention (Hallahan, Lloyd & Stoller, 1982), with a tactile prompt where a beep would disturb the class (Amato-Zech, Hoff & Doepke, 2006); Reid, Trout and Schartz (2005) review it alongside self-monitoring of performance for students with ADHD. The interlocking-schedule session sheet is the school’s own (Royal Palm School manual, pp. 64–70): an interlocking schedule, in Ferster and Skinner’s (1957) term, makes the response requirement depend on the time elapsed, so the requirement can fall to protect quality or rise to build fluency. Goal stepping follows the changing-criterion logic (Hartmann & Hall, 1976).

Every point sheet is also a schedule of reinforcement, and the vocabulary for it is Ferster and Skinner’s (1957): the rating at the end of each period is an interval arrangement, the points needed for the reward are a ratio, and the end-of-day exchange makes the points conditioned reinforcers in a chain that ends at the back-up. Ferster and Skinner define the interlocking schedule as one in which “the organism is reinforced upon completion of a number of responses; but this number changes during the interval which follows the previous reinforcement” (Chapter 2); their example is a requirement of 300 that falls in a straight line to 1 over 10 minutes, and the school’s sheet, 20 items falling by 2 every 2 minutes, is the same schedule with smaller numbers. Catania’s (2007) table of schedule combinations gives the same definition in the company of the other combined schedules. The section “What the Schedule Does” below says what follows from each for the design.

What the Research Supports

Self-monitoring works, and the recording is part of whyAcross the school literature from 1988 to 2008, self-observation and recording of a predefined behavior are the cornerstones of the interventions that worked, with and without reinforcement attached (Briesch & Chafouleas, 2009); the systematic reviews since report the same for students with behavior problems (Bruhn et al., 2015) and for reducing challenging behavior (Smith, Thompson, Maynard & Kim, 2022). Reactivity is real but not reliable on its own, which is why this form attaches consequences from the first day.
Attention or performance: both workStudents who recorded whether they were paying attention and students who recorded how much work they finished both improved on on-task behavior and productivity (Lloyd, Bateman, Landrum & Hallahan, 1989; Maag, Reid & DiGangi, 1993). Pick the question the plan needs answered: the cued-interval sheet asks about attention; a count of items finished asks about performance; the interlocking sheet asks both at once.
Matching, with points for the match, generalizesRhode, Morgan and Young (1983) had six students rate their own behavior and work each period on a 0 to 5 scale, matched by the teacher with bonus points for a match within one point; matching was faded from every period to random and then to none, and the gains moved into the regular classroom with a reduced form of the procedure. The matching ladder on the Teach sheet is that sequence.
Self-management reaches social behavior and autismChildren with autism taught to self-monitor appropriate responses to questions with a wrist counter and a token exchange improved their social behavior and reduced disruption in settings where no adult was treating them (Koegel, Koegel, Hurley & Frea, 1992).
A home component strengthens a report cardAcross 17 daily-behavior-report-card studies and 228 students the mean improvement-rate difference was .61, and higher home involvement and use across the whole day went with stronger effects (Vannest et al., 2010). The home note and the whole-day sheet are on this form for that reason.
The points are a token economyA token economy has six procedural parts: the target responses, a token that works as a conditioned reinforcer, the back-up reinforcers, and three schedules (token production, exchange production, exchange) (Ivy, Meindl, Overley & Robson, 2017). Hackenberg’s (2018) review of token reinforcement sets out how exchange schedules and token-production schedules change the value of a point; the Reinforcement sheet names each of the six parts so none is left to chance.
Self & Match has an applied test with mixed functionsBulla and Frieder (2017) used the Self & Match system with attention extinction for a student with autism whose vocal stereotypy was maintained by attention and possibly by automatic reinforcement. Each interval the student circled a happy or sad face to “Was I quiet?”, the adult circled one too, a matched happy face earned a star, and four or five stars of six bought the reward he had chosen. Stereotypy fell from 80% of intervals to between 2% and 13% once the interval was lengthened from 10 to 15 and then 20 seconds (at 10 seconds he could not get into his work and aggression rose); the system was then faded into the class token economy with a variable-time praise timer, and the behavior stayed low (10%), with partial maintenance four months later (31%, still below baseline). The lessons on this form: the interval must be long enough to work in, criteria move on the data, and fading is planned.
Cued self-monitoring of attention in general educationRafferty, Arroyo, Ginnane and Wilczynski (2011) taught three fifth-grade students with ADHD to answer a cued “Was I paying attention?” during spelling practice. On-task behavior rose from 38–52% to 80–88%, two of the three reached the level of their peers in the same room, and weekly spelling accuracy rose by 15 to 33 points. Their nine planning steps (define, choose a measure, three to five baseline occasions, check for a performance deficit, design the materials and cue, teach with modelling and guided and independent practice, monitor, fade and check maintenance) are the Setup and Teach sheets of this form.
Self-monitoring of performance builds fluencyFarrell and McDougall (2008) combined a vibrating cue, a visual cue, self-graphing and a range-bound goal for five high-school students with disabilities during 3-minute math warm-ups. Correct digits rose by 77% over five weeks against 12% for 23 classmates, with accuracy held at 98%; the students liked it and named the vibrating timer as what they liked best. The increasing interlocking sheet and the performance count on the Targets sheet are the same arrangement: pace and quality together.
Accuracy is checked, not assumedCraig (2010) ran a self-administered DRO with self-monitoring for his own nail biting and had a second person check photographs against the record; the check kept the record honest and the biting fell from 16.5 to under 2 episodes a day. Ruby and DiGennaro Reed (2022) found group-home staff self-monitoring with accuracies of 21–68%, and most of the errors were failures to record at all rather than wrong records, although the target behavior still improved. The teacher’s independent rating on this form is the check; a cue to record is the answer to the omissions.
Adults can self-monitor tooJustus, Hott and Heiniger (2023) had three elementary teachers count their own behavior-specific praise on a hand counter, with nothing else added; praise rose during instruction and rose further during transitions. Ruby and DiGennaro Reed (2022) raised positive staff–consumer interactions with tablet self-monitoring, alone for one of three staff and with text-message feedback for the other two. When the Staff Fidelity checklist shows the adult side slipping, hand the adult a counter (Forms PD-1 and TI-1).
What the chapters on arranging reinforcement addDeLeon, Bullock and Catania (2013) and DeLeon, Graff, Frank-Crawford, Rooker and Bullock (2014) review the reinforcer side of an arrangement like this one. Choice among reinforcers is itself preferred and varied reinforcers hold up where a constant one declines; praise is the cheapest and best-fitting consequence but is not a reinforcer for everyone and must be tested; praise for effort rather than ability leads children to choose harder tasks; a reward the student can get free elsewhere buys less as the price rises; a reward delivered without any earning requirement loses value; and students often prefer, and work as well for, access accumulated to the end of the work over the same amount handed out in pieces. The Reinforcement sheet’s menu, delivery choice, back-up class, open-or-closed question and thinning row come from these findings.
Token loss is punishmentBehavior kept up by tokens behaves like behavior kept up by money, and taking tokens away works as a punisher that reduces responding (Raiff, Bullock & Hackenberg, 2008, reviewed in DeLeon et al., 2013). That is the evidence behind the rule that points are never removed: a response-cost sheet is a different intervention with different side effects, and it is not what this form builds.
Tokens bridge the delayReinforcers that are exchanged later, such as money, lose value with delay more slowly than reinforcers that are consumed at once (Charlton & Fantino, 2008, in DeLeon et al., 2013), and token systems “mediate delays between responding and reinforcer delivery” and resist satiation when they buy several things (DeLeon et al., 2014). The point earned at 9:15 carries the morning’s behavior to the 2:30 exchange; the more the menu offers, the better it carries.
Check-in / check-out has its own evidenceThe Behavior Education Program (Crone, Hawken & Horner, 2010) adds a morning check-in and an afternoon check-out with one adult to a 0-1-2 daily card. Hawken and Horner (2003) and Todd, Campbell, Meyer and Horner (2008) showed reductions in problem behavior in elementary schools; Hawken, Bundock, Kladis, O’Keeffe and Barrett (2014) and Maggin, Zurheide, Pickett and Johnson (2015) reviewed the single-case literature and found consistent effects, strongest for students whose behavior is maintained by adult attention. Campbell and Anderson (2011) took the program apart and found that the teacher feedback after each period was the component that mattered for the students in their study. The card here is that card; the function question on Setup is there because March and Horner (2002) found the program worked best when the function fit.
Self-monitoring of performance, and graphing itHarris, Friedlander, Saddler, Frizzelle and Graham (2005) compared self-monitoring of attention with self-monitoring of performance for students with ADHD during spelling practice: both raised on-task behavior, and performance monitoring was at least as good for the work itself. DiGangi, Maag and Rutherford (1991) added self-graphing to self-monitoring and found the reactive effect on on-task behavior and academic performance grew. The performance sheet counts the work and gives the student the bar to color.
Contracts work, with the terms exactKelley and Stokes (1982) raised the classroom work of disadvantaged youths with contingency contracts for daily academic output; Miller and Kelley (1994) combined goal setting and contracting and improved children’s homework accuracy and on-task behavior; Cantrell, Cantrell, Huddleston and Wooldridge (1969) reported the earliest school cases in the Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis; Mruzek, Cohen and Smith (2007) used contracts with students with autism in a public school. Bowman-Perrott et al. (2015) meta-analyzed the single-case research and found moderate effects on behavioral and academic outcomes. The Contract page follows Homme et al.’s (1970) form and rules.
The whole package, not just the recordingSelf-management packages in the school literature combine self-monitoring with goal setting, self-evaluation, self-instruction and self-reinforcement (Cooper, Heron & Heward, 2020). Meichenbaum and Goodman (1971) trained impulsive children to talk themselves through tasks; Rhode, Morgan and Young (1983) had students evaluate themselves against a standard; Koegel et al. (1992) let children with autism deliver their own token rewards. The Teach page writes each component so the sheet is not the only part of the system.
Goals are stepped, not set onceHartmann and Hall (1976) describe the changing-criterion design: a criterion raised in steps, each held until performance meets it, so the student is always within reach of the next reward. The record applies the rule written in advance.

What the Schedule Does

Ferster and Skinner (1957) recorded, schedule by schedule, what each arrangement of reinforcement does to behavior. The definitions below are from their Chapter 2 (“Schedules of reinforcement studied”) and the patterns from Chapters 4 and 5 (fixed ratio, fixed interval) and the opening of Chapter 6 (variable interval); the family of combined schedules is set out in Catania (2007). The quotations are from the B. F. Skinner Foundation e-book of the 1957 text.

The post-reinforcement pause and ratio strainIn a fixed ratio “a response is never reinforced just after a previous reinforcement,” so the reinforcer itself becomes the signal for a low rate: the pause after reinforcement, which lengthens as the ratio grows. Raise the ratio too far and the performance is “strained”: Chapter 4 shows a bird on FR 120 pausing for up to two hours after each reinforcement before running the ratio. Two findings from the same chapter matter for a point sheet. When a large ratio was reduced, the pauses “do not disappear immediately”; and when it was raised again they did not return at once either. So a lowered goal needs a few days to show its effect, and a raised one may look fine for a while before the strain appears. A points goal is a ratio: it is raised only by the step written in advance, only after 4 of 5 days, and lowered on fewer than 2 of 5; the decreasing interlocking sheet exists to prevent the strain on a long task (its own title says so).
The fixed-interval scallopA fixed-interval schedule “normally generates a stable state in which a pause follows each reinforcement, after which the rate accelerates to a terminal value” (Chapter 5): the scallop. Because reinforcement is timed, the schedule differentially reinforces responses that follow pauses, “the longer the interval since the last response, the more likely the next response is to be reinforced.” A rating at the end of every period can shape the same thing: work in the last minutes, drift in the first. The answers on this form are shorter periods, a rating of the whole period rather than its end, and a variable cue for the interval sheet. (Ruby and DiGennaro Reed, 2022, looked for this scalloping in staff interactions and did not find it; it is a pattern to watch for, not a certainty.)
Variable schedules hold steadyThe variable-interval schedule “is designed to produce a constant rate by not permitting any feature of the bird’s behavior to acquire discriminative properties,” and its shortest interval is set near zero “to prevent the development of a pause after reinforcement” (Chapter 6). The matching ladder thins the teacher’s matching from every period to a random sample, as Rhode, Morgan and Young (1983) did, which makes the match bonus a variable schedule; the sample must be genuinely unpredictable to the student, and it should sometimes include the period right after a reward.
Differential reinforcement of rateFerster and Skinner arrange differential reinforcement of low rates with “a timer which is reset by each response” that sets up reinforcement when it runs out, and note that reinforcing high rates is best done by taking “several responses into account in measuring the rate” rather than one fast pair (Chapter 3). The decreasing interlocking sheet pays for taking the time to do the work well; the increasing one pays for pace measured over whole minutes, which is what Farrell and McDougall (2008) built fluency with.
Interlocking: responses and time combinedIn Ferster and Skinner’s example the number required “may be set at 300 immediately after reinforcement, but it is reduced linearly, reaching 1 after 10 minutes. If the organism responds very rapidly, it will have to emit nearly 300 responses for reinforcement. If it responds at an intermediate rate, it will be reinforced after a smaller number… Many different cases are possible, depending upon the way in which the number changes with time” (Chapter 2). Berryman and Nevin (1962) ran rats along a continuum from a fixed ratio of 36 to a fixed interval of 2 minutes through such schedules and found performance changed continuously along it. The school’s sheet is the schedule written as a column, the items required at each minute, and the Direction field is the “different cases” clause.
Adjusting schedules: the changing criterionFerster and Skinner also define the adjusting schedule, “in which the value of the interval or ratio is changed in some systematic way after reinforcement as a function of the immediately preceding performance,” and point out that the interlocking change happens between reinforcements while the adjusting change happens after them (Chapter 2). The session sheet adjusts within a session; the goal-stepping rule on the Reinforcement sheet adjusts across days. Both are written in advance so the adjustment is a rule, not a mood.
The count is a stimulus: show the pointsChapter 4 argues that the number of responses since reinforcement is itself a discriminative stimulus and a conditioned reinforcer: a count of zero just after reinforcement “is the occasion upon which a response is never reinforced,” and each response is reinforced in part because it “advances this stimulus toward the reinforced end of the continuum.” Their added-counter experiments made the count visible and changed the pattern. On a point sheet the running total, the per-period match points, and the “2 in a row, I can earn” line are that counter; a sheet that hides the total until the end throws the effect away.
Delayed reinforcement reaches backA reinforcer strengthens not only the response that produced it but the responses before it, with less effect the further back they are; “if an error is immediately followed by a reinforced correct response… that reinforcer will probably strengthen the error along with the correct response” (DeLeon, Bullock & Catania, 2013). A match point given right after a rough minute pays for the rough minute too. Rate the whole period, and when a period ends badly let the rating say so.
Interval schedules are steadier than ratiosWhen a ratio slips, reinforcers come less often, which slows responding further, which slows reinforcement further; interval schedules hold more stable responding because a wide range of rates earns about the same reinforcement (DeLeon et al., 2013, after Nevin & Grace, 2000). The period rating is an interval arrangement and is the steadier part of the sheet; the points goal is the ratio and is the part that can spiral, which is what the lower-on-fewer-than-2-of-5 rule is for.
Chained schedules and the value of a pointIn a chained schedule the stimulus produced by finishing the first component “as a conditioned reinforcer, reinforces a response to the first stimulus”; conditioned reinforcers such as a light or buzzer “are useful because they can be made contingent on a particular form of a response” (Chapter 2). A point is such a stimulus, given for exactly the behavior named on the sheet; Hackenberg (2018) treats token economies as second-order schedules of this kind. The point keeps its value only while it reliably leads to the back-up, so an exchange that is skipped, delayed, or made unaffordable weakens every point before it.

How the Figures Are Calculated

Points possibleSelf & Match: periods × targets × the highest match value. Contract: periods × targets. Rubric: periods × the top level’s points. Cued intervals: the number of intervals. Expectations and earns: periods × targets (one face each). Interlocking: the session is met or not.
Points goalThe goal percent of the points possible, rounded up; this is the number on the sheet (“If I earn 39 of 48 points…”).
Percent of pointsPoints earned ÷ points possible × 100, per day.
AgreementRatings on which the student and the teacher agreed ÷ ratings both made × 100. Only matched ratings count; a period the teacher did not rate is left out.
Baseline meanThe mean percent over the teacher-only days (phase 0). The suggested first goal is that mean plus 10 points, rounded to 5, between 50 and 80: a working convention.
Share at each level (rubric)Periods at the level ÷ periods rated that day × 100, as the school’s point sheet shows.
Decision rulesWritten on the Reinforcement sheet and applied to the last five days: raise the goal when it was met on 4 of 5; lower it or revisit the reinforcer when met on fewer than 2 of 5; move down the matching ladder when agreement over the last five days is below 80%; move up when it is at or above 90% and the goal was met on 4 of 5. The two agreement rules apply only to a system with a teacher match (Self & Match, cued intervals, the interlocking session, the rubric with the teacher matching). All four are working conventions of this form, not published thresholds.
Performance sheetPoints possible are the sessions on the sheet; a session counts as met when the count reaches the goal; the goal percent is the share of sessions met. The graph top is the number entered; the goal line is drawn at the goal count.
Check-in / check-outPoints possible are periods × expectations × 2; the daily percent is points earned over points possible, against the goal entered on the System sheet (80% is the program’s usual starting goal and a working convention here).
Interlocking requirementStarting at the initial requirement, the figure changes by the step every N minutes in the chosen direction, between the floor and the ceiling. The sheet lists the requirement at each minute; the session ends when the items are done and both engagement checks at that minute are Yes.

What Keeps a Sheet Honest, and What Breaks It

Points are never removedA sheet that takes points away becomes a punishment card; students stop rating honestly and start arguing. Mismatches earn nothing; they do not cost anything already earned.
Honest No paysThe 2-1-0 arrangement pays a point for an agreed No. Praise the honest No out loud; it is the behavior that makes the rest of the system work.
The reward is chosen, and soonChoice from a menu each morning keeps the reinforcer current; delivery the same day, within the student’s reach at the start (a goal met on fewer than half of baseline days is too far away).
The teacher rates firstA few days of teacher-only rating give the baseline and the goal, and let the adults practise the rating before the student watches them do it.
Fade on the dataMatching is faded as agreement holds, not on the calendar; a drop in agreement is the signal to step back, and nothing is lost by doing so.
The sheet is not the planSelf-monitoring tracks and rewards the replacement behaviors the plan teaches; it does not replace teaching them. A behavior with an automatic component needs the FBA first and a component that competes with the behavior alongside the sheet: Bulla and Frieder (2017) paired Self & Match with attention extinction and a chosen reward for stereotypy that was partly automatic, and it worked only once the interval was long enough for the student to get into his work.
The rule lives on the sheet, not in the adult’s mouthA bribe is a contingency announced at the moment of a request; the child learns to comply only when the offer is made (DeLeon et al., 2013). The goal sentence is written on the sheet once and read at the start of the day; nobody says “do this and you get your point” before each task.
Not for behavior that already happensThe one consistent finding in the overjustification literature is that added rewards can lower behavior that already occurs at a high level without them (DeLeon et al., 2013). The performance-deficit check on the Setup sheet is there so the sheet pays for a behavior that is in the repertoire but not yet frequent, not for one the student already does freely.
Record first, then rateMost self-monitoring errors are missed recordings, not wrong ones (Ruby & DiGennaro Reed, 2022). The cue, the sheet on the desk, and the one prompt in the fidelity checklist are there so the record is made at all.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated system is a third-grader’s pictorial Self & Match sheet with three targets over six periods, a 4-day teacher-only baseline, three weeks of self-rating with the goal stepped from 70 to 80, and the matching ladder at phase 1.
