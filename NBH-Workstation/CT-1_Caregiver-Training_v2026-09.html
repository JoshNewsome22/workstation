What the family was told this is for
Caregivers are not staff, and this form is not Form ST-1 with the nouns changed. A caregiver has no supervisor, no colleague to tap out to, no end of shift, and a relationship with the student that continues after the procedure ends. They also hold information no one else has. Training that ignores this produces agreement in the session and nothing afterwards.

If the Behavior Comes Back

This is the part that matters most. Briggs et al. (2018) found resurgence in 76% of applications when an FCT schedule was thinned; every episode was temporary, but the thinning was run by highly trained therapists in a structured setting, and the authors suggest that episodes may be more sustained when caregivers, who may be less likely to hold extinction, run it. Kimball et al. (2023) note that far fewer relapse-mitigation procedures have been evaluated for caregiver behavior than for client behavior, a gap they call vitally important. Delivering the reinforcer once the behavior is already resurging would likely sustain the episode (Briggs et al., 2018), which makes it the error this training is built to prevent.

How Caregiver Training Differs from Staff Training

The procedure is the same package — instruction, modeling, rehearsal, feedback, to a criterion — and Form ST-1 covers it for staff. What differs is everything around it. A caregiver has no supervisor to escalate to, no colleague to take over, and no point at which the day ends. The relationship continues after the procedure, so a procedure that damages it costs more than it does at school. And the caregiver knows things about the student that no assessment will surface, which is why the goals sheet comes before the training sheet on this form.

The criterion here defaults to 80% rather than the 90% used for staff. That is a working convention rather than a finding: a caregiver running a plan across a whole day under conditions nobody controls is a different performance from an aide running it for twenty minutes with a BCBA present, and a criterion that cannot be met in the real setting produces either a false record or an abandoned plan.

Why the Relapse Response Is the Centrepiece

Across the schedule-thinning literature, resurgence is the normal case rather than the exception, and delivering the reinforcer while the behavior is occurring would likely sustain an episode rather than let it pass (Briggs et al., 2018). In the clinic data Briggs et al. analyzed, the procedure was held by highly trained therapists in a structured setting. At home it is done by a parent, at the end of a day, often alone, with the behavior escalating and an easy way to stop it available. Rehearsing that specific moment — not describing it — is the part of this training most likely to change what actually happens.

The last question under If the Behavior Comes Back asks what would make the family stop running the plan. It is there because the answer is usually concrete and usually fixable, and because a family that has never been asked will stop without saying so.

The Rehearsal Runner

At the top of the Training sheet, Set up a round offers the next empty round, or one you choose, with its arrangement, and fills in the date from this device’s calendar; on a phone it can be used standing in the routine. Code each step as the caregiver performs it with Correct, Commission, Omission or N/A, one step at a time or with all steps listed (keys C, M, O and N; the arrow keys move between steps, Z undoes the last code and Space pauses the round timer), with the round’s accuracy against the criterion, and the critical steps correct, shown as you go. A step that comes due more than once in a round is coded each time and written to the grid as on Forms TI-1 and ST-1: Commission if any of those times was a commission error, otherwise Omission if any was an omission error, otherwise Correct. That is a working convention, not a finding.

End round gives a feedback prompt built from that round’s own codes: each step not performed correctly, critical steps first, with its description and whether the error was one of commission or omission, then the steps performed correctly. Parsons, Rollyson & Reid (2012) recommend feedback following each role play, describing what was performed correctly and specifying what was not. Save to the round writes its codes, date and arrangement, and the feedback you record, through the grid’s own fields, so every figure and the decision are recalculated as for hand coding, and ticks, as you choose, the components delivered under Components Delivered. A round set up as Home probe is coded the same way, and saving it also fills In-home probe date and result, Steps correct on the probe and Critical steps correct on the probe from its codes (an entry already there is kept or replaced, as you choose), so the probe’s figure no longer has to be typed; the round itself counts in the grid like any other round, as the table below says. Replacing a round that holds entries, and Discard, ask first. The runner never prints.

How the Figures Are Calculated

Round accuracyWithin one rehearsal round: correct steps ÷ scored steps, with N/A and blank cells left out of the denominator. A round with no scored step is left out of every figure; a round marked Home probe is counted like any other round. The probe that clears the caregiver is the one recorded under Follow-up and the Family’s View.
Per-step accuracyFor each step, the percentage of the rounds in which it was scored that it was performed correctly.
Critical stepsThe same calculation restricted to steps marked critical, pooled across the decision rounds: once the criterion has been met, the latest rounds it counts (the latest two when it asks for two consecutive rounds); before that, every scored round. Every step marked critical must be scored in the decision rounds and correct every time.
Rounds to criterionThe number of scored rounds up to and including the round in which the criterion was first met on the required number of consecutive rounds. The card and the verdict name that round as it is labelled on the grid.
Competency decisionCriterion met on the required consecutive rounds and still met in the latest rounds it counts, and every step marked critical scored in those rounds and correct every time, and the relapse response rehearsed under a hard condition, and an in-home probe recorded with the steps correct on it at or above the criterion percentage. Parsons, Rollyson & Reid (2012) suggest that where the mastery criterion is a lower percentage than 100%, certain critical steps be identified that must be performed at 100%; holding the in-home probe to the rehearsal percentage is this form’s convention, as it is on Form ST-1. When the critical steps correct on the probe are recorded (the rehearsal runner fills them from a round set up as Home probe), every critical step scored on the probe must have been correct, as in the decision rounds. That, too, is this form’s convention, as on Form ST-1.
