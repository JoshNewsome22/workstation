Build and print the visuals a plan needs at true size: communication boards with a category band and velcro dots, picture-card sheets for cutting, large rule cards (safe hands, walking feet, sitting, waiting, quiet voice), first-then boards, visual schedules, choice boards, token boards, wait and countdown cards, and a feelings scale. Communication Board

Picture Cards for Cutting

Sheets of repeated cards, like the Writing and Shopping sheets: a picture with its word, a fixed number of each, cut lines between them. Laminate, cut, and put velcro on the back. Two inches is the usual exchange-card size; one and a half or two inches matches the visual schedule here, and one inch a small desk strip; three inches suits a first-then board.

Rule Cards and the Expectations Poster

Large cards for the expectations a plan teaches: a picture, the rule in two or three words, and a short sentence the adult says when pre-correcting. Tick the rules this student needs; each prints at the size chosen. The poster puts the ticked rules on one page for the wall. Keep the wording positive, what to do rather than what to stop. Choose the rules from the plan, and leave out eye-contact and still-hands rules unless the team has decided they matter for this student.

Visual schedule

A schedule tells the student what comes next; it does not by itself reduce problem behavior at transitions, above all when the next activity is less preferred. Put a preferred step after each hard one (first-then), reinforce the transition itself, and write the response to problem behavior on Form TD-1; an extinction step belongs there, marked as extinction, and is never printed on the visual. Mark each step Work or Preferred below: the form flags two or more work steps in a row with no preferred step after them.

If reading the word is a goal, pictures beside the word can slow it; fade the picture. Conditional discrimination training moved schedule control from pictures to printed words for two preschoolers (Miguel et al., 2009). Joint schedules increased peer engagement for three pairs of preschoolers (Betz et al., 2008), and linked schedules taught a game to six children (Brodhead et al., 2014): mark the steps the two do together as Together. A watch-based schedule supported independent routines and was less conspicuous than a binder (Jimenez-Gomez et al., 2021): Save for a tablet or watch makes a one-step-at-a-time page of this schedule that opens in any browser.

What This Form Builds

Visual supports are an evidence-based practice for learners with autism in the National Clearinghouse review (Steinbrenner et al., 2020), and most of what this form prints has its own line of research. The communication board and the picture cards are the materials of the Picture Exchange Communication System (Bondy & Frost, 1994): the learner hands a picture to a partner to request, and the system moves through six phases from a single exchange to sentences and comments. Charlop-Christy, Carpenter, Le, LeBlanc and Kellet (2002) taught PECS to three children with autism and saw gains in speech and social behavior and reductions in problem behavior alongside the exchanges; those were three children, and not the usual outcome: Flippin, Reszka and Watson (2010) judged PECS promising but not yet established, with small to moderate gains in communication and small to negative gains in speech; Ganz et al. (2012) meta-analyzed the broader aided-AAC literature. The board is a tool, not a program: the teaching protocol, the partner training and the data belong on Forms SA-1 and TD-1.

The visual schedule and the first-then board rest on the activity-schedule research: MacDuff, Krantz and McClannahan (1993) taught children with autism to follow photographic schedules through long chains of activities with maintenance and generalization; Lequia, Machalicek and Rispoli (2012) reviewed activity schedules for challenging behavior, and Knight, Sartini and Spriggs (2015) judged visual activity schedules an evidence-based practice. Dettmer, Simpson, Myles and Ganz (2000) showed visual supports easing transitions; in the one JABA test, schedules alone did not reduce problem behavior at transitions to a less preferred activity (Waters, Lerman & Hovanetz, 2009), so the schedule comes with a preferred step after the hard ones and a written response on Form TD-1. The first-then board is the Premack principle made visible (Premack, 1959): the preferred activity follows the less preferred one. The choice board rests on the choice-making literature (Shogren, Faggella-Luby, Bae & Wehmeyer, 2004); the token board on token reinforcement (Hackenberg, 2018; Form TE-1); the wait card and countdown on delay-tolerance teaching (Hanley, Jin, Vanselow & Hanratty, 2014; Form DT-1). Rule cards are the materials of precorrection (Colvin, Sugai & Patching, 1993): the adult shows the card and states the expectation before the situation that evokes the problem, then reinforces the expected behavior. The feelings scale follows a widely used program (Buron & Curtis, 2003) whose peer-reviewed evidence is limited; it is here as a teaching visual, with the plan’s coping responses written beside each level.

What the Research Supports

Picture exchange teaches requestingIn PECS the learner initiates by giving a picture, which is why the cards are loose and the board is velcro: the picture has to travel. Phase I is a single exchange with two trainers (one prompting from behind), Phase II adds distance and persistence, Phase III discrimination between pictures, Phase IV sentence strips, Phases V and VI answering and commenting (Bondy & Frost, 1994). Charlop-Christy et al. (2002) report all three children reaching Phase VI with collateral gains; Flippin et al. (2010) judged PECS promising but not yet established: gains in communication were small to moderate, and gains in speech small to negative.
Schedules build independenceMacDuff et al. (1993) used graduated guidance to teach photographic schedules and faded all prompts; the children moved through chains of leisure and homework activities on their own and kept doing so with new photographs. Knight et al. (2015) rated visual activity schedules evidence-based on the single-case literature, and Lequia et al. (2012) found reductions in challenging behavior across the studies they reviewed, with on-schedule behavior and transitions the usual targets. MacDuff et al. gave manual prompts from behind and faded them as rapidly as possible, from guidance to spatial fading, then shadowing, then distance; Brodhead, Courtney and Thaxton (2018) also taught schedule use with physical guidance. The research is hands-on, so the limit is written: guidance only while the student allows it (How to teach it, on the printed pages). Parents taught photographic schedules at home, and engagement and social initiations rose and disruptive behavior fell, maintained for up to 10 months (Krantz, MacDuff & McClannahan, 1993); photographic schedules increased independent playground activities for three children (Akers et al., 2016). The schedule is the prompt that stays: fade the adult, not the schedule. When the schedule was removed, responding fell to baseline (Brodhead et al., 2014, 2018).
A schedule alone does not fix transitionsDettmer et al. (2000) added visual schedules and transition cues for two children with autism and recorded shorter transition latencies and fewer physical prompts. But a schedule tells the student what comes next; it does not by itself reduce problem behavior at transitions, above all when the next activity is less preferred. For two children, visual schedules alone were ineffective when going from a preferred to a non-preferred activity; problem behavior fell only when DRO and extinction were added, with or without the schedule (Waters et al., 2009). Problem behavior was more likely in transitions toward a context with less reinforcement (Castillo et al., 2018), dawdling was seen mostly in transitions to lean contexts, and rich reinforcement programmed in the next context shortened transitions (Jessel, Hanley & Ghaemmaghami, 2016). Advance notice of a change reduced stereotypy for one man with autism (Tustin, 1995): signals can help, but the reinforcement arrangement carries most of the effect. So put a preferred step after each hard one (first-then), reinforce the transition itself, and write the response to problem behavior on Form TD-1; any extinction step belongs there, marked as extinction, and is not printed on the visual.
Guidance only while the student allows itA physical prompt is given only while the student allows it. If the student pulls away, stiffens or pushes the adult’s hand, the adult lets go, waits, and offers a lighter prompt or a short break. Holding a hand, an arm or any body part to stop a response, or as a consequence, is restraint and is not a prompt. For three adults with profound multiple disabilities, a teaching program that gave a brief break and a preferred activity after resistance reduced resistance and signs of unhappiness, and performance of the teaching steps did not suffer (Green et al., 2005). Assent can be withdrawn at any time and should be watched for (Morris, Detrick & Peterson, 2021). The model prompt was more efficient than the physical prompt for 6 of 10 learners (Seaver & Bourret, 2014): giving up guidance often costs nothing. Physical guidance after a refusal is guided compliance, which is escape extinction, not a teaching prompt: it is written on Form TD-1, marked as extinction, and never the default.
Choice itself mattersShogren et al. (2004) meta-analyzed choice-making as an intervention for problem behavior and found reductions across studies; a choice board gives the choice a form a non-speaking student can use. Tiger, Hanley and Hernandez (2006) found that some preschoolers (3 of 6) consistently preferred having a choice even when the outcomes were the same, and that the preference for choice grew when more items were offered.
Precorrection with a cardColvin et al. (1993) set out precorrection: identify the context, state the expected behavior, modify the context, rehearse, reinforce, prompt, monitor. The rule card is the prompt and the rehearsal aid; the poster is the context modification.
Tokens and waitingToken boards carry the token-production and exchange schedules Hackenberg (2018) reviews; the token board here shows the student the count, which Ferster and Skinner’s analysis of the count as a stimulus argues for (Form SR-1). A signal helps a learner wait (Vollmer et al., 1999), but the requirement during the wait does the teaching: in Ghaemmaghami, Hanley and Jessel (2016) a delay that simply ran out failed and a delay ended by a small task or calm behavior worked, as in Hanley et al. (2014). The countdown says what each square stands for; the delay itself is run on Form DT-1.
Picture typeReal objects and photographs are usually easier to match to the thing they stand for than line drawings (Mirenda & Locke, 1989). Use the type the student already matches, and teach each picture with more than one example of the item: neither a photo nor a drawing carries over to new examples on its own. The library gives both: the student’s own photos and the pictograms.

Making and Using the Visuals

SizesExchange cards 2 inches; schedule cards 1.5 to 2 inches; first-then cards 3 inches; rule cards half a page for a desk, a full page for the wall. The preview is drawn at the printed size.
PrintingPrint at 100%, not “fit to page”. Laminate boards and cards; put the hook side of the velcro on the board dots and the loop side on the cards so cards do not catch on clothing.
TeachingA visual that is handed to a student without teaching is decoration. Each visual has a teaching procedure in the research named above; write it on Form SA-1 or TD-1, and prompt from behind the student when the visual is for communication. The first-then and schedule pages print a How to teach it box for the adults (Strips page); Start an SA-1 program for this schedule writes the schedule as a task analysis for Form SA-1, taught as a total task (v25.65).
From pictures to wordsIf reading the word is a goal, pictures beside the word can slow it; fade the picture. Conditional discrimination training moved schedule control from pictures to printed words for two preschoolers with autism, who then also read the words (Miguel, Yang, Finn & Ahearn, 2009). The cards and the schedule each have a Words only step.
Joint schedules, and a schedule on a watchJoint activity schedules increased peer engagement for three pairs of preschoolers with autism (Betz, Higbee & Reagon, 2008); linked schedules taught hide-and-seek to six children (Brodhead et al., 2014), and linked digital schedules increased cooperative play (Campbell et al., 2026). A watch-based schedule supported independent routines and was less conspicuous than a binder (Jimenez-Gomez, Haggerty & Topçuoglu, 2021). The schedule can print a partner’s strip with the Together steps linked, and be saved as a page for a tablet or watch.
WordingRules say what to do. Labels are the student’s words. One picture means one thing: do not reuse a picture for two labels.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated visuals are a NEEDS board, three card sheets, five rule cards with the poster, and every strip.
