What This Form Builds

Visual supports are an evidence-based practice for learners with autism in the National Clearinghouse review (Steinbrenner et al., 2020), and most of what this form prints has its own line of research. The communication board and the picture cards are the materials of the Picture Exchange Communication System (Bondy & Frost, 1994): the learner hands a picture to a partner to request, and the system moves through six phases from a single exchange to sentences and comments. Charlop-Christy, Carpenter, Le, LeBlanc and Kellet (2002) taught PECS to three children with autism and saw gains in speech and social behavior and reductions in problem behavior alongside the exchanges; Flippin, Reszka and Watson (2010) meta-analyzed the communication effects, and Ganz et al. (2012) the broader aided-AAC literature. The board is a tool, not a program: the teaching protocol, the partner training and the data belong on Forms SA-1 and TD-1.

The visual schedule and the first-then board rest on the activity-schedule research: MacDuff, Krantz and McClannahan (1993) taught children with autism to follow photographic schedules through long chains of activities with maintenance and generalization; Lequia, Machalicek and Rispoli (2012) reviewed activity schedules for challenging behavior, and Knight, Sartini and Spriggs (2015) judged visual activity schedules an evidence-based practice. Dettmer, Simpson, Myles and Ganz (2000) showed visual supports easing transitions. The first-then board is the Premack principle made visible (Premack, 1959): the preferred activity follows the less preferred one. The choice board rests on the choice-making literature (Shogren, Faggella-Luby, Bae & Wehmeyer, 2004); the token board on token reinforcement (Hackenberg, 2018; Form TE-1); the wait card and countdown on delay-tolerance teaching (Hanley, Jin, Vanselow & Hanratty, 2014; Form DT-1). Rule cards are the materials of precorrection (Colvin, Sugai & Patching, 1993): the adult shows the card and states the expectation before the situation that evokes the problem, then reinforces the expected behavior. The feelings scale follows a widely used program (Buron & Curtis, 2003) whose peer-reviewed evidence is limited; it is here as a teaching visual, with the plan’s coping responses written beside each level.

What the Research Supports

Picture exchange teaches requestingIn PECS the learner initiates by giving a picture, which is why the cards are loose and the board is velcro: the picture has to travel. Phase I is a single exchange with two trainers (one prompting from behind), Phase II adds distance and persistence, Phase III discrimination between pictures, Phase IV sentence strips, Phases V and VI answering and commenting (Bondy & Frost, 1994). Charlop-Christy et al. (2002) report all three children reaching Phase VI with collateral gains; Flippin et al. (2010) find small to moderate effects on communication across the single-case studies and note that speech gains are variable.
Schedules build independenceMacDuff et al. (1993) used graduated guidance to teach photographic schedules and faded all prompts; the children moved through chains of leisure and homework activities on their own and kept doing so with new photographs. Knight et al. (2015) rated visual activity schedules evidence-based on the single-case literature, and Lequia et al. (2012) found reductions in challenging behavior across the studies they reviewed, with on-schedule behavior and transitions the usual targets.
Transitions go better with a visualDettmer et al. (2000) added visual schedules and transition cues for two children with autism and recorded shorter transition latencies and fewer physical prompts.
Choice itself mattersShogren et al. (2004) meta-analyzed choice-making as an intervention for problem behavior and found reductions across studies; a choice board gives the choice a form a non-speaking student can use. Tiger, Hanley and Hernandez (2006) found preschoolers preferred having the choice even when the outcomes were the same.
Precorrection with a cardColvin et al. (1993) set out precorrection: identify the context, state the expected behavior, modify the context, rehearse, reinforce, prompt, monitor. The rule card is the prompt and the rehearsal aid; the poster is the context modification.
Tokens and waitingToken boards carry the token-production and exchange schedules Hackenberg (2018) reviews; the token board here shows the student the count, which Ferster and Skinner’s analysis of the count as a stimulus argues for (Form SR-1). Hanley et al. (2014) taught tolerance of denial and delay with a signal and something to do during the wait; the wait card and countdown are those signals.
Picture typePhotographs are more concrete than line drawings and are the usual first step for a learner new to pictures; a drawing generalizes across the many toilets and buses a photo does not show. The library gives both: the student’s own photos and the pictograms.

Making and Using the Visuals

SizesExchange cards 2 inches; schedule cards 1.5 to 2 inches; first-then cards 3 inches; rule cards half a page for a desk, a full page for the wall. The preview is drawn at the printed size.
PrintingPrint at 100%, not “fit to page”. Laminate boards and cards; put the hook side of the velcro on the board dots and the loop side on the cards so cards do not catch on clothing.
TeachingA visual that is handed to a student without teaching is decoration. Each visual has a teaching procedure in the research named above; write it on Form SA-1 or TD-1, and prompt from behind the student when the visual is for communication.
WordingRules say what to do. Labels are the student’s words. One picture means one thing: do not reuse a picture for two labels.
