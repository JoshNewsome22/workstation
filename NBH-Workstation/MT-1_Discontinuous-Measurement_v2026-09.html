The Interval

Choose the interval length from the behavior and the observation length from the activity; the number of intervals follows from the two: a 30-minute observation in 10-second intervals is 180 intervals, in 20-second intervals 90, and a 10-minute observation in 20-second intervals is 30. The derived count shows beside the selectors, and only lengths that divide the observation evenly are offered.

IntervalWhat it suits
10 secondsBrief, discrete behaviors (talk-outs, hitting, swiping materials) with partial interval: for events of this kind Rapp et al. (2008) found that only 10-second partial-interval recording reliably detected most changes. The finest look for momentary sampling.
15 to 30 secondsMomentary samples of long-duration behavior (on-task, engagement, out of seat): with intervals up to 30 seconds momentary time sampling detected most moderate and large changes in duration events (Rapp et al., 2008). Whole interval for a behavior you are increasing.
1 minute or moreScreening, or a behavior that comes in long bouts. A poor estimate of anything brief, and the longer a partial interval, the more it overestimates.

The paper sheet this form was modelled on set the interval by a ratio, minutes of observation : seconds of interval (10 minutes at 1:2 is 20 seconds). The form shows that ratio when there is one; a file saved with a ratio keeps its interval.

Start from the episode. How long one episode of the behavior lasts sets the error more than anything else. In a full simulation, error was smallest when the interval was no longer than an episode and grew sharply when it was longer, and the authors warn that any one-line rule for choosing the interval will mislead (Wirth et al., 2014). So the sheet asks three things: how long a typical episode lasts, about how many come in the observation, and whether how often or how long matters more. The Suggested measure then reads:

Countable episodes, and how often mattersEpisodes with a clear start and end, fewer than about 1 every 10 seconds: count them on Form OB-1’s Live Recorder. Partial-interval data were a poor index of how often a behavior occurred (Powell & Rockinson, 1978), and most published studies of free-operant behavior now record continuously (55% of them from 1995 to 2005; Mudford, Taylor, & Martin, 2009).
How long mattersMomentary time sampling, with an interval no longer than the typical episode. It estimated duration better than partial interval (Harrop & Daniels, 1986).
Brief episodes, too many to countPartial interval, 10-second intervals. It estimates how often, and undercounts when episodes come several to an interval.
A behavior you are increasingWhole interval or momentary time sampling.

An interval longer than the typical episode is flagged. When the observer is also teaching, a very short momentary interval can lower agreement: with one observer watching 20 children, 30-second momentary intervals gave agreement below 80%, and 60 to 120 seconds gave above 90% with under 10% error (Hanley et al., 2007). Check with a second observer before relying on it.

Which Method, and What It Does to the Number

All three produce a percentage of intervals, and none of them produces a duration. The percentage is an estimate: partial and whole interval are biased in known directions, and momentary time sampling errs in both — which is why the method must be recorded on every sheet and held constant across a phase.

Momentary time samplingLook only at the moment the interval ends and score whether the behavior is occurring then. Its errors are not biased on average, but in any one observation they can be large. It tends to read low when the behavior fills little of the time and high when it fills most of it. It is least accurate in short observations (Wirth et al., 2014). Its errors shrink as the interval gets short relative to the behavior, and it has estimated duration better than either interval method. It frees the observer between marks — which is why it suits a teacher who is also teaching. Brief behaviors are missed unless one is under way at a mark.
Partial intervalScore the interval if the behavior occurred at any point during it. Overestimates duration, and the longer the interval the worse the overestimate. Useful when you want to be sensitive to occurrence of a behavior you are trying to reduce, and appropriate for brief, discrete behaviors that momentary sampling would miss.
Whole intervalScore the interval only if the behavior occurred throughout it. Underestimates duration. Suits behavior you are trying to increase — engagement, on-task, sustained play — because it sets a conservative bar and will not credit a fragment.

A practical consequence worth stating to a team: switching a reduction target from partial interval to momentary sampling will usually make the graph fall without anything about the behavior changing. Record the method on the graph, not only on the sheet.

Interval measures tend to show less change than really occurred. When the same treatment data were judged by structured visual criteria from continuous records and from momentary or partial-interval data, every error the interval data produced was a missed effect: a change that was there and did not show (Meany-Daboul et al., 2007). Changes in the continuous measure were larger than the changes in the interval measures (Powell et al., 1977), and partial interval, though it detects change, tends to understate how much (Harrop & Daniels, 1986). As a behavior you are reducing falls, partial interval’s overestimate grows, so the graph will understate the improvement. Every verdict and the foot of the graph say so: a small drop, or none, does not show that the plan failed. Check against a count or a timed sample before changing the plan.

Short observations and a behavior that fills little of the time. Behavior that filled 1 to 3% of the time was poorly estimated even from 135-minute samples, and behavior filling 10 to 25% needed at least 105 minutes to come within 20% error (Mudford et al., 1990). When the behavior is scored in under 10% of intervals in an observation of 15 minutes or less, the sheet says so: add observations across the day before comparing phases.

Phase Changes on the Session Log

The session log uses the same convention as Form DD-1, so the two graphs read the same way.

Full phase changeA solid vertical line, and the data path breaks at it. Use it when the condition itself changes — the plan starts, a component is added or withdrawn, a reversal begins. Each condition is then read on its own, because level, trend, and variability are not meaningful across a boundary the intervention moved.
Conditional changeA dashed vertical line, and the path continues. Use it for something that changed inside the condition without changing the condition — a new aide, a medication change, a room move, a holiday. The data stay one series because the intervention did not change, but the marker is there when you are asked why that week looks different.

The label you type appears beside the line. Labels alternate between two heights so that two changes close together do not overlap, and a label near the right edge flips to the left of its line. The measure is written above the graph, and a session measured differently from the one before gets a dotted line: a step there may be the measure. Form GB-1’s criterion for the behavior, when it is a percentage of intervals, is a dashed green line; under the graph, each condition’s sessions, dates, level and agreement.

The Observation Timer

The timer above the sheet takes its settings from the sheet. The observation length sets the clock and the interval length sets the marks, so the marks always match the rows: 5 minutes in 5-second intervals runs 5:00 with a mark every 5 seconds (60 marks); 10 minutes in 20-second intervals runs 10:00 with a mark every 20 seconds (30 marks). Method, length and interval are locked while the timer runs, so the marks cannot drift away from the sheet; changed once marks are scored, they ask first and then clear the marks.

At each markThe timer alerts you with a beep, a flash around the edge of the screen, and a vibration on a phone or tablet. The interval waiting to be scored turns amber on the sheet. Score it with the large +, − and N/A buttons, with the keys +, − and N, or on the sheet itself. A press always fills the oldest interval still waiting, so a missed mark is never skipped over silently.
Momentary time samplingAt the beep, look at the student and score what is happening at that moment. The buttons will not score a mark before it arrives.
Partial intervalPress + the moment the behavior occurs; that interval is settled, and its alert is skipped (unless an earlier interval is still waiting: a press fills that one first). At the beep, press − if the behavior never occurred.
Whole intervalPress − the moment the behavior stops or is absent; that interval is settled (unless an earlier interval is still waiting: a press fills that one first). At the beep, press + only if the behavior lasted the whole interval.
AlertsSound, flash and vibration can each be turned off, and Alerts on turns all of them off at once; the clock and the amber highlight keep running. 3-2-1 ticks adds three soft ticks before each mark when marks are 10 seconds or more apart, so you can look up in time for a momentary sample. Test alert plays one alert. These settings are saved with the data file.
Pause and resetPause stops the clock, for example when the student leaves the room. Each pause keeps when it began, how long it lasted and why (left the room, a break, a plan procedure running, other), and the sheet prints them. Time paused while a plan procedure ran is flagged measured outside the procedure only: the sheet then says nothing about the behavior during the procedure, and leaving that time out overestimated the effect of response interruption and redirection for both children studied (Carroll & Kodak, 2014). Reset timer returns the clock to 0:00 without touching the marks; Clear sheet for the next session resets both. The date and start time fill in when you press Start, if they are blank.
TimingThe clock is computed from the device’s own time rather than by counting ticks, so it does not drift. Beeps are scheduled ahead on the audio clock, so on a computer they keep time even if you switch to another tab; a phone or tablet may pause a page that is not on screen, so keep the form in front during the observation. While the timer runs, the screen is kept awake where the browser allows it.

Agreement

Interval data support point-by-point agreement directly: the number of intervals both observers scored the same way, divided by the number of intervals both observers scored + or −, times 100. Occurrence agreement is the intervals both scored + divided by the intervals either scored +; non-occurrence agreement is the same for −. Overall agreement is hard to interpret when the behavior is scored in very few or very many intervals, so report occurrence agreement for a behavior scored in few intervals and non-occurrence agreement for one scored in most — two observers who both score mostly zeros will produce a high overall figure that says nothing about whether they agree on the behavior, and so will two who both score mostly plus marks.

An interval either observer marked N/A, or that only one observer scored, is left out of all three figures: N/A means the observer could not see, so it is neither an agreement nor a disagreement. The sheet flags occurrence or non-occurrence agreement below 80% and names the figure to act on: occurrence agreement when fewer than half of the two observers' marks in the intervals compared are +, non-occurrence agreement when more than half are. The 80% cut-off is this form's convention, not a finding. Capture carries the three figures to the session log’s IOA column, as point-by-point / occurrence / non-occurrence, and the CSV lists the log after the intervals.

Under each figure is the agreement two observers would reach by chance, scoring independently at the rates they did (Hopkins & Hermann, 1977); a figure close to its chance value says little about agreement on the behavior, whatever the 80% convention says.

Under point-by-point agreement is kappa, the same comparison as one number: (observed − chance) ÷ (1 − chance), from the same intervals. Kappa below .60 is flagged; .60 is Hartmann’s (1977) suggestion, not a validated cut-off. Kappa cannot be computed when both observers scored every interval compared the same way.

Coverage and drift. Under the graph, each condition shows its sessions with IOA out of all its sessions; a condition below 20%, or with none, is flagged. The usual convention is IOA on 20 to 33% of sessions; for highly trained clinic observers 10% did as well as 30%, but the authors warn that schools and homes may differ (Hausman et al., 2022). Each IOA session keeps the second observer’s name or role, and agreement is shown for each pairing of observers. Possible drift is flagged when a condition’s mean agreement is 10 or more points below the first condition’s, or when one pairing agrees 10 or more points above the others: observers who work together can keep agreeing with each other while both drift from the definition (Kazdin, 1977), and observers scored differently when they knew who was checking them (Romanczyk et al., 1973).

Observers checked against a scored example. Agreement shows that two observers scored alike, not that either scored correctly. Each observer is checked against a scored example (a scored video, or this form’s simulation) before their data count, by this form’s default to 90% or more; video training worked as well as live training in fewer sessions (Dempsey et al., 2012). Repeat the check every 4 to 6 weeks, and after any agreement flag. Give observers feedback on accuracy against the example, not on whether the data look as hoped: brief feedback and consequences for what observers scored biased their scoring, and a clearer definition only partly protected against it (Lerman et al., 2010). Tick when the second observer did not know the phase or what change was expected.

An observer who is also teaching. Tick when the observer was also running the class or session; the graph marks those sessions with a T. When teachers observed students, their prompts to those students rose, and those students changed more (Hay et al., 1977). An outside observer for some sessions shows whether the teaching observer’s sessions differ.

Writing help

A multi-line text box on this form shows an Improve wording button at its corner when you are in it and whenever it holds text; from a keyboard, Alt+Enter in the box (Option+Return on a Mac or iPad) opens the same panel. Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. Rewrite with Claude works only in the forms opened from the website: a single-use passcode from your BCBA unlocks the tab (in the workstation, every form in that tab) until the session’s time is up or the tab is closed; press Lock when you finish on a shared iPad. Before anything is sent, the student’s name and ID become placeholders such as [Student], other names can be added under Also hide, and the panel shows exactly what will be sent; the names are put back in the answer. Use it for real cases only with your district’s or agency’s approval. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print.

About the simulation
