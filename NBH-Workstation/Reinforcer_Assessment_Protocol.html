Reinforcer Assessment Protocol

Tests whether stimuli identified by preference assessment (Form PA-1) actually function as reinforcers, and how strong they are, using single-operant, concurrent-operants, and progressive-ratio arrangements. Each sheet scores its own design; the summary combines them into a reinforcer verdict and schedule recommendation.

Selection Guide
Why a reinforcer assessment after a preference assessment A preference assessment ranks stimuli; a reinforcer assessment shows whether delivering a stimulus contingent on a response increases or maintains that response. The two usually agree at the top of the hierarchy (Fisher et al., 1992; Piazza et al., 1996), but the agreement depends on the arrangement: under concurrent operants only the more preferred stimulus appears to reinforce, while under a single-operant test the less preferred stimulus often reinforces too (Roscoe, Iwata, & Kahng, 1999). Reinforcer effects also change with the response requirement, so a stimulus that maintains responding on FR 1 may fail at the ratio the plan will actually use (Roane, Lerman, & Vorndran, 2001). Test the stimulus under the conditions of the plan.

Decision Rules

1. What question does the plan need answered?
"Will this stimulus reinforce at all?" (absolute effect) → single-operant test: the response with the stimulus contingent versus the same response with no programmed consequence. "Which of these stimuli should the plan use?" (relative effect) → concurrent-operants test: two or more identical responses, each producing a different stimulus, plus a control option. "How much work will this stimulus support?" (potency) → progressive-ratio test: the ratio grows within the session until responding stops; the break point is the measure.
2. Is the student's preference hierarchy flat, or do several stimuli tie near the top?
Run a progressive-ratio comparison. Stimuli chosen equally often in a paired-stimulus assessment can separate as the ratio increases: for all 4 participants in Roane et al. (2001), one of two such stimuli supported more responding as the requirement increased. Where paired-choice ranks differ, higher-ranked stimuli produced larger break points in 10 of 12 comparisons (DeLeon et al., 2009).
3. Will the plan use a thin schedule, a token exchange, or a long task before reinforcement?
Test at the terminal ratio or with the same delay and magnitude the plan will use. Magnitude effects may depend on the requirement: differences between magnitudes did not appear at the lowest ratio requirements in three of four evaluations (Trosclair-Lasserre et al., 2008). A single-operant FR 1 test is not evidence for an FR 10 plan.
4. Did the preference assessment happen more than a few days ago, or is the stimulus an edible?
Run a brief MSWO (Form PA-1) immediately before each reinforcer assessment session. When a daily brief MSWO and an earlier paired-choice assessment disagreed, participants generally allocated more responses to the daily top-ranked item under a concurrent schedule (DeLeon et al., 2001); under progressive-ratio schedules, a single paired-stimulus assessment and daily MSWOs predicted reinforcer efficacy equally well, and the paired-stimulus assessment more consistently identified the most effective reinforcer (Call et al., 2012).
5. Are you testing a social stimulus, attention, or escape?
Deliver the interaction itself as the consequence and use a response that does not already produce attention (a practice rule of this form). Kelly et al. (2014) assessed preference among photographed forms of attention, with a control card that produced nothing, and then tested the forms as reinforcers for mands. For escape as a reinforcer, the consequence is a break from the task and the control condition is the task with no break; document that arrangement in the setup.
6. Is a stimulus that the plan depends on losing effect?
Re-run the progressive-ratio test and compare break points to the earlier ones. A falling break point with stable preference points to satiation or a magnitude problem; a falling preference rank points to a need to rotate stimuli (practice rules of this form). Hanley, Iwata, and Roscoe (2006) found preferences relatively stable over 3 to 6 months for 80% of participants, showed that extended daily access to a high-preference item could shift preference, and suggested varying reinforcers and assessing preference frequently.
7. When should I run the condition-comparison (COA) mode instead of stations?
Stations test which stimulus the student works for when the work is identical. A condition comparison (COA; Randall & Kranak, 2026) tests which contingency the student stays in when two are available at once, measured as the share of the session spent on each side. Use the COA mode when a functional analysis is not feasible in the setting (for example, district policy) or was undifferentiated after modifications such as a pairwise FA, extended sessions or additional reinforcers, and the question is which classes of reinforcer (attention, tangibles, escape from a demand) the student will allocate behavior toward; practitioners who can run an FA are encouraged to do that first (pp. 781–782). The prerequisite is that the student makes discriminated choices; diagnosis and verbal repertoire are not the inclusion criteria. If that is uncertain, run a pre-assessment first: no stimuli versus all stimuli on the two halves, or equal stimuli on both halves. A student who switches often between equal sides or allocates to the empty side may not yet have the repertoire (p. 782); the fallback is a free-operant arrangement with stimulus fading, 10 items then fewer and further apart. A COA does not show what maintains the target behavior; it identifies reinforcers for choice-making, which the replacement behavior can be reinforced with (p. 780). The tutorial assumes a socially mediated function; suspected automatic function calls for a competing-stimulus assessment first. It is not suitable when the student cannot scan, select and engage with two concurrently available conditions (p. 794).
8. How do I read a COA pairing?
A COA compares two test conditions, not a test against a control, so what a pairing can show depends on what the two conditions share and what only one of them has (p. 787). Classes present in both conditions at the same level are constant variables and allow no specific determination; a class present in one condition only is the test variable. Attention plus tangibles versus academic worksheets alone tests attention, tangibles and escape; the same attention plus tangibles versus high-preferred toys alone no longer tests escape. Tag demands as potentially aversive stimuli, not escape: escape is read from allocation away from that side (p. 786). The sheet derives the constant and test variables for each pairing and prints what allocation toward each side is evidence for. Weight later pairings more than earlier ones and read the cumulative pattern rather than any one pairing (p. 792); then confirm the chosen reinforcer in the single-operant or progressive-ratio sheet before it is written into the plan (p. 792; Randall et al., 2025, as cited in Randall & Kranak, 2026). Preferences shift over time, so replicate and re-check (Hanley et al., 2006).
9. What do I do if the student will not choose, or problem behavior occurs during a COA?
Problem behavior. Some pairings, such as two non-preferred demand conditions, can occasion problem behavior. Allow the student to avoid making either choice (for example, reinforce the problem behavior with escape), collect data on the time spent avoiding a choice and on the problem behavior, and redesign so that any evocative choice is presented with an option that has not produced problem behavior in the past; if problem behavior continues to occur frequently and the COA was chosen because an FA failed to evoke it, progress to an FA (pp. 789–790). Refusal to choose. Remove the instruction, or offer a third option in which no choice is required between the two primary options and keep that option for the rest of the assessment; if refusal persists even after teaching tolerance of choice-making, discontinue the COA (p. 790). Automatically maintained behavior that disrupts sessions. First identify stimuli that could mitigate it, for example with an augmented competing stimulus assessment (Frank-Crawford et al., 2023, as cited in Randall & Kranak, 2026).

Procedural Rules That Apply to Every Design

  • Response. Choose a free-operant response the student can already emit, that is easy to count, that produces nothing on its own, and that is effortful enough to stop when reinforcement is absent. Arbitrary responses (pressing a switch, placing a block in a bin, sorting cards) give the cleanest test; a target academic response gives the most useful one. Define it before the first session.
  • Consequence. Deliver the stimulus immediately (within 2–3 s) in a fixed magnitude: one edible piece, or a fixed number of seconds of access after which the item is removed. Do not add praise unless praise is the stimulus under test.
  • Control. Every design needs a no-consequence condition with the same response and materials. Without it, responding could reflect the materials rather than the stimulus.
  • Motivating operations. Restrict access to the tested stimuli for at least a few hours before sessions; run sessions at a consistent time; for edibles, not immediately after a meal. Record any pre-session access.
  • Pre-session exposure. Before the first reinforcement session with a stimulus, prompt the response once and deliver the stimulus so the contingency contacts behavior.
  • Measurement. Responses per minute for single-operant; responses at each option and each option’s share of them (allocation) for concurrent operants; break point for progressive ratio. Record problem behavior every session. Collect IOA on at least one session per condition when the result will be written into a plan.
  • Decision. Judge single-operant and concurrent-operants outcomes by visual analysis of level, trend, and overlap across replicated phases; the summary sheet computes ratios to support that judgment, not to replace it.
  • Ending the assessment. This form sets no rule for when an assessment is complete. The behavior analyst decides from the stability of responding across sessions in each design. The minimums on the sheets (at least 3 sessions per condition for a Clear single-operant effect; a control and 2–3 sessions per stimulus for progressive ratio) are floors, not stopping rules, and the stop rule for problem behavior, the stop interval and the session cap end a session, not the assessment.

Running Sessions at the Table

  • The runners. The Single operant, Concurrent operants, and Progressive ratio sheets each open with a Run This Session panel. It uses the sheet's own parameters: the session length from the toolbar; the progression, starting ratio, and stop interval from the toolbar; the progressive-ratio session cap and each stimulus's magnitude from Setup. It times the session, cues each reinforcer and times its access, and when you press Save to the sheet it writes the session into the next empty row, so the sheet, its graph, and the summary update. The runner is for the person collecting data and does not print.
  • Before you start. Check the condition and the Access (s) the runner has read from Setup, from the stimulus's magnitude or, failing that, from Consequence magnitude and timing. An edible, a token, or a portion with no stated time (one piece, a praise statement) gets 0 s: the runner cues the delivery and times nothing. A stimulus with no access time anywhere in Setup gets 20 s, and the panel says it is a default. The panel repeats the response definition and the MO control from Setup, and when a stimulus has no session on that sheet yet it reminds you to prompt the response once and deliver the stimulus first. If the next empty row already names a condition (or, on the concurrent-operants sheet, a position), the runner takes it as the plan for that session.
  • Access periods. When a reinforcer is due, the runner cues it; for a timed item it counts the access down and cues the end, when you remove the item. A response during an access period is counted, but it does not count toward the next reinforcer or ratio.
  • Single operant. Tap Response for every response. In a stimulus condition the runner counts toward the schedule (FR 1 unless you set another Schedule (FR) to test a plan schedule) and cues the reinforcer each time the requirement is met; in Control the response produces nothing. Minutes on the sheet writes the minutes run, or, when access time is subtracted, the minutes run less the access time. In a reversal design the runner ticks Phase start when the condition differs from the session before.
  • Concurrent operants. Set Option A position (the runner suggests the next position in the rotation), then prompt one response at each station and deliver its consequence before pressing Start session; those responses are not counted. Tap the button for the station the student responds at (keys 1, 2, 3, in the order of the sheet's columns). Each stimulus option runs its own schedule and access time; the control option produces nothing. The summary also shows changeovers for reference; the sheet has no column for them.
  • Condition comparison (COA). With Arrangement on the Concurrent operants sheet set to Condition comparison, the sheet opens a different runner. Choose the pairing and the side of Condition 1 (the runner suggests the side opposite the pairing’s last session), place the student at the neutral spot, give the contingency overview, then press Start session and tap Side 1, Neither or Side 2 as the student moves (keys 1, 0, 2); each is a stopwatch and only one runs at a time. Refusing to choose (R) is a sub-timer under Neither, Problem behavior (P) counts instances, and Prompt to choose (C) logs each prompt with its time. The session ends at the toolbar length or with End session, and Save to the sheet writes the durations, problem behavior, prompts and the side of Condition 1 to the next empty row of the session grid.
  • Progressive ratio. The runner shows the ratio in effect and the responses toward it. When the ratio is met it cues the reinforcer, times the access, and moves to the next ratio in the progression. The stop-interval clock restarts with each response and at the end of each access period, and it waits while an item is being accessed; the session ends when the stop interval runs out or at the session cap. Save to the sheet ticks the completed ratios, and the sheet computes the break point, responses, and ratios completed from the ticks. In a control session the same progression runs and completing a ratio produces nothing. If Setup has no session cap, the runner uses 20 min and enters it in Setup when you save, so the printed parameters show the cap that was used.
  • Problem behavior. Tap Problem behavior for each instance. The sheet records whether problem behavior occurred in the session; the runner shows Setup's stop rule, and End session ends the session early when the rule applies.
  • Mistakes and interruptions. Undo removes the last tap and cancels the access period that tap started; once that access period is over, the tap can no longer be undone in the runner, so correct the row on the sheet after saving. Pause stops the clock, the stop interval, and any access time; Resume goes on. Discard throws the session away without writing anything. One session runs at a time; on the other views a pill in the corner shows it and returns to it.
  • Alerts and keys. A rising tone, an amber flash around the screen edge, and a vibration on a phone or tablet mean deliver the reinforcer now; a falling tone means access is over; a longer tone and a green flash mean the session is over; a soft tone marks a completed ratio in a progressive-ratio control session. Alerts on, Sound, Flash, and Vibrate turn them on or off, and Test alert plays one. Keys while a session runs: Space or R for a response (1, 2, 3 on the concurrent-operants sheet), P for problem behavior, Z to undo. Keep the form in front during a session; the screen is kept awake where the browser allows it.
Decision rules synthesize Fisher et al. (1992), Piazza et al. (1996), Roscoe et al. (1999), Roane et al. (2001), and the cautions in Poling's (2010) commentary; the condition-comparison (COA) mode follows Randall and Kranak (2026). Full references are on the summary sheet.

"Ratio to control" is the stimulus mean divided by the control mean. "Overlap" is the share of stimulus sessions at or below the highest control rate. The verdict labels are working conventions for this practice: Clear = ratio ≥ 2 and no overlap across at least 3 sessions per condition; Probable = ratio ≥ 1.5 or overlap ≤ 25%; None otherwise. Single-operant arrangement after Roscoe, Iwata, and Kahng (1999, JABA, 32, 479–493), who found that 7 of 8 participants consistently preferred the high-preference stimulus under a concurrent schedule, yet for 6 of those 7 the low-preference stimulus, available alone, maintained response rates as high as those for the high-preference stimulus under the concurrent schedule; this arrangement answers the absolute question. The multielement comparison of several stimuli against a no-reinforcer control is this form's arrangement; Roane, Lerman, and Vorndran (2001) tested their stimuli under progressive-ratio schedules in a single-operant arrangement.

Verdict labels are working conventions, applied in this order: Preferred = mean allocation ≥ 60% and the most responses (or a tie for the most) in more than half the sessions; Comparable = not Preferred, and mean allocation within 20 percentage points of the highest mean allocation, so a leading option under 60% is Comparable; Not chosen = mean allocation < 20%; Lower = any other option: 20% or more of the responses, but more than 20 points below the leader. (1992, JABA, 25, 491–498) and Piazza, Fisher, Hagopian, Bowman, and Toole (1996, JABA, 29, 1–9), who used it to show that high-preference stimuli functioned as reinforcers relative to lower-preference ones. Potency labels are working conventions and are only meaningful within one set of parameters. Steps are counted along the sheet’s progression, with each mean placed at the first ratio at or above it. High = mean break point at least 3 steps above the control mean and, once a plan ratio is entered on the summary sheet, above the plan ratio; Moderate = at least 1 step above the control mean but not High; Low = less than 1 step above the control mean. The control carries no label, and a stimulus has none until it and the control each have a scored session. Responses counts the responses in the completed ratios; Ratios completed counts the ticked ratios: in a stimulus session each one produced a reinforcer, and a control session delivers none.

Progressive-ratio reinforcer assessment after Roane, Lerman, and Vorndran (2001, JABA, 34, 145–167), who found that, for all 4 participants, one of two stimuli chosen equally often in a paired-stimulus preference assessment supported more responding than the other as the ratio requirement increased, and after DeLeon, Frank, Gregory, and Allman (2009, JABA, 42, 729–733), who found that higher-preference stimuli from a paired-choice assessment produced larger break points in 10 of 12 comparisons. Penrod, Wallace, and Dyer (2008, JABA, 41, 177–188) found that high-preference stimuli maintained responding under higher schedule requirements than low-preference stimuli for 3 of 4 participants and were equally effective for the fourth; Trosclair-Lasserre et al. (2008, JABA, 41, 203–220) found that differences between reinforcer magnitudes did not appear at the lowest schedule requirements in three of four evaluations, suggesting that magnitude effects may be mediated by the schedule requirement. Poling (2010, JABA, 43, 347–349) notes that PR schedules are not an especially quick way to scale reinforcer potency and that long ratios are aversive, argues that members of protected populations should not be exposed to them unless there are clear offsetting benefits, and lists the influence of initial ratio and step size on applied findings among uninvestigated questions. In basic research a longer stop criterion increased the number of ratios completed (Stafford & Branch, 1998, JEAB, 70, 123–138), so parameters are fixed here and printed with every result.

S. Before the First Session

Setup · Stimuli Under Test · Designs Planned · the toolbar

Everything the sessions depend on is decided and written on Setup before the first one: the question the plan needs answered, the stimuli from PA-1 and their magnitudes, the response, the control condition, the motivating operations, the signal for each condition, and who does what.

    AssessorStudentData collectorSecond observerThe Run This Session panel, drawn largePlacemat: a condition’s signal (multielement)A label for the reader: what a station produces

    What You Say and Do

    • Write the question the plan needs answered, and tick the designs that answer it.
    • Carry the stimuli over from PA-1 in the exact form they will be delivered, each with its magnitude.
    • Define the response, the materials and their position, the consequence, the control condition, the MO control and, for a multielement design, the session signal, all before the first session.
    • Agree the roles: the assessor runs the session and delivers every consequence; the data collector runs Run This Session; a second observer records independently for IOA.

    What Goes on the Sheet

    • Setup: Question the plan needs answered, Preference assessment source, Response (operational definition), Materials and position, Consequence magnitude and timing, Control condition, MO control before sessions, Session signal (multielement only).
    • Stimuli Under Test: Stimulus (exact form delivered), Type, PA-1 rank / %, and Magnitude, portion, or access rule.
    • Designs Planned; Session cap for PR (min), Problem behavior to record, Stop rule for problem behavior.
    • The toolbar: Stimuli under test, Session length (min), and PR progression / start / stop (s).

    A. The Single-Operant Test

    Single operant · Run This Session · the Condition Summary

    The single-operant test answers the absolute question: does delivering the stimulus contingent on the response increase that response, compared with the same response when it produces nothing? The example uses the multielement design: the control and one stimulus per session, in a counterbalanced rotation, each with its own signal.

      What You Say and Do

      • Control: the response produces nothing; stay present and neutral.
      • Before the first reinforcement session with a stimulus: prompt the response once and deliver the stimulus.
      • Stimulus session: deliver the stimulus within 2–3 s of each response (or each nth, on a plan schedule), at the set magnitude, with no praise; remove a timed item when the runner cues the end of access.
      • Data collector: set Condition, check Schedule (FR), Access (s) and Minutes on the sheet, press Start session, and tap Response for every response (Space or R) and Problem behavior for each instance (P).

      What Goes on the Sheet

      • Save to the sheet writes Date, Condition, Responses, Minutes and PB into the next empty row; Rate / min is computed.
      • Minutes on the sheet: Minutes run, or Minutes run less access time when access time is subtracted from the session.
      • Reversal: Phase start on the first session of each phase; the runner ticks it when the condition changes.
      • The Condition Summary: Ratio to control, Overlap with control, Reinforcement effect.
      Decision. RA-1’s working conventions: Clear = ratio ≥ 2 and no overlap across at least 3 sessions per condition; Probable = ratio ≥ 1.5 or overlap ≤ 25%; None otherwise. Confirm by visual analysis of the plot, including level change at each phase reversal.

      The Sheet’s Procedure, Word for Word

      Single-Operant AssessmentEach session lasts the toolbar session length. In Control sessions the response produces nothing. In a stimulus session every response (or every nth, if testing a plan schedule) produces the stimulus at the set magnitude. Reversal: run control until stable, then one stimulus, then control, then the stimulus again. Multielement: alternate control and stimulus sessions in a counterbalanced order with a distinct signal for each. Record total responses and the minutes actually run; rate is computed. If access time is subtracted from the session, enter the minutes the response was available.

      B. The Concurrent-Operants Test

      Concurrent operants · Run This Session · the Allocation Summary

      The concurrent-operants test answers the relative question: which stimulus is chosen when both are available for equal work? The stations are identical and equally spaced; only what follows a response at each one differs.

        What You Say and Do

        • Before each session: one prompted response at every station, the control included, each followed by its consequence. The runner does not count these.
        • Then the session without prompts: each response produces its station’s consequence within 2–3 s; nothing follows at the control station.
        • Data collector: set Option A position, press Start session, and tap the button of the option the student responds at (keys 1 to 3).

        What Goes on the Sheet

        • Save to the sheet writes Date, Option A position, the responses at each option and PB.
        • Total and each option’s share are computed.
        • The Allocation Summary: Mean % allocation, Sessions with most responses, Position effect, Relative standing.
        Decision. RA-1’s working conventions, applied in this order: Preferred = mean allocation ≥ 60% and the most responses (or a tie for the most) in more than half the sessions; Comparable = not Preferred, and mean allocation within 20 percentage points of the highest mean allocation, so a leading option under 60% is Comparable; Not chosen = mean allocation < 20%; Lower = any other option: 20% or more of the responses, but more than 20 points below the leader. A stimulus that is “not chosen” here can still be a reinforcer; that is the single-operant question.

        A Second Example: the Condition-Comparison (COA) Mode

        Julia, from the tutorial

        • A 12-year-old whose school does not allow functional analyses (entry reason 1). Her desk is split with tape, a lettered colored placemat on each side; the teacher is the confederate; puzzles, Wikki Stix and coloring pages are the tangibles (Randall & Kranak, 2026).
        • Seven conditions: A nothing; B academic demands alone; C demands plus teacher assistance; D high-preferred tangibles, no attention; E tangibles plus teacher attention; F teacher attention alone; G low-preferred tangibles. Pairings in order: A vs B, A vs C, C vs D, D vs E, E vs F, D vs G.
        • First session, A vs B: about 40% of the 5 minutes in B and 60% in A, switching about halfway. The sides are swapped and the pairing is run again until the same majority holds for three consecutive sessions.

        What the Sheet Decides

        • A over B and A over C: evidence for escape from the demand. D over C: tangibles beat attention plus demands. E over D and E over F: tangibles plus attention preferred.
        • D vs G went back and forth, with no majority even after five sessions: the sheet labels the pairing No majority. The tutorial read this as possibly no discriminable difference and rotated the high- and low-preferred tangibles for variety.
        • The tutorial’s conclusion: escape sensitivity plus tangible (and attention) reinforcers to test in a differential-reinforcement arrangement for work completion. The COA identifies reinforcers for choice; it does not show the function of the disruptive behavior. Load the simulation and switch Arrangement to Condition comparison to see this record on the sheet.

        The Sheet’s Procedure, Word for Word

        Concurrent-Operants AssessmentSet out two or three identical response stations, equally spaced. Each station produces a different consequence: one stimulus each, and, when included, a control station that produces nothing. Before each session, prompt one response at every station and deliver its consequence so the student contacts each contingency. Then run the session without prompts and record responses at each station. Rotate station positions every session and record the position of Option A. Compare allocation across stations.

        C. The Progressive-Ratio Test

        Progressive ratio · Run This Session · the Break-Point Summary

        The progressive-ratio test answers how much work a stimulus will support. The ratio grows after each reinforcer until responding stops, and the break point, the last completed ratio, is the measure. The example uses the +2 progression from FR 1, a 120-s stop interval and a 20-minute cap.

          What You Say and Do

          • One stimulus per session, from the starting ratio; when the ratio is met, deliver it at the set magnitude, then the next ratio in the progression.
          • Control session: the same progression, and nothing when a ratio is met.
          • Data collector: set Condition, check Access (s), press Start session, and tap Response for every response. The runner counts toward the ratio, cues each reinforcer, times the access, and ends the session at the stop interval or the session cap.

          What Goes on the Sheet

          • Save to the sheet ticks the completed ratios; Break point, Responses and Ratios completed are computed from the ticks.
          • The ratio in progress when the session ended is not ticked and does not count; the runner’s Session Summary shows it, for reference.
          • PB when problem behavior occurred; the Parameters line prints the progression, start, stop interval and cap.
          Why. Break points can change with the stop interval, session cap and magnitude, and the number of ratios completed falls as step size grows, so results are only comparable within the same parameters, which are printed with every result. A single break point is unstable: 2–3 sessions per stimulus, and a control.

          The Sheet’s Procedure, Word for Word

          Progressive-Ratio AssessmentOne stimulus per session. Begin at the starting ratio. When the ratio is met, deliver the stimulus at the set magnitude, then advance to the next ratio in the progression. Continue until no response occurs for the stop interval, or the session cap is reached. Tick each ratio as it is completed; the break point is the last completed ratio. A ratio still in progress when the session ends is not ticked and does not count. Run a control session (same progression, no consequence) and 2–3 sessions per stimulus in a counterbalanced order.

          D. Reading the Summary

          Break-Point Summary · Summary: Plan ratio / schedule, Reinforcer verdict, Recommendations for the Behavior Plan

          The summary sheet reads each stimulus across the three designs: the single-operant effect, the concurrent-operants standing and the progressive-ratio potency, against the plan’s ratio and the PA-1 rank, and turns them into a reinforcer verdict and the recommendations for the plan.

            What You Say and Do

            • Enter the plan ratio before reading potency; it applies the plan-ratio criterion.
            • Read each stimulus across its row: SO verdict, CO standing, PR potency, then the reinforcer verdict.
            • Compare the verdicts with the PA-1 rank or percentage, and note where they part.

            What Goes on the Sheet

            • Plan ratio / schedule.
            • Reinforcer(s) selected and why; Schedule and magnitude supported by the data; Reinforcers to rotate or reserve; Conditions not tested (delay, task type, setting, staff); Re-test trigger.
            • Interobserver agreement (design, sessions, %); Problem behavior across sessions (summary); the signatures.
            Decision. The reinforcer verdict is a working convention, applied in this order: Confirmed when the single-operant verdict is Clear, or the progressive-ratio potency is High, or the potency is Moderate and no plan ratio is entered, or the single-operant verdict is Probable and the potency Moderate; Relative only when the stimulus is Preferred or Comparable under concurrent operants, its single-operant verdict is not None and its potency is not Low; Probable when the single-operant verdict is Probable or the potency is Moderate; Not supported when the single-operant verdict is None or the potency is Low; Untested when no design has a result for the stimulus, or its only result is a concurrent-operants standing of Lower or Not chosen, which cannot show that a stimulus is not a reinforcer. The single-operant route does not check the plan ratio: a Clear effect is evidence at the schedule tested (Guide, rule 3).

            E. Stopping a Session, and Testing Again

            Stop rule for problem behavior · the session cap · MO control · Re-test trigger

            When a session ends before its time, when an assessment has enough sessions to be read, and when a stimulus is tested again.

              What You Say and Do

              • End the session when the written stop rule is met: Problem behavior, then End session in the runner.
              • Record any pre-session access, and do not test an edible immediately after a meal.
              • Run a brief MSWO (Form PA-1) before each session when the preference assessment is more than a few days old or the stimulus is an edible.
              • Decide when the assessment is complete from the stability of responding across sessions: RA-1 sets no stopping rule, and its minimums are floors.

              What Goes on the Sheet

              • PB for any session with problem behavior.
              • MO control before sessions, with any pre-session access recorded.
              • Re-test trigger, on the summary.
              Decision. When a stimulus the plan depends on is losing effect, re-run the progressive-ratio test and compare break points to the earlier ones. RA-1’s practice rules: a falling break point with stable preference points to satiation or a magnitude problem; a falling preference rank points to a need to rotate stimuli.

              Terms, as the Form Uses Them

              For the assessor, the data collector and the analyst
              Reinforcer assessment
              Shows whether delivering a stimulus contingent on a response increases or maintains that response. A preference assessment (Form PA-1) ranks stimuli; this form tests them as reinforcers.
              Absolute and relative effects
              The absolute question, “Will this stimulus reinforce at all?”, is tested with a single operant. The relative question, “Which of these stimuli should the plan use?”, is tested with concurrent operants.
              Potency
              How much work a stimulus will support, measured by the break point under a progressive ratio.
              The response
              A free-operant response the student can already emit, easy to count, producing nothing on its own, and effortful enough to stop when reinforcement is absent; the same in every condition.
              Magnitude
              The fixed amount delivered: one edible piece, or a fixed number of seconds of access after which the item is removed. Written for each stimulus in Magnitude, portion, or access rule.
              Control condition
              The same response and materials with no consequence. Every design has one; without it, responding could reflect the materials rather than the stimulus.
              Pre-session exposure
              Before the first reinforcement session with a stimulus, the response is prompted once and the stimulus delivered. Under concurrent operants, one prompted response at every station, before each session.
              MO control
              Access to the tested stimuli restricted for at least a few hours before sessions, sessions at a consistent time, edibles not immediately after a meal, and any pre-session access recorded.
              Reversal (A-B-A-B) and multielement
              Two single-operant designs. Reversal: control until stable, then one stimulus, control, the stimulus again, with Phase start at each change. Multielement: control and stimulus sessions alternating in a counterbalanced order, each with its own Session signal.
              Rate / min and Minutes
              Responses divided by Minutes: the minutes actually run, or, when access time is subtracted from the session, the minutes the response was available. The runner’s Minutes on the sheet picks Minutes run or Minutes run less access time.
              Ratio to control and Overlap with control
              A stimulus’s mean rate divided by the control’s; and the share of its sessions at or below the highest control rate.
              Reinforcement effect
              Clear = ratio ≥ 2 and no overlap across at least 3 sessions per condition; Probable = ratio ≥ 1.5 or overlap ≤ 25%; None otherwise. Working conventions, confirmed by visual analysis of the plot.
              Station and Option A position
              Identical response stations, one per option, equally spaced. The positions rotate every session, and the sheet records where Option A stood: L, M or R.
              Mean % allocation
              An option’s share of each session’s responses, averaged across sessions. Sessions with most responses counts the sessions in which it led.
              Position effect
              Computed for Option A, whose position the sheet records: Suspected when its mean allocation differs by more than 40 points between positions; n/a for the other options.
              Relative standing
              Applied in this order: Preferred = mean allocation ≥ 60% and the most responses (or a tie for the most) in more than half the sessions; Comparable = not Preferred, and within 20 percentage points of the highest mean allocation; Not chosen = under 20%; Lower = any other option, 20% or more but more than 20 points below the leader.
              Progression and starting ratio
              The step by which the ratio grows after each reinforcer: +1, +2, +5, ×2, or 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 …; each session begins at the starting ratio. Set on the toolbar.
              Stop interval
              The seconds without a response that end a progressive-ratio session: 120 in the example. The runner restarts it with each response and at the end of each access period, and holds it during access.
              Session cap
              The longest a progressive-ratio session may run: 20 minutes in the example, set in Session cap for PR (min).
              Break point
              The last completed ratio. A ratio still in progress when the session ends is not ticked and does not count.
              Responses and Ratios completed
              Responses counts the responses in the completed ratios; Ratios completed counts the ticked ratios: in a stimulus session each one produced a reinforcer, and a control session delivers none.
              Potency labels and the plan ratio
              Steps are counted along the sheet’s progression, with each mean placed at the first ratio at or above it. High = mean break point at least 3 steps above the control mean and, once a plan ratio is entered on the summary sheet, above the plan ratio; Moderate = at least 1 step above the control mean but not High; Low = less than 1 step above the control mean. The plan ratio is typed as a number or as FR n.
              Reinforcer verdict
              Confirmed, Relative only, Probable, Not supported or Untested, tested in that order as the summary defines them. Untested when no design has a result for the stimulus, or its only result is a concurrent-operants standing of Lower or Not chosen.
              Run This Session
              The runner at the top of each assessment sheet: it times the session, counts responses, cues each reinforcer and times its access, shows a Session Summary, and with Save to the sheet writes the next empty row.
              IOA
              Agreement between the data collector’s record and a second observer’s independent record, on at least one session per condition when the result will be written into a plan.
              Re-test trigger
              The condition, written on the summary, under which the team tests the stimulus again: for example, a falling response rate, or each new schedule step.
