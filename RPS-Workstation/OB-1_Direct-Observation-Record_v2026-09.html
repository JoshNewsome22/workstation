Comparison Peer

A behavior is discrepant only against the room it happens in. Where a comparison peer is used, pick a same-sex classmate of about the same age whom the teacher considers typical for that setting, and score the peer on the same definition, at the same moments, in the same activity. Record no name: the peer is not the subject of this record and has no consent on file for it.

Basis for the choice
Direct observation is the part of a functional behavior assessment that is not filtered through someone's recollection. Indirect (verbal-report) measures have repeatedly been shown to have poor reliability or validity, which is why the observation carries the weight: on the Functional Analysis Screening Tool, pairs of informants agreed on the outcome in 64.8% of cases, and the outcome matched a functional analysis in 44 of 69 (63.8%; Iwata et al., 2013). What an observation can establish is that the behavior occurs, at what level, and under which conditions; only an experimental analysis establishes what maintains it (Iwata & Dozier, 2008).

What Followed the Behavior

How These Statements Are MadeThey are working conventions of this form. Entries the figures cannot use come first. A condition is named as present in every observation once the behavior occurred in at least two observations and the condition was ticked on each of them. Demands withdrawn is named when it was ticked on at least 60% of the observations in which the behavior occurred, and on at least two; removal is named whenever it was ticked. The peer comparison pools each row of the interval sample over every observation: discrepant when the student’s percentage is at least twice the peer’s, not clearly discrepant when it is less than one and a half times the peer’s. Nothing is said between the two, or when no peer interval is marked, because the form cannot tell a peer at 0% from a peer who was not scored. Agreement below 80% is named for each observation.

Statement for the FBA Summary (Form FS-1)

Carried to FS-1

The draft is written only when you press the button, and it asks before replacing anything written here. It names the conditions and consequences ticked on at least 60% of the observations in which the behavior occurred, and on at least two.

A scatter of conditions is a description of where the behavior was seen, not of what maintains it. Kahng et al. (1998) found that visual inspection of 15 scatter plots showed no reliable temporal pattern in any of them, while statistical control charts of the same data showed one or more half-hour periods in which problem behavior was more likely in 12, so treat a flat profile as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no pattern, and test any pattern against ABC data (Form ABC-1) or a functional analysis (Form EA-1) before it reaches the behavior plan.

Guide: Running a Direct Observation

1. Before the First Observation

EvidenceAn observation scores a definition, not an impression. Two observers scoring the same definition at the same time show whether the record is consistent, which is not the same as accurate. Agreement of 80% has long been used as the minimum acceptable level, and low agreement can reflect an unclear definition or recording system, observers not yet trained to it, or observer drift (Cooper, Heron, & Heward, 2020).
Applied guidanceBring the definition from Form TB-1 and read it once before starting. Agree with the teacher beforehand where you will sit, that you will not respond to the student, and what you will do if the student approaches you. Tell the teacher you are recording the setting as well as the student.

2. How Many, and When

Applied guidanceOne observation describes one period. Behavior that is maintained by escape from demands may not appear in a preferred activity, and a record built from a single observation of one activity cannot distinguish a setting effect from a low rate. Observe across at least three occasions, covering the activity the referral names and at least one contrasting activity, at different times of day. Where the referral concerns a specific routine, observe that routine more than once. Note whether the observation was announced: a first announced observation is usual, and an unannounced one afterwards is what tells you whether the announced one was typical.

3. The Comparison Peer

Applied guidanceA peer comparison answers a question the student's own data cannot: whether this level of behavior is unusual in this room. It is a screening comparison, not a norm. Record the peer's data under the same definition and the same intervals, keep the peer anonymous, and do not carry peer data into the student's plan as a target.

4. What the Record Can and Cannot Say

EvidenceDescriptive observation identifies events that co-occur with the behavior, and co-occurrence is not function: attention that follows the behavior may be what maintains it, or may simply be how adults respond to problem behavior whatever maintains it. Attention was the most common consequence for 8 of 12 participants in Thompson and Iwata (2007) but maintained the behavior for only 2 of them, and in Lerman and Iwata (1993) descriptive analysis was useful in showing whether self-injury was related to social or nonsocial contingencies but limited in telling attention from escape.
Applied guidanceWrite conditions as observed, and hypotheses as hypotheses. Where the same condition precedes most occurrences, carry it to Form ABC-1 for conditional probabilities, and to Form EA-1 if it is worth testing directly.

5. Recording Conventions

  • Write what you saw. "Pushed the worksheet away and put his head down" is an observation; "refused" and "was frustrated" are conclusions.
  • Time-stamp the narrative. A narrative without times cannot be lined up against the interval data or against anyone else's record.
  • Record the setting, not only the student. Group size, staffing, what was being asked, and what was available are what make the observation interpretable later.
  • Say what was not seen. An observation in which the behavior did not occur is data, and it belongs in the record with the rest.
  • Do not intervene. Where safety requires it, stop the observation, say so on the sheet, and do not score that period.
  • Score the interval sample by its rule. Partial interval: mark an interval if the behavior occurred at any time during it. Whole interval: mark it only if the behavior lasted the whole interval. Momentary time sampling: mark it if the behavior is occurring at the moment the interval ends. The sample covers only its own intervals, while the count covers the whole observation; note in the narrative the time the sample starts, so the two can be lined up.

6. The Live Recorder

  • Before You Start. The recorder sits above the sheets on the Observations view and does not print. A planned length ends the observation with an alert. Switch Comparison peer off when no peer is observed, so its count stays empty rather than 0.
  • Recording. Tap Student or Peer for every occurrence; paused time stays out of the minutes. Start sample runs the toolbar’s sample and alerts at the end of each interval not yet scored. Partial interval: + as soon as the behavior occurs, − at the end. Whole interval: − as soon as the behavior is not occurring, + at the end only if it lasted throughout. Momentary time sampling: what each person is doing when the interval ends.
  • Saving. Save to the record types the date, times, minutes observed, counts and marks into the next empty observation or the one chosen, and asks before replacing anything; the sample’s start goes into the narrative, pauses and unscored intervals into the notes. The Minutes box follows the times until minutes are typed. Discard asks first and writes nothing.
  • Alerts and Keys. Tone, flash and vibration can each be turned off. The keys are on the buttons: 1 and Shift+1 count, 2 and 3 (Shift for the peer) score + and −, 4 scores − for both, Space pauses, Z, Shift+Z and X undo.
