Plans, scores, and cross-checks informant-report instruments (FAST, QABF, MAS, PBQ) and structured interviews (FAI, FACTS, open-ended) for one target behavior. Each worksheet computes category totals, informant agreement, and differentiation; the convergence sheet combines every informant and instrument into one hypothesis and routes it to descriptive assessment (Forms ABC-1, SP-1) or functional analysis. Guide: What Indirect Assessments Can and Cannot Tell You

Indirect assessments are informant reports: rating scales and interviews about the conditions under which a behavior occurs. They structure the interview and generate hypotheses. They do not identify function; a functional analysis does. Direct observation adds correlations that help decide what the analysis tests. Evidence boxes report what the cited studies found; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses. Full references are on the convergence sheet.

1. Reliability: Do Two Informants Give the Same Answers?

EvidenceIwata, DeLeon, and Roscoe (2013) had two informants complete the FAST independently for each of 196 behaviors (151 individuals): mean item-by-item agreement was 71.5% (range 28.6% to 100%), agreement on the highest-scoring category was 64.8%, and antecedent items were more reliable (M = 78.9%) than consequence items (M = 67.7%). Their comparison table of published item-level agreement shows the MAS at 41% to 63% across four studies (Zarcone et al., 1991; Sigafoos, Kerr, & Roberts, 1994; Conroy et al., 1996; Duker & Sigafoos, 1998) and the QABF at a median of 78% (Nicholson, Konstantinidi, & Furniss, 2006). Zarcone et al. (1991) failed to replicate the interrater correlations of .66 to .92 reported by Durand and Crimmins (1988). Shogren and Rojahn (2003) found interrater coefficients of .46 to .60 for QABF subscales and .35 to .73 for MAS subscales. In an informal analysis of several rating scales, Iwata et al. (2013) also found item agreement inversely correlated with the number of response options, one of their two reasons for the FAST’s yes/no format. Dracobly, Dozier, Briggs, and Juanico (2018) found FAST agreement higher among behavior-analyst experts (M = 83.3%) than caregivers (M = 71.5%). McIntosh et al. (2008) summarized 10 studies of the FACTS interview; in the test–retest data they reported (13 informants, interviewed twice 5 to 7 days apart), agreement was .62 for setting events, .77 for antecedents and .92 for function, and agreement on maintaining consequence across 5 to 8 school staff informants ranged from .50 to .88.
Applied guidanceNever rely on one informant. Administer each instrument independently to at least three people who see the behavior daily, in the routines where it occurs, and record agreement on this form the way Iwata et al. (2013) did: item by item, and on the highest category. Treat item-level agreement below 80% as a reason to interview the informants about the items on which they disagreed, not as a reason to average their scores. Choose informants by contact: in the test–retest data McIntosh et al. (2008) reported, the responses that changed came from informants whose mean contact with the student in the routine assessed was under an hour per week.

2. Validity: Does the Outcome Match a Functional Analysis?

EvidenceIwata et al. (2013) compared FAST outcomes with 69 functional analyses: overall correspondence was 63.8% (social-positive 77.8%, social-negative 56.0%, automatic-positive 61.5%). When both informants agreed on the FAST category, correspondence rose to 70.8%, driven by 100% correspondence for social-positive reinforcement; social-negative correspondence stayed at 54.6% even with informant agreement. In most matching cases the winning category led by a single yes answer. Paclawskyj, Matson, Rush, Smalls, and Vollmer (2001) found the QABF and MAS agreed more with each other than either did with a functional analysis (QABF 56.3%, MAS 43.8%; 69.2% and 53.8% once the 3 participants with undifferentiated analyses were excluded). Smith, Smith, Dracobly, and Pace (2012) obtained agreement among at least 4 of 5 respondents for 52% of target behaviors on the MAS and 57% on the QABF; where such agreement existed, functional-analysis results corresponded in 6 of 7 QABF cases and 4 of 7 MAS cases. Matson, Bamburg, Cherry, and Paclawskyj (1999) reported that treatments matched to QABF outcomes produced larger reductions than a standard package. Hall (2005) found QABF outcomes matched functional analyses in 3 of 4 cases, versus 1 of 4 for descriptive assessment. Reviews by Sturmey (1994), Floyd, Phaneuf, and Wilczynski (2005), and Kelley, LaRue, Roane, and Gadaire (2011) concluded that psychometric support for indirect methods remains limited.
Applied guidanceAn indirect outcome is a hypothesis with a documented error rate of about one in three for the FAST (Iwata et al., 2013) and higher for the QABF and MAS (Paclawskyj et al., 2001). Verify it. The order this practice uses: (1) indirect assessment with multiple informants (this form); (2) descriptive assessment in the routines the informants named (Forms ABC-1 and SP-1); (3) functional analysis when the plan will change reinforcement contingencies, when informants disagree, or when the behavior is dangerous. When informants strongly agree on social-positive reinforcement, a single-function test with a matched control (Iwata & Dozier, 2008) may suffice to verify; when they agree on escape, keep the full multielement analysis, because agreement did not improve accuracy for that category.

3. Reading the Profile

EvidenceIwata et al. (2013) noted that, in most cases where FAST and FA outcomes corresponded, the FAST conclusion was decided by a one-question difference, and that the automatic-negative (pain-attenuation) category rests on inferences from nonhuman work or correlational data rather than experimental data with clinical populations; O'Reilly (1997) showed self-injury that was sensitive to attention and demands only while otitis media was present. Querim et al. (2013) found stereotypy is most often maintained by automatic reinforcement and Marcus et al. (2001) identified operant functions (positive or negative reinforcement) for the aggression of 7 of 8 children and adolescents. Beavers and Iwata (2011) found that 77 of the 88 cases of multiple control they identified in JABA involved collapsed response topographies and suggested that apparent multiple control often may be a by-product of that aggregation. Thompson and Borrero (2011) noted that even accurate descriptive correlations are weak indicators of function.
Applied guidanceScore one operationally defined behavior (Form TB-1) per administration; do not pool topographies. Report the margin between the highest and second-highest category with every outcome. A tie, a margin of one FAST item, a Likert margin under 10% of the subscale maximum (1.5 on the QABF, 2.4 on the MAS, 1.8 on the PBQ), or a top score below one-third of the maximum is recorded as weak differentiation and treated as no outcome. A high physical or pain-attenuation profile triggers a medical or nursing referral before any behavioral conclusion. A high PBQ setting-events column is not a function; it identifies conditions to record on the ABC and scatter-plot sheets. Use the topography prior as a check, not a conclusion: a social outcome for stereotypy or an automatic outcome for aggression is unusual and should be verified before it drives a plan.

4. Administration and the Verification Record

EvidenceIn Iwata et al. (2013), informants were parents, relatives, teachers, aides, and direct-care staff; pairs completed the FAST independently on the same day or within 2 to 3 days, typically in 15 to 20 minutes. The authors listed what an accurate answer requires: having observed most of the events asked about, sorting them into the scale's categories, estimating their conditional probabilities, and recalling all of it at once; they contrasted this with the simpler task of a direct observer. They scored FAST–FA correspondence for each pair as complete (1), partial (.5), or none (0), and they proposed using the FAST's open-ended first section to check the item answers for inconsistencies.
Applied guidanceAdminister within 3 days across informants and record training and contact on the setup sheet. Enter the open-ended FAST answers and any inconsistencies on the FAST sheet before interpreting totals. After the functional analysis, enter its outcome on the convergence sheet: the form scores each instrument's correspondence as the share of its informant outcomes that match (1, .5 or 0 with two informants, as Iwata et al. scored pairs) so the practice accumulates its own validity record instead of relying on the published rates alone.

Enter each informant's answer by item number: Y, N, or NA. Items 1 to 4 score social-positive reinforcement (attention or preferred items), 5 to 8 social-negative reinforcement (escape), 9 to 12 automatic reinforcement (sensory stimulation), and 13 to 16 automatic reinforcement (pain attenuation). The category total is the count of yes answers; the outcome is the highest total (Iwata et al., 2013). Items answered NA by either informant are excluded from that pair's agreement, as in the original reliability analysis.

Structured Interviews: FACTS, FAI, and Open-Ended

One card per interview. Record the routine analysis and the summary statement in the FACTS format (setting event; antecedent; behavior; maintaining consequence) and the informant's confidence rating. For an open-ended interview, record the idiosyncratic antecedents and consequences the informant described and the synthesized contingency they suggest; these feed the design of test conditions rather than a conclusion (Hanley, 2012). Set the number of interview cards in the toolbar.

FACTS after March et al. (2000) and McIntosh et al. (2008, JPBI, 10, 33–45); Functional Assessment Interview after O'Neill et al. (2015); open-ended interview after Hanley (2012, Behavior Analysis in Practice, 5, 54–72). Confidence rating (1 to 6) is the FACTS item; it records the informant's certainty, not the validity of the statement.

Functional Analysis Verification Record

Complete after the analysis. Each instrument’s correspondence is the share of its informant outcomes whose highest category matches the FA result: with two informants this is the 1, .5 or 0 that Iwata et al. (2013) scored for each pair of FASTs (complete, partial or no agreement), and with more informants it is the proportion that matched. A tie counts as a match if either highest category matched, as Iwata et al. scored ties, so outcomes flagged weak are included here; a profile with nothing endorsed matches nothing. A FAST social-positive outcome matches an FA outcome of attention or tangible, and interviews are grouped by the instrument named on their cards. The record is the local validity check for this practice's indirect assessments.
