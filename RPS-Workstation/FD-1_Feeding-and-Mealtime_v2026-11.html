Feeding and Mealtime

A student’s eating at school and at home, in four parts: a screen that records the doctor’s and the swallowing clearance, sorts the problem (refusal, or selectivity by type or by texture) and says who leads; a described meal, bite by bite; the foods the student eats now, the target foods and the preferred foods; a plan that starts from mealtime structure and a menu of procedures in which the student can always decline; and one bite-by-bite data sheet with its graph and the student’s weight. It reads the health record from Form DM-1 and sits beside Forms TB-1 (the feeding targets), TD-1 (the protocol cards), HD-1 (home data) and CT-1 (caregiver training).

Part 2. Meal Observation

Watch the student’s usual meal with the usual feeder, about 10 to 20 bites. Change nothing: this is a description of the meal as it happens.

  • Offered: the student took the bite from the plate, or an adult offered it in front of the student.
  • Accepted: the whole bite passes the lips within 5 s.
  • Mouth clean at 30 s: no food the size of a pea or larger in the mouth 30 s after the bite went in. The mouth check is a request (“Show me”); the student opens their own mouth. Staff never open it. A check the student does not do is recorded as not checked.
  • Packed, expelled, refusal, gag or cough: as defined on Form TB-1 (feeding targets). Refusal is any refusal response on TB-1’s “Feeding refusal” target (feed-refuse).
  • What the adult did within 10 s: coaxed or talked, reprimanded, removed the bite or ended the meal, gave a preferred food or toy, praised, or waited.

What followed refusal and acceptance

This observation describes what usually follows refusal; it does not test it. Form FD-1 does not build a mealtime functional analysis (one in which bites are removed, or attention is given, after refusal on purpose). Escape is the working hypothesis: refusal may be kept going because it ends or delays the bite or the meal.

Part 3. Plan

Stop rule (every feeding plan)

  • Stop the meal and tell the school nurse the same day if the student coughs or chokes, has a wet or gurgly voice, gags again and again, vomits, or shows signs of pain.
  • If the student cannot breathe, cough or speak, follow the choking procedure and call 911.

Do not delay, shorten or skip meals or snacks to build hunger.

              Rapid eating: pacing, in this order

                Escape extinction means a refused bite no longer ends: the adult keeps the bite at the lips until it is taken, puts spat-out food back, or guides the student’s hand to the mouth. School teams may run it only with written consent, planned with the family, the doctor and a feeding specialist. Never force the mouth open.

                What This Form Is For

                Feeding problems are common: estimates reach 90% in children with autism (Volkert & Vaz, 2010). The same “eats too little” can be different problems that need different plans (Munk & Repp, 1994), and some of them are medical first. This form holds, in one place, what a school or home team needs before it changes a meal: the clearance, the kind of problem, who leads, a described meal, the foods, a plan in which the student can always decline, and data that shows whether the food is swallowed as well as accepted.

                Part 1. Screen and Gate

                Red flagsWeight loss or flat growth; tube feeding or nutrition drinks; coughing, choking or a wet or gurgly voice during or after meals; gagging or vomiting at meals; signs of pain (reflux, constipation, dental pain); eating only purees or one texture; meals longer than 30 minutes; drinking too little; a history of pica. Each is answered yes, no or unknown.
                Problem typeMunk and Repp (1994) assessed food type and texture with 5 people with severe disabilities: the same limited intake broke into four problems (total refusal, selectivity by type, by texture, and by both). Food refusal here means eating too little to meet needs, or depending on a tube or nutrition drinks.
                Who leadsA red flag answered yes, food refusal, or selectivity by texture (alone or with type): “Medical feeding team leads. The school and home plan holds mealtime structure, data and caregiver support only, until the team advises.” When the team’s advice is recorded (who, and the date), the plan offers the rest of the menu and the restrictive option for the team to choose from. Selectivity by type, every red flag answered no, and the doctor’s clearance on file: “School or home plan may use the non-forcing menu (Part 3).” The doctor’s clearance missing (or the swallow evaluation, when a swallowing red flag or a texture problem is recorded): the form does not print a treatment page.
                WhyIn a review of antecedent interventions in this journal from 2000 to 2012, every case in which an antecedent intervention worked alone involved food selectivity (five of six children with selectivity); they were tried alone for only eight of 20 children with food refusal, and for refusal they enhanced escape extinction (67% of participants) rather than replacing it (Seubert et al., 2014). Lowering the texture cut packing and raised grams eaten for 3 children, and all gained weight (Patel et al., 2005): texture is a medical and skill question, so the texture, the liquid and the bite size come from the SLP or the feeding team, and a step goes up only when one of them writes it here.
                PicaA pica history links to Form TB-1’s pica target and the TD-1 pica cards. In a series of 33 consecutive cases, treatment reduced pica by at least 90% for 30 people, and the gains held with new implementers and settings for 26 of 29; the assessments were run by trained therapists in consultation with medical staff (Frank-Crawford et al., 2025).
                Sensory-based approachesIn direct comparisons, consumption rose with behavioral treatment and not with a modified sequential oral sensory approach (Peterson et al., 2016) or with sensory integration therapy (Addison et al., 2012). Both behavioral treatments included escape extinction in a clinic.

                Part 2. Meal Observation

                What it isA description of the usual meal with the usual feeder, bite by bite. For each adult response the form shows how often it followed refusal and how often it followed acceptance, with the meal minutes and the portion eaten. It describes; it does not test.
                Why escape is the working hypothesisIn parent-fed meals, attention and escape most often followed refusal (Borrero et al., 2010). Parents coaxed, reprimanded, allowed breaks and gave preferred foods or toys; when these were tested, they worsened behavior for 10 of 15 children (67%), and of the 10 with differentiated results, 90% were sensitive to escape (Piazza et al., 2003). Across a systematic review, escape alone or in part maintained inappropriate mealtime behavior in 92% of cases (Saini et al., 2019). The form notes attention when coaxing or a reprimand followed half or more of the refusals.
                The formatBites at set times (about every 30 s), the same bite size and prompt each time, and a mouth check: the structured mealtime assessment, which computer-based training taught to 14 undergraduate students and two registered behavior technicians (Van Arsdale et al., 2025).

                Part 3. Plan

                The menuMealtime structure first, then simultaneous presentation, differential reinforcement, a high-p sequence and progressive high-p with demand fading (for selectivity), and stimulus fading. Each item names its TD-1 cards, where the study and its full procedure are; the plan runs the steps printed here.
                The evidenceSimultaneous presentation raised consumption at once for 2 of 3 children with selectivity (Piazza et al., 2002). Condiments on refused vegetables raised acceptance with no escape prevention and no reinforcement (Ahearn, 2003). At a school, simultaneous presentation reduced packing (Buckley & Newchok, 2005). Simultaneous reinforcement worked faster than delayed reinforcement (Kern & Marder, 1996). Three preferred-food bites before a refused food raised acceptance, and were then faded (Meier et al., 2012). The progressive high-p sequence with demand fading increased eating without escape extinction for two boys aged 9 and 10 with autism (Penrod et al., 2012). Preschool teachers faded chocolate syrup out of milk at regular meals (Tiger & Hanley, 2006). Acceptance fell in an orderly way as the amount on the spoon rose from empty through dipped, quarter, half and level (Kerwin et al., 1995). Mothers trained to offer target foods regularly, with specific prompts and attention, raised their children’s acceptance at home (Werle et al., 1993).
                The review pointAcross treated cases, interventions without escape extinction reached clinical significance in 33% of cases, in a mean of 9.5 sessions; with it, in 49%, in a mean of 13.2 sessions (Scott et al., 2024). The plan is reviewed after the number of meals set on the Plan page (10 to 15): if acceptance of a target food has not risen, or refusal, gagging or distress has risen, the form prints “Stop and refer to a medical feeding team before adding escape extinction.”
                Escape extinction (RESTRICTIVE)Keeping the bite at the lips until it is taken, putting spat-out food back, or guiding the hand to the mouth means a refused bite no longer ends. It is a restrictive step: never the default, run by a school team only with written consent and a plan made with the family, the doctor and a feeding specialist, and printed only when all four are recorded. The mouth is never forced open. For food refusal, reinforcement alone often did not raise acceptance; escape extinction added to it did (Patel et al., 2002).
                A bite at the lipsA bite offered at the lips and removed calmly when the student does not take it within the wait is an ordinary way to offer a bite. Only keeping it there is escape extinction.
                Rapid eatingPutting utensils down between bites controlled bite rate at school lunch (Epstein et al., 1976). A vibrating pager taught in the classroom lengthened meals for 3 teenagers with autism (Anglesea et al., 2008), and fading the pager by its strength kept a safe pace where fading by frequency did not (Valentino et al., 2018). Prompting and reinforcement lowered bite rate for an adolescent with an esophageal stricture (Shore et al., 1999). Blocking a reach is a restrictive step on TD-1’s card and is not part of this pathway.
                Setting events and meal timingAcceptance was less likely to persist after disrupted sleep when escape extinction was not in place (Reed et al., 2005), so each meal row has three ticks: slept poorly, unwell, ate within the last hour, and the graph marks those meals. Meal schedule and quantity changed problem behavior (Wacker et al., 1996): meal timing is logged, never changed to build hunger.

                Part 4. Data

                The measures% of bites accepted; % mouth clean of accepted bites (bites not checked are left out and counted); % packed of accepted bites; expulsions per accepted bite; refusal per presentation; meal minutes; seconds per bite (meal minutes × 60 ÷ bites accepted); grams eaten when the plate is weighed before and after, with the family’s agreement; and the number of target foods eaten at criterion.
                CriterionEach bite is scored accepted or not, so mastery is 100%: a target food counts as eaten at criterion when, at its last 3 meals, every bite of it offered was accepted and every accepted bite that was checked was mouth clean at 30 s.
                Why mouth cleanAcceptance alone can mislead: mouth clean is the product measure of swallowing (Patel et al., 2002), and packing lowered intake while grams eaten tracked it (Patel et al., 2005).
                WeightEntered beside the graph from the nurse or the family’s doctor. A weight lower than at the weigh-in before prints “Tell the nurse and the family’s doctor.”

                About the simulation

                Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated student eats enough but a narrow range of foods (selectivity by type), has every red flag answered no and the doctor’s clearance on file, and is observed at one lunch. The plan uses mealtime structure, simultaneous presentation and differential reinforcement with two target foods; ten meals are entered, three before the plan and seven after, with three weigh-ins.
