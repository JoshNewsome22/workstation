What the Respondents Agree and Disagree About

Answers are laid side by side on the questions that bear on function. Agreement across people who see the student in different places is the useful signal; a disagreement is not noise to be averaged away but a finding to record and, where possible, test directly.

Why Interview, and What It Can and Cannot Give You

An interview serves five purposes (O'Neill et al., 2015):

  • Specify and confirm target behaviors.
  • Identify the specific conditions under which target behaviors are most likely and least likely to occur.
  • Identify potential antecedents, setting events, and lifestyle factors that are influencing the student's behavior.
  • Provide a broader perspective than that achieved by direct observation.
  • Provide an opportunity to establish rapport with school personnel and families.

Conduct interviews face to face with people who know the student well and interact with them across a range of settings and situations. Questions may need to be repeated and clarified for some respondents, and follow-up questions should be asked to encourage thoughtful, open communication.

What this is worth as evidence. Interviews are an indirect assessment. On the level-of-evidence scheme used in Form FS-1 they sit at tier 4: they license statements about what informants describe, not about what maintains the behavior. Agreement among four informants raises confidence that the informants agree; it does not raise the tier. Treat the hypothesis that comes out of this form as the thing to examine, not as the finding: direct observation on Forms ABC-1 and SP-1 adds correlations that help decide what a functional analysis on Form EA-1 tests. Open-ended interviews suggest more functions than they confirm: compared with a functional analysis, 64% of the contingencies the interview implicated were not relevant (Fisher, Greer, Romani, Zangrillo & Owen, 2016), and 46.9% of the functions it identified were false positives while only 5.6% of the true functions were missed (Greer, Mitteer, Briggs, Fisher & Sodawasser, 2020). Attention was the function named wrongly most often. So use the interview to add conditions to the analysis, never to drop one.

The Three Question Sets

Parent or guardianHome-focused, and opens by asking the parent to describe their child rather than the problem. Includes two Yes / No / Sometimes matrices: contributing health and lifestyle factors, and the request and routine conditions under which the behavior is more likely. Five questions added in this revision ask how the child communicates and asks for things, when the behavior started and how it has changed, whether it happens when the child is alone, how hard it is and how long it lasts, and why the parent thinks it happens.
Teacher, staff member, related service providerSchool-focused, covering the behavior, strengths and skill deficits, when and where it is most and least likely, triggers, precursors, what follows it, the respondent's own view of function, what has already been tried, and what works proactively. Added in this revision: how the student communicates and asks, what the student works for, the history, whether it happens when the student is alone, how hard and how long, and a Yes / No / Sometimes checklist of setting events on the school day.
StudentFirst person and strengths-first, asked in plain language. Preferences and dislikes, what is easy and hard, when things go best, what they want to work for, what happens around the behavior, what they notice beforehand, what helps them calm down, and, added in this revision, what they do when they need help or want a break. The student’s own interview for the report, with the assent plan and the student’s hypothesis, is Form SI-1: use this set when the district form is required, after assent is recorded there. Replace the words [target behavior] with the student's own word for it before asking.
Added to the parent and school sets (v25.64)Four questions from the open-ended interview: which behavior worries the person most and should be worked on first; what the student does just before it starts, and which behaviors happen together and in what order; what the adult does to calm the student or end an episode; and when it does not happen, even when it would be expected. Ask the second and third about the last time it happened, not about what usually happens. The priority answer goes to the case for Form TB-1’s candidate list, and the answers about what comes just before go as candidate precursors marked “reported, confirm by observation” (Forms TB-1 and ABC-1). Hanley, Jin, Vanselow and Hanratty (2014) asked “What is the single most concerning problem behavior?” in the interview that designed their analyses, and Miltenberger and Fuqua (1985) list setting the priority and asking under what conditions the problem does not occur among the interviewer’s own responses. Caregivers ranked precursors in the order that observation later confirmed (Herscovitch, Roscoe, Libby, Bourret & Ahearn, 2009), but a precursor is defined by what is measured to come just before the behavior, so the report is a lead, not a finding.
Interviewer checklistEach interview carries a short checklist, ticked by the interviewer or by a second person who listened: opened with an open question, asked for other problems, set the priority, asked how often, how long and how hard, asked about antecedents and when the behavior does not occur, asked about consequences, asked about goals, and the number of closed or yes / no questions asked. These are the interviewer responses Iwata, Wong, Riordan, Dorsey and Lau (1982) and Miltenberger and Fuqua (1985) defined and trained to 90% to 100%; in Iwata et al. (1982), better interviewer behavior brought more client responding. The checklist is saved with the interview and printed with it.

Respondent pages. When someone cannot be interviewed in person, Respondent pages (toolbar) makes a one-page copy of their question set, as a file to attach to an email or as a link when the workstation is on a website, that they answer in writing on any phone or computer. Short answers come back by email as a code, longer ones as a file the page saves, and Collect responses reads either into a new or chosen respondent's record and the Respondents table. The page shows the student as initials and ID only and sends nothing anywhere but the email the respondent chooses to send. A written answer is not an interview: follow up where you can. A collected response sets the respondent's format to Written (respondent page), and the page asks whether the respondent understands the behavior's definition; the answer is shown with the response and on the interview heading.

Writing help

A multi-line text box on this form shows an Improve wording button at its corner when you are in it and whenever it holds text; from a keyboard, Alt+Enter in the box (Option+Return on a Mac or iPad) opens the same panel. Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print. The answer boxes of the interview have no button: an answer is the respondent’s report, kept in the words they used, so it is not checked or rewritten. The boxes where you sum up (what the respondents agree and disagree about) have it; quote a respondent’s own words there, in quotation marks, which Check wording leaves alone.
