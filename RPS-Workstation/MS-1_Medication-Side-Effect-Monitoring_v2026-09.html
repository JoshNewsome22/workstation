Guide: Why and How School Teams Monitor Medication Effects

Psychotropic medication changes the same behavior the plan measures. Side effects can look like target behavior (restlessness, irritability, refusal), can mask improvement (sedation), and can be missed at brief clinic visits because the school day is where they occur. This form organizes what school staff can observe and reports it to the prescriber, who makes every decision about the medication. Evidence boxes report what the cited sources say; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses. Side-effect lists are drawn from the medication's FDA prescribing information and the drug information of MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic; they are reference lists, not predictions for any student.

1. The Behavior Analyst's Role

EvidenceValdovinos and Kennedy (2004) framed medication side effects behavior-analytically: drugs alter motivating operations, reinforcer value, and response effort, so the behavior analyst's data are often the most sensitive record of drug effects. Li and Poling (2018) surveyed BCBAs: a majority worked with individuals with autism who take at least one psychotropic medication, their training relevant to these drugs was inconsistent (many reported it inadequate), and they were not consistently involved in monitoring drug effects; the authors urged behavior analysts to develop strategies that foster collaboration with psychiatrists and other physicians. Kalachnik (1999) and Matson and Mahan (2010) described side-effect rating instruments and argued for routine monitoring of medicated individuals with intellectual disability, for whom self-report is often unavailable.
Applied guidanceSchool staff observe and record; the prescriber interprets and decides. Every observation is a described sign (tremor of the hands at rest, drowsy with head down, refused lunch), never a diagnosis. Monitoring starts before a new medication or dose change so there is a baseline, continues daily for the first weeks, and is reported to the prescriber on a fixed schedule and immediately for any red-flag item. A release of information signed by the parent or guardian is on file before any report is sent.

2. Antipsychotics: Extrapyramidal, Metabolic, and Endocrine Effects

EvidenceRisperidone and aripiprazole carry FDA indications for irritability associated with autism in children; in the RUPP Autism Network trial (2002), risperidone reduced irritability in most children, with weight gain averaging 2.7 kg over 8 weeks versus 0.8 kg on placebo. Correll et al. (2009) followed antipsychotic-naive youth for a median of 10.8 weeks and found weight gain with each of olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, and aripiprazole, the largest on olanzapine; lipids rose significantly with olanzapine and quetiapine (triglycerides also with risperidone), glucose and insulin with olanzapine, and the metabolic changes with aripiprazole were not significant. The American Diabetes Association, American Psychiatric Association, and allied groups (2004) recommended baseline weight (BMI), waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and lipids; weight again at 4, 8, and 12 weeks and then quarterly; blood pressure, glucose, and lipids at 12 weeks, then blood pressure and glucose annually and a normal lipid profile every 5 years; and, for children and adolescents, height with weight and a BMI percentile for age and sex. Extrapyramidal symptoms include acute dystonia (prolonged abnormal contractions of muscle groups, which may occur in the first few days of treatment), akathisia (subjective and observable restlessness, pacing, inability to sit still), parkinsonism (tremor, rigidity, slowed movement, reduced facial expression, drooling), and tardive dyskinesia (involuntary movements of the face, mouth, tongue, and limbs; the risk increases with the duration of treatment and the cumulative dose, and it can develop after relatively brief treatment, even at low doses); the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (Guy, 1976) is the standard screen, and Schooler and Kane (1982) proposed research criteria for tardive dyskinesia: at least 3 months of cumulative antipsychotic exposure, movements rated at least moderate in one body area or at least mild in two, and no other condition that could produce them. The AACAP practice parameter (2011) recommends AIMS screening at baseline and at least every 6 months for youth on antipsychotics. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (fever, severe rigidity, confusion, unstable vital signs) is rare and an emergency.
Applied guidanceFor any antipsychotic, check at least these items on the plan (the form checks none by default): weight and appetite; sedation; restlessness; tremor, stiffness, and drooling; and plan an AIMS-style screen at baseline, at 3 months, and every 6 months, recorded on the EPS sheet by staff trained in the scale. Aripiprazole plans include akathisia from day one; risperidone and paliperidone plans include prolactin-related signs (breast changes, discharge, menstrual change), reported by the nurse or parent. Acute dystonia and NMS-pattern signs are red flags: call the school nurse at once and contact the prescriber immediately. NMS-pattern signs, or any difficulty breathing or swallowing, are an emergency: call emergency services.

3. Mood Stabilizers, Anticonvulsants, Stimulants, and Others

EvidenceLithium has a narrow margin between therapeutic and toxic levels; tremor, vomiting, diarrhea, unsteadiness, slurred speech, and confusion are among the toxicity signs, which call for the doctor immediately (FDA prescribing information; MedlinePlus). Valproate carries boxed warnings for hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, and fetal risk; lamotrigine and carbamazepine carry boxed warnings for serious rash; carbamazepine also for aplastic anemia and agranulocytosis; topiramate and zonisamide can reduce sweating and cause overheating in children; levetiracetam is associated with irritability and behavioral change. Stimulants commonly reduce appetite and sleep and can slow growth; antidepressants carry boxed warnings for suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children, adolescents, and young adults, atomoxetine in pediatric patients 6 years and older, and viloxazine in patients with ADHD; alpha-2 agonists (guanfacine, clonidine) cause sedation and lower blood pressure and pulse, with rebound hypertension if stopped abruptly (Mayo Clinic; MedlinePlus; FDA labeling). Antiepileptic drugs as a class carry an FDA warning for suicidal ideation.
Applied guidanceThe library flags the red-flag items for each medication (rash, toxicity pattern, overheating, breathing or swallowing difficulty, fainting, statements of self-harm, jaundice, unusual bleeding, severe abdominal pain). Any red-flag observation, at any rating, is reported at once, whatever the reporting schedule: to the school nurse, who reaches the parent and the prescriber; an item marked emergency, or any difficulty breathing or swallowing, is a call to emergency services first. Behavioral side effects (irritability, activation, disinhibition) are recorded alongside the BIP data so the prescriber can see whether the target behavior changed in step with a dose change. A change in step with a dose change is a correlation in time, not proof that the medication caused it.

4. Recording Conventions

Applied guidanceThe tracking grid uses the scatter-plot convention (Touchette, MacDonald, & Langer, 1985) adapted to severity: blank = not observed; slash = mild or questionable; light = moderate (clearly present, not interfering); dark = marked (interfering with the day or needing a response). Red-flag items shade orange at any rating. One row per watched side effect, one column per school day, plus rows for whether the dose was given and for the BIP target behavior at the same severity scale so the two can be read together. Vitals are entered on the vitals sheet on the ADA/APA schedule; BMI is computed. The report sheet summarizes each watched item by week, lists AIMS results against the Schooler-Kane criteria, and lists every red-flag entry with its date.
Extrapyramidal descriptions and scale conventions after Guy (1976), Barnes (1989), Simpson and Angus (1970), and Schooler and Kane (1982). Metabolic schedule after the ADA/APA/AACE/NAASO consensus (2004) and the AACAP practice parameter (2011). Medication side-effect lists compiled from FDA prescribing information and the drug information of MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic (accessed September 2026); verify against the current label. Nothing on this form is medical advice.

Observe the student seated, hands unsupported, at rest and while distracted (e.g., tapping fingers, opening the mouth, protruding the tongue, walking). Rate 0 none, 1 minimal (may be extreme normal), 2 mild, 3 moderate, 4 severe. Movements seen only on activation score one point lower than those seen spontaneously.

Recorded by the school nurse where available. ADA/APA (2004) schedule for second-generation antipsychotics: weight (BMI), waist, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and lipids at baseline; weight at 4, 8, and 12 weeks, then quarterly; blood pressure, fasting glucose, and lipids at 12 weeks, then blood pressure and glucose annually and a normal lipid profile every 5 years (glucose and lipids per the prescriber).
