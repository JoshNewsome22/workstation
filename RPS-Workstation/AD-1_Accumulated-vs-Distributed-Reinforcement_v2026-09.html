Accumulated vs. Distributed Reinforcement: Efficacy, Preference & Implementation

An assessment-to-implementation packet for deciding how a student’s reinforcement should be delivered: in small amounts right after each response (distributed), or banked across the work and delivered in one uninterrupted block at the end (accumulated). It tests what each arrangement does to work, learning and problem behavior, asks the student which one they choose, counts the time each one costs, and then builds the chosen arrangement into daily programming. Work the tabs in order; each carries forward decisions from the last.

E. Removal Probe

Give the reinforcer for the distributed unit (for example 30 s), end it with the planned routine, and present the next task. Time how long the student takes to give the item back and to start working again, and count problem behavior from removal until work resumes. None of the studies ran a separate probe like this. It measures the cost the authors describe: Robinson and St. Peter (2019) reported that problem behavior “typically occurred during and after the removal of the preferred item” in their distributed condition (p. 1087), and one teacher in Gingras (2022) wrote that “transitioning back after one minute was a challenge” (p. 21).

B. Parameters Held Constant Across Arrangements

Choose the timing rule deliberately; it changes the result. DeLeon et al. (2014) removed token-trading and reinforcer-access time from session time (pp. 299, 301), and Robinson and St. Peter (2019) paused the work timer during distributed access, so both measured rate over work time. Fulton et al. (2020) ran a 10-min session timer that was never paused. With the clock stopped, the pauses after each removal still count as work time. That is where Robinson and St. Peter tentatively placed the difference: the accumulated arrangement’s higher rates “seemed to be due, at least in part, to elimination of pauses and problem behavior that occurred after the removal of the reinforcer” (p. 1087). Tab 4 records work time and total time either way.

E. Design and Decision Rules

Mandel (2021) required 100% independent correct across three consecutive sessions and at least two days; if an arrangement had not produced mastery after twice the sessions the first-mastered arrangement needed, and responding was not increasing, the target was switched to that arrangement (pp. 31–32). Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) called a condition ineffective when mean problem behavior was 50% or more of the baseline mean, with no decreasing trend, and not because of one elevated session (p. 1578); Tab 4 applies the first part of that test and leaves the other two to visual analysis.

Preference assessment plan (Tab 5)

Stopping rules as used: five consecutive selections of one arrangement, or at most 15 trials (Mandel, 2021, p. 33); the same link five consecutive times, 8 of 10, or no differentiated responding for 10 consecutive sessions (Frank-Crawford et al., 2021); removing an option after three consecutive selections (Robinson & St. Peter, 2019, p. 1081).

Comparison by Arrangement

    Reading a multielement graph. Look for separation between the arrangements’ data paths that holds across the whole phase, not only in the means. DeLeon et al. (2014) found the highest mean rates with accumulated access for all three participants analyzed, and still reported that the differential effects “were not replicated with Evan or Alice” (p. 301). A difference in means with overlapping paths is not a replicated difference.

    Preference: Concurrent-Chains Assessment

    The student chooses the arrangement. Each arrangement has its card (the initial link); touching a card starts a session run under that arrangement (the terminal link). All five studies that measured choice used concurrent chains; Gingras (2022) asked with a questionnaire instead (Tab 8).

    1. Say what each card means. Mandel (2021) stated the contingency for each card at the start of the assessment (p. 33). Robinson and St. Peter (2019) restated each rule at every choice (p. 1081).
    2. Forced exposures. Guide the student to each card in turn and run its session. DeLeon et al. (2014) ran two forced exposures (one per arrangement) before every five choice trials (p. 303); Mandel (2021) ran two per condition at the start (p. 33).
    3. Choice. Present the cards in a row and say one fixed sentence (“Pick one”; Mandel used “pick the one you want to do,” allowing up to 5 s). Rotate the positions across trials so a side preference cannot pass for an arrangement preference.
    4. Terminal link. Remove the other cards and run the chosen arrangement exactly as in Tab 4.
    5. Next trial after the reinforcement ends. Record every choice, including no selection.

    The most effective arrangement is not always the one chosen. In Robinson and St. Peter (2019), “students do not always prefer the most efficacious reinforcer arrangement” (p. 1076): two of three preferred distributed reinforcement even though it “continued to produce problem behavior.” In Fulton et al. (2020), preferences were idiosyncratic: one child chose distributed breaks on all 8 trials (and on the last 6 of 7 when timers showed each break’s length), one chose accumulated breaks on all 10, and one favored accumulated breaks over his first 29 sessions, also choosing distributed and baseline, and then chose baseline on the final nine (pp. 790–791). Removing a preferred option can be costly: when Robinson and St. Peter removed the accumulated option from one student’s choice array, the first session after its removal “necessitated involvement of the school crisis team” (pp. 1083–1084).

    No-Work Check (optional)

    If the student chooses the distributed card, find out whether they avoid the longer block itself or the wait and work before it. Fulton et al. (2020) report that the child who chose distributed breaks “consistently chose the 7:30 timer” when no work was required, and suggest the larger reinforcer may have been discounted because of the delay to it (p. 793; data not shown). Offer both amounts with no work required.

    Procedural Integrity and Interobserver Agreement

    Only three of the six works reported procedural integrity (Robinson & St. Peter, 2019: 100%; Gingras, 2022: 100%; Mandel, 2021: means of 99.5% and 97.6%). An arrangement comparison is especially exposed to drift: a distributed unit that stretches from 30 s to 50 s, or an accumulated block cut short, changes the very variable being compared.

      C. Implementation Plan

      The studies did not test warnings, ending routines or return-to-work routines; Mandel (2021) notes that behavior after the final reinforcer was removed was not recorded (p. 62). Whatever the plan specifies here, keep measuring what happens at removal.

      Extending the work requirement

      Build steps from the current requirement. The ladder keeps the amount per response constant, so the block grows with the work. Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) thinned from FR 1 toward FR 10 after three sessions per condition (six for Roland), with the unit price rising at each step (pp. 1577–1578). That differs from this ladder; for one participant (Roland) the authors could not say whether a constant unit price would have kept the accumulated arrangement more effective (p. 1582). In their study the accumulated break was the first arrangement dropped for two participants (Sawyer, during the second FR 1 phase; Roland, at FR 2), and treatment effects held only to FR 4 and FR 2 for those two (p. 1583). Re-check problem behavior and choice at every step.

      Definitions

      Mandel (2021, p. 10), following DeLeon et al. (2014): in a distributed arrangement, “a reinforcer is delivered immediately after a specific response requirement is met; both the duration or amount of the reinforcer and the response requirement are usually small.” An accumulated arrangement “involves banking a specified parameter of reinforcement (i.e., time, quantity) through an instructional period or larger work requirement; the entire quantity of the reinforcer is delivered at the end of the period or larger work requirement.” When each banked unit is marked by a token, picture or item on a plate, the arrangement is token-mediated. When the bank is emptied every few responses rather than once, this form calls it intermediate.

      Study by Study

      DeLeon, Chase, Frank-Crawford, Carreau-Webster, Triggs, Bullock, & Jennett (2014)

      Four hospital inpatients aged 15–20 admitted for problem behavior. Experiment 1 (activities): reversal design with multielement phases; each response earned 30 s of access at once (distributed) or a token worth 30 s traded at the end of the session (accumulated); token-trading and access time were removed from session time when rate was calculated (pp. 299, 301). Experiment 2: concurrent chains with activities and edibles; ten demands per terminal link, 30 s or one piece per response vs. tokens traded after ten for up to 300 consecutive seconds or ten pieces; two forced exposures before every five choices (p. 303).

      • Finding Responses per minute, accumulated vs. distributed: Evan 1.7 vs. 0.9, then 0.8 vs. 0.8; Alice 1.2 and 1.8 vs. 0.8 and 0.6; Jillian 1.8 vs. 1.4 (baselines 0.1, 0, 1.5). The authors state that the differential effects “were not replicated with Evan or Alice” (p. 301). The fourth participant’s efficacy assessment was stopped for lack of a usable baseline.
      • Finding “All participants preferred delayed, accumulated access when the reinforcer was an activity. Three participants also preferred accumulated access to edible reinforcers” (p. 293). With edibles, Evan was indifferent. When the delay to exchange was lengthened, Alice’s choices moved toward indifference and Jillian’s did not.
      • Finding Time to complete ten tasks, excluding reinforcement: with activities, shorter under accumulated for all four (80, 82, 171 and 95 s vs. 93, 119, 199 and 193 s); with edibles, shorter for two (111 and 187 vs. 128 and 265 s) and longer for two (76 and 91 vs. 52 and 54 s) (p. 308).
      • Explanation “duration of access, rather than frequency of delivery, controlled choices in Experiment 2” (p. 309); distributed delivery may add handling costs: time to reorient to the task after each delivery, which may have lowered distributed rates (p. 301), and repeated reorientation to the activity itself, which may have favored choosing accumulated access (p. 309); edibles may disrupt responding less (p. 309); tokens may act as conditioned reinforcers or signal the delay (pp. 301, 312).
      • Authors “despite the inherent delay, accumulated reinforcement is just as effective and is often preferred by learners over distributed reinforcement” (p. 293); accumulated reinforcement “may provide a more efficient use of work periods without detriment to the overall effectiveness of the teaching program” (p. 308); “duration of access is an important determinant of reinforcing efficacy for activities” (p. 309).
      • Limitations stated: Experiment 1 was brief and may have been rule governed (pp. 301–302); tokens appeared only in the accumulated condition for some participants (p. 312); conclusions about the longer exchange delay “are limited” (p. 309); generality is uncertain “beyond individuals with relatively sophisticated verbal repertoires” (p. 311). No procedural integrity was reported. Total reinforcement was equated in Experiment 2 (p. 310) but not in Experiment 1.

      Fulton, Tiger, Meitzen, & Effertz (2020)

      Three boys (8, 11 and 13 years) with autism; functional analyses showed escape-maintained problem behavior for two and multiple sources of reinforcement for one. Differential reinforcement of compliance with a tablet break: 30 s after every compliance (distributed) or praise and a 7.5-min break after 15 compliances (accumulated). In both, problem behavior produced the next prompt, not a break; in baseline it produced 30 s of escape. A 10-min session timer was never paused (p. 787). Reversal and multielement designs, then concurrent chains.

      • Finding “Accumulated-reinforcement arrangements did not occasion additional problem behavior, but rather resulted in consistently lower levels of problem behavior for 2 of 3 participants” (p. 782). Hugh: a second distributed phase did not replicate the first (problem behavior 1.9 per minute, 82% compliance); accumulated reduced problem behavior below 0.1 per minute with 95% compliance. Kevin: distributed 3.1 per minute and 60.4% compliance; accumulated zero and 99% (pp. 789–790). In the final multielement comparisons both arrangements kept problem behavior low.
      • Finding “Participants demonstrated idiosyncratic preferences” (p. 782): Lyle chose distributed on all 8 trials (and on the final six consecutive of 7 when timers showed each break’s length), Hugh chose accumulated on all 10, and Kevin favored accumulated across his first 29 sessions, also choosing distributed and baseline, and then chose baseline on the final nine (pp. 790–791). Accumulated work periods typically lasted about 3 min (p. 787).
      • Explanation “It seems likely that any putative reinforcing stimulus has a critical magnitude above which the stimulus will serve as a reinforcer and below which it will not” (p. 791); “the increased reinforcement delays associated with accumulated reinforcement may have resulted in a discounting of the larger-magnitude reinforcer” (p. 793). Why distributed breaks worked in the multielement phases is “admittedly speculative” (pp. 792–793).
      • Authors “practitioners should be mindful of the role magnitude may play in their intervention design” (p. 792); in interpreting differential reinforcement of compliance as a competition between positive and negative reinforcers, “it is important to factor in both the delay to the positive reinforcer as well as prolonged exposure to the aversive elements of instruction delivery” (p. 793); when a child chooses not to participate, an analyst can “modify conditions to promote choosing therapeutic engagement” (p. 794).
      • Limitations stated: the explanations were not tested (pp. 792–793); the authors are not aware of procedures to identify critical magnitudes (p. 792). No formal verbal assessment was done (p. 784), and no procedural integrity was reported.
      • Before citing: the text appears to swap participant names twice (p. 789, “Hugh and Lyle’s”; p. 793, “Kevin was the only participant”); check the results section and figures.

      Robinson & St. Peter (2019)

      Three 10-year-olds with ADHD or ADD and chronic, severe problem behavior in an alternative-education center. Math flashcards read aloud. Each correct answer earned 15 s with a chosen toy, either at once with the hidden work timer paused (distributed) or summed and delivered after a 3-min work period (accumulated); a control condition had no toys. Problem behavior was ignored during sessions. Multielement design with block randomization; then concurrent chains in which an option was removed after three consecutive selections (p. 1081). Rate was correct responses per minute of work time (p. 1082); procedural integrity was 100% (p. 1079).

      • Finding Correct responses per minute, accumulated / distributed / control: Willis 7.6 / 4.4 / 2.0; Miles 4.8 / 2.4 / 2.1; Harmony 4.3 / 3.1 / 2.9. Problem behavior, percentage of intervals: Willis 1 / 28 / 65; Miles 14 / 20 / 56; Harmony 0 / 5 / 2. Accuracy was “generally undifferentiated” between the two arrangements (p. 1082).
      • Finding “The accumulated condition, which was the most efficacious condition for all three students, was not necessarily the most preferred” (p. 1083). “Two students preferred distributed reinforcers, even though this arrangement continued to produce problem behavior” (p. 1076). When the accumulated option was removed from Willis’s choice array, the first session after its removal “necessitated involvement of the school crisis team” (pp. 1083–1084).
      • Explanation The accumulated arrangement’s higher rates “seemed to be due, at least in part, to elimination of pauses and problem behavior that occurred after the removal of the reinforcer” (p. 1087); “problem behavior typically occurred during and after the removal of the preferred item during the distributed condition” (p. 1087). For the preference mismatch they considered impulsive choice, the choice instructions, card color, and the absence of tokens in the accumulated condition (pp. 1085–1086).
      • Authors “accumulated-reinforcer arrangements may be preferable in academic environments for students with a history of chronic or severe problem behavior” (p. 1084); “students do not always prefer the most efficacious reinforcer arrangement” (p. 1076); accumulated strategies “seem to hold much promise” for interventions that are easy for teachers to use (p. 1087).
      • Limitations stated: an uncontrolled classroom (p. 1085); class-wide tokens delivered after sessions may have suppressed problem behavior (p. 1086); no token feedback during accumulated sessions (p. 1086); short 3-min work periods and 10-s intervals that hid response sequences (p. 1087). No functional analysis was conducted by the authors. The amount per response was equated; total access was not.

      Frank-Crawford, Borrero, Newcomb, Doan, Fisher, & Rooker (2021)

      Eight participants aged 7–18, most with autism and intellectual disability, with escape-maintained problem behavior by functional analysis. Reinforcers were food or a break without attention or leisure items, 1 piece or 30 s per compliance, either at once or as tokens traded at the end of the 10-task terminal link (Experiment 1) or of the 20-trial session (Experiment 2). Experiment 1 (n = 5): concurrent-chains preference, with problem behavior on extinction. Experiment 2 (n = 5): efficacy without escape extinction (problem behavior produced 30 s of escape) in a reversal design with multielement phases, then schedule thinning (FR 1, 2, 4, 5, 10) after three sessions per condition (six for Roland), with the unit price rising at each step (pp. 1577–1578).

      • Finding “Accumulated breaks were more preferred than distributed breaks for most participants” (p. 1581): four of five. Only two “clearly and consistently preferred accumulated food to distributed (Finn and Joel)” (p. 1576).
      • Finding “In no case was accumulated break more effective than distributed reinforcement” (p. 1581). At FR 1 the accumulated break (or, for Finn, a variant) gave outcomes similar to distributed breaks for four of five participants, and accumulated food (or the variant) was as effective as distributed food for all four who received food (p. 1581). For Finn, problem behavior fell in all conditions only after variant conditions, with tokens traded every 10 trials, were added (p. 1579). “Results were mixed during schedule thinning” (p. 1581): the accumulated break was dropped first for two participants.
      • Finding Instruction time was longest with the accumulated break for four of five participants; “arranging a break in an accumulated manner may not increase the efficiency of task completion” (p. 1580).
      • Explanation The function of problem behavior may influence preference (pp. 1575–1576); a medication started during the study may have acted as a motivating operation for one participant (p. 1576); distributed food may not raise handling costs the way leisure activities do (p. 1580); rapid alternation in a multielement design may have favored the distributed break, which should be verified “in isolation” (p. 1581); rising unit price, effort and delay during thinning may favor distribution (pp. 1581–1582).
      • Authors “there may be added value in arranging accumulated access to reinforcers for individuals with escape-maintained problem behavior, and in arranging accumulated access to break, in particular” (p. 1576); propose combining the break with activities and/or implementing extinction when possible as one way to strengthen an accumulated break (pp. 1582–1583); “for some individuals, distributed arrangements may be more effective as reinforcement schedules are thinned” (p. 1583).
      • Limitations stated: the cards were complex and several participants did not pass the discrimination test; only two participants completed both experiments; support for preference predicting efficacy is limited; effects held only to FR 4 and FR 2 for two participants (p. 1583). No procedural integrity or social validity was reported.
      • Before citing: the counts of effective arrangements differ between the abstract (p. 1566) and the discussion sections (pp. 1580–1581); the stated aim includes “without the use of extinction” (p. 1568), yet problem behavior in Experiment 1 was on extinction (p. 1572).

      Mandel (2021), doctoral dissertation

      Two boys with autism aged 3.67 and 4.25 years, taught tacts or intraverbals in 10-trial discrete-trial sessions. Each independent correct response earned praise plus one edible or 30 s of an activity, either at once (distributed edible, distributed activity) or placed on a visible plate as the edible itself or as a picture worth 30 s and delivered after the last trial, with a timer showing the time earned (accumulated edible, accumulated activity). No token training was done. Adapted alternating treatments within a nonconcurrent multiple baseline across three comparisons per child; then concurrent chains with four cards, two forced exposures per condition, and a stop at five consecutive identical choices or 15 trials (p. 33).

      • Finding “For both participants, the distributed arrangements resulted in the greatest efficiency in terms of sessions and the distributed with edibles arrangement resulted in the shortest duration to mastery criterion” (p. 53). Mean minutes to mastery including reinforcement: Liam 17.8 (distributed edible) to 55.3 (accumulated activity); Asher 13.7 to 66.6 (pp. 40–42).
      • Finding “both distributed arrangements resulted in acquisition across all comparisons and participants. In contrast, the accumulated with activity and edible arrangements resulted in acquisition for only 50% and 83% of comparisons, respectively” (p. 53); the rest were switched to the first-mastered arrangement. The distributed edible arrangement had the least disruptive behavior for both boys.
      • Finding Preference: Liam chose distributed activity on 88.9% of trials and accumulated edible on 11.1%; Asher chose distributed edible on 60% and distributed activity on 40% (p. 44). The author judged all procedures socially acceptable from caregiver and clinician ratings (p. 44); one clinician was neutral on the discrete-trial item (Table 5).
      • Explanation Framed as possibilities: tokens in other studies may have been “conditioned reinforcers and thus the accumulated arrangements actually included a FR1 schedule” (p. 57); age and self-control (pp. 57–58); the accumulated conditions’ stimuli may have functioned as a reflexive conditioned motivating operation (CMO-R) (pp. 58–59); little history with accumulated arrangements (p. 59); edibles take less time to consume (p. 54). The accumulated activity arrangement “seemed to become efficacious after repeated exposure” (p. 60).
      • Authors “for clinicians working with young children, the use of a distributed arrangement with edibles should be considered, unless edible reinforcers are not appropriate”; the accumulated activity arrangement should be avoided with young children “whenever possible”; “when possible, clinicians should attempt to include the participant’s most preferred arrangement within their programming” (p. 63).
      • Limitations stated: disruptive behavior was measured only after data collection ended, so consequences for it were not specified (pp. 61–62); behavior after the final reinforcer was removed was not recorded (p. 62); two participants; possible outside exposure to targets; the effect of the symbols is unknown; establishing operations were not controlled (sessions after lunch) (p. 61).
      • Before citing: the text and tables disagree on several values (for example, Liam’s distributed-edible sessions, 5.7 in the text and 5.4 in Table 3); means for accumulated conditions include sessions after the switch to a distributed arrangement (Table 3, note a).

      Gingras (2022), master’s thesis

      Two students at risk for emotional or behavioral disorders (5 and 8 years) in general-education math classes of 20–25 students; their teachers implemented. A silent timer cued a check every 5 min; on-task behavior earned praise and a picture token worth 1 min, up to six per 30-min session, spent at once (distributed) or all at the end of class (accumulated). Multiple baseline across participants with embedded alternating treatments; preference by questionnaire. Integrity 100%.

      • Finding Off-task behavior (10-s partial intervals, researcher data), baseline / accumulated / distributed: Dean 48.90% / 19.45% / 34.51%; Jess 64.56% / 26% / 51.56% (p. 16). “Results indicated that the accumulated arrangement led to a greater reduction in off-task behavior for both participants” (p. iv).
      • Finding Both students chose rewards at the end of class (p. 22). One teacher wrote that the student “preferred the accumulated model as transitioning back after one minute was a challenge” (p. 21).
      • Authors “differential reinforcement with accumulated reinforcers may be a feasible and effective intervention for disruptive behavior of students in general education classroom” (p. 29).
      • Limitations stated: uncontrolled classroom variables; only two students because of COVID-19; the teachers’ momentary time-sampling data were inaccurate (p. 28).
      • Before citing: the thesis reports an 8.81% advantage for Jess, but its own means differ by 25.56 points (p. 17); treat the reported summary values with care.

      Across the Six Works NBH

      • Where accumulated did as well or better: activities with adolescents and young adults (DeLeon et al.), academic responding by students with chronic problem behavior (Robinson & St. Peter), escape-maintained problem behavior with a large tablet break (Fulton et al.), off-task behavior (researcher-recorded) in general-education classes (Gingras), and food at FR 1 (Frank-Crawford et al.).
      • Where distributed did better: discrete-trial acquisition with two preschoolers (Mandel), breaks as the schedule was thinned (Frank-Crawford et al.), and ten-task completion time with edibles for two of four participants (DeLeon et al.).
      • Preference is individual. Most participants chose accumulated access to activities (DeLeon et al.) and to breaks (Frank-Crawford et al.), but choices split in Fulton et al. and Robinson and St. Peter, and both preschoolers chose distributed arrangements (Mandel). The arrangement chosen was not always the one that worked best.
      • Cost cuts both ways. Accumulated access shortened work time with activities (DeLeon et al.) but lengthened instruction time with breaks (Frank-Crawford et al.) and time to mastery with preschoolers (Mandel). The studies timed sessions differently (work time vs. total time), so their rates cannot be compared directly.
      • So this form tests each student. Efficacy (Tab 4), choice (Tab 5), cost (Tab 3D and the Total column in Tab 4), and whether the effect holds as the requirement grows (Tab 7).

      What the Evidence Does Not Tell You Yet NBH

      • No study tested warnings, ending routines or return-to-work routines, and none reports behavior after the final removal: Mandel (2021, p. 62) names this as a limitation, and Fulton et al. (2020, p. 787) did not extend data collection into reinforcement periods.
      • Only three of six works reported procedural integrity.
      • Only Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) thinned the schedule; how long an accumulated effect holds as the work requirement grows is otherwise untested.
      • Tokens appeared in some accumulated conditions and not others, so token effects and accumulation effects are partly confounded (DeLeon et al., 2014, p. 312; Mandel, 2021, p. 57).
      • Twenty-two participants in single-case designs; children under 6 appear only in Mandel (2021; 3.67 and 4.25 years) and Gingras (2022; one 5-year-old in kindergarten).
      • Generalization and maintenance were not the focus of any of the six.

      What every session has in common

      Read once; the sections below change only the card, the sentence, and when the reinforcer arrives.

      Every session in this evaluation has the same shape. The arrangement’s card goes in view, the adult says one fixed sentence, the student works through the same kind of task, and the reinforcer arrives either after each response or all at once at the end. Everything else stays the same across arrangements: the task, the prompts, the consequence for problem behavior, and the way access ends. If any of those drift between arrangements, the comparison measures the drift.

      Two adults are best: one runs the session, the other keeps the clock and the counts, which also gives you the interobserver agreement Tab 6 asks for.

      Materials: one card per arrangement, the task materials, the reinforcer ready to hand over at once, a timer the student can see, a stopwatch for work time, the token board if a token arrangement is in the comparison, and the session sheet.

        A. A distributed session

        Reinforcer after every response · Tab 4 log

        Each response earns a small amount right away: 30 s of the tablet in this example. The session has as many deliveries and removals as responses. Time each return to work: it is the cost distributed delivery adds. Robinson and St. Peter (2019) reported that problem behavior “typically occurred during and after the removal of the preferred item” in their distributed condition (p. 1087).

        A. At the table, step by step

          What you say and do

          • Every problem you finish, you get 30 seconds of tablet right away. Once, before the first task.
          • After each qualifying response: praise, and the reinforcer within the delivery window. Stop the work clock if Tab 3 reads rate on work time.
          • At the end of the unit: the written ending routine, then the next task at once.

          What goes on the sheet

          • Responses, problem behavior, work time, access time and total time.
          • The seconds from each removal to the next response; their mean goes in Resumes.
          • Where problem behavior happened (during work, during access, or at removal), in the notes.
          Decision. None from one session. Distributed sessions alternate with the others until each arrangement has the minimum number of sessions set in Tab 3; then Tab 4 compares them.

          B. An accumulated session

          Reinforcer banked, delivered in one block · Tab 4 log

          Each response banks the same amount, and the whole amount comes at the end in one uninterrupted block. The student works straight through with no removals until the block ends, but every banked response waits for its reinforcer. Say the total aloud before the block starts, and time the block on a visible timer.

          B. At the table, step by step

            What you say and do

            • Every problem you finish earns 30 seconds. After ten, you get all your tablet time at once.
            • After each qualifying response: praise, and a visible mark (a tally, or a picture or the item itself on a plate, as in Mandel, 2021).
            • At the end of the work: stop the work clock, state the total, set the timer and give the whole block without interruption.

            What goes on the sheet

            • Responses, problem behavior, work time, access time and total time.
            • Resumes stays blank: there are no returns to work inside the session.
            • Any problem behavior at the single removal, in the notes.
            Decision. As for A: alternate until each arrangement has its minimum sessions, then compare in Tab 4. A block that was cut short or extended is not a clean accumulated session; note it on the row.

            C. Accumulated with tokens

            A token for each response, exchanged at the end · requires Tab 2C

            The same as B, except that each banked unit is a token the student can see and count. Run it only after the student exchanges independently: DeLeon et al. (2014) trained exchange from 1 to 5 to 10 tokens first (pp. 297–298). Mandel (2021), who did no token training, raised trained tokens as a possible reason accumulated arrangements did better in other studies (p. 57).

            C. At the table, step by step

              What you say and do

              • Every problem earns a token. Each token is 30 seconds. After ten, you trade them for all your time.
              • After each qualifying response: praise, and a token within the delivery window.
              • At the end: count the tokens together, state the time, set the timer and give the whole block.

              What goes on the sheet

              • As for B, plus the exchange time.
              • An exchange that is not independent within 10 s, in the notes; if it recurs, return to token training.
              Decision. Comparing C with B separates the effect of the tokens from the effect of banking. If C does better than B, keep the tokens in the plan.

              D. A choice trial (concurrent chains)

              Initial link: the card · terminal link: the session · Tab 5

              The student picks the arrangement. The cards go down in a row; the adult says what each one means, then one fixed instruction; the student touches a card; the other cards go away, and the chosen arrangement runs as a full session. Forced exposures come first, so that the student has met every card’s outcome before choosing.

              D. At the table, step by step

                What you say and do

                • Forced exposure: guide the student’s hand to one card and run that session, once per card before the first choice (or as Tab 3 plans).
                • This one: tablet right away. This one: all your time at the end. This one: no tablet. Then Pick one. and wait up to 5 s.
                • Rotate the card positions every trial.

                What goes on the sheet

                • Trial type (forced or choice), the card picked, its position, and whether the session was completed.
                • No selection within 5 s: present the trial once more; if there is still no selection, record it and end the trial (items on Mandel’s, 2021, integrity checklist).
                • Problem behavior during the terminal link.
                Decision. Stop by the rule written in Tab 3, for example five consecutive choices of one arrangement, or at most 15 trials. Check for a position bias before reading the result.

                E. Breaks for escape-maintained behavior

                Compliance earns a break · Tab 4 log

                When problem behavior is maintained by escape from demands, the reinforcer for compliance can be the break itself: a short break after every compliance (distributed), or the breaks banked and taken at once after a set number (accumulated). Fulton et al. (2020) compared 30 s after each with 7.5 min after 15, both with a tablet, and the accumulated break lowered problem behavior for two of three children. With breaks that had no leisure items, Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) found the accumulated break never more effective than distributed reinforcement.

                E. At the table, step by step

                  What you say and do

                  • The instruction, then the prompt sequence written in Tab 3.
                  • Compliance: praise, and either the break at once (distributed) or a count toward the block (accumulated).
                  • Problem behavior: exactly the Tab 3 consequence, the same in both arrangements, whether that is continued prompting (escape extinction) or a fixed escape period.

                  What goes on the sheet

                  • Instructions presented (Opportunities), compliance (Responses) and problem behavior.
                  • Work time and total time. Instruction time is where Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) found the accumulated break slowest, for four of five participants.
                  Decision. Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) propose combining the break with activities and/or implementing extinction when possible as one way to strengthen an accumulated break (pp. 1582–1583). If an accumulated break is chosen, check it again at every step of the ladder.

                  F. Raising the requirement

                  Daily use · Tab 7 ladder

                  Once an arrangement is chosen, the work requirement grows by the ladder in Tab 7. The amount per response stays the same, so the block grows with the work. Measure what the evaluation measured, advance and step back only by the written rules, and check the student’s choice again every few steps.

                  F. At the table, step by step

                    What you say and do

                    • The sentence with the new numbers, once, before the first task.
                    • Everything else exactly as in the chosen arrangement’s sessions.

                    What goes on the sheet

                    • Rate on work time, problem behavior and total time, one session a day, with the step number in Phase.
                    • Two choice trials every few steps, in the Tab 5 format.
                    Decision. Advance on the written criterion; step back on the written criterion. Frank-Crawford et al. (2021) suggest that “for some individuals, distributed arrangements may be more effective as reinforcement schedules are thinned” (p. 1583); in their study the work per reinforcer rose at each step, which this ladder avoids.

                    Terms, as the sheets use them

                    For whoever is holding the clipboard
                    Distributed arrangement
                    A small amount of the reinforcer right after each response, or each small requirement. Many deliveries and many removals per session.
                    Accumulated arrangement
                    Each response banks the same amount, and the whole amount is delivered in one uninterrupted block at the end of the work. One delivery, one removal.
                    Accumulated with tokens
                    The banked units are tokens (or pictures, or items on a plate) the student can see; they are exchanged for the block at the end.
                    Intermediate arrangement
                    Units are banked and exchanged every few responses: a block after every 5 problems instead of after all 10.
                    Unit and block
                    The unit is the amount one response earns (30 s, one piece). The block is the total delivered at once in an accumulated arrangement: responses times the unit.
                    Work time and total time
                    Work time runs only while the student is working (the clock stops during access and exchange). Total time runs from the first task to the end of the last access. Rate is read on work time; cost on total time.
                    Resumes
                    Seconds from the removal of the reinforcer to the next response. It exists only where the reinforcer is removed mid-session: distributed and intermediate arrangements.
                    Handling or re-orientation cost
                    Time spent setting the reinforcer up or getting back into it on each delivery (DeLeon et al., 2014, p. 309), and getting back to the task afterward (p. 301). Both recur with every distributed delivery.
                    Concurrent chains
                    A choice procedure. The initial link is the choice between cards; the terminal link is the session run under the chosen arrangement.
                    Forced exposure
                    A trial on which the student is guided to one card and experiences its session, so every outcome has been met before free choices begin.
                    Stopping rule
                    The written criterion that ends the preference assessment: for example, five consecutive choices of one arrangement, or a maximum number of trials.
                    Critical magnitude
                    Fulton et al.’s (2020) term for the amount above which a stimulus serves as a reinforcer and below which it does not (p. 791): a 30-s unit may fall below it, a 7.5-min block may not.
                    Ineffective (Frank-Crawford rule)
                    Mean problem behavior at 50% or more of the baseline mean, with no decreasing trend, and not because of one elevated session (Frank-Crawford et al., 2021, p. 1578).
                    Interobserver agreement (IOA)
                    Two observers score the same session independently. Total count for totals, trial-by-trial for choices, total duration for times. Collect it on at least a third of sessions.
