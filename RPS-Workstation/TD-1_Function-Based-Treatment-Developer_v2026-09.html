Guide: Function-Based Treatment and How to Choose One

A function-based treatment changes some part of the contingency that maintains the behavior: it abolishes the establishing operation, withholds the reinforcer (extinction), delivers it for another response (differential reinforcement), or delivers it on a time schedule (noncontingent reinforcement). Treatments are matched to the function identified on Form EA-1; a procedure that works for one function may do nothing for another. Evidence boxes report what the cited studies found; Applied guidance boxes state the practice rule this form uses. The escape model is published; the attention, tangible, and automatic frameworks are this practice's assemblies of the cited studies.

1. Why the Function Decides the Treatment

EvidenceIwata, Pace, Cowdery, and Miltenberger (1994) showed that extinction reduced self-injury only when its form matched the function: each of 3 children, whose self-injury was maintained by attention, by escape, or automatically, was exposed to at least two forms of extinction, and self-injury fell only when the reinforcer shown to maintain it was discontinued. Carr and Durand (1985) taught a request for attention to children whose behavior was attention-maintained and a request for assistance to children whose behavior was escape-maintained; each reduced behavior only when the reinforcer matched. Tiger, Hanley, and Bruzek (2008) reviewed 91 FCT articles (204 participants): functions were escape from demands in 43% of cases, attention 32%, materials 29%, and 71 of 91 studies used a functional analysis to identify the reinforcer. Harding, Wacker, Berg, Lee, and Dolezal (2009) reported that across 95 in-home functional analyses, 85% of children's destructive behavior was socially maintained and 75% of those treated with FCT showed a 90% or greater reduction.
Applied guidanceThe developer accepts one function per target behavior from EA-1. Multiply controlled behavior gets a component for each function (Day, Horner, & O'Neill, 1994). An undifferentiated analysis is not a license to guess: return to EA-1's undifferentiated sequence, or, if treatment cannot wait, build a package with an in-treatment test (reversal) so the function is confirmed by the treatment data.

2. Escape-Maintained Behavior: The Geiger, Carr, and LeBlanc (2010) Model

EvidenceSix treatment classes have support for escape functions: activity choice (Dyer, Dunlap, & Winterling, 1990; Kern et al., 1998); curricular and instructional revision (Dunlap, Kern-Dunlap, Clarke, & Robbins, 1991; Dunlap & Kern, 1996), including denser positive reinforcement during tasks, which reduced escape-maintained behavior even when escape remained available (Lalli et al., 1999; Ingvarsson, Hanley, & Welter, 2009); demand fading (Pace, Iwata, Cowdery, Andree, & McIntyre, 1993), which works best with escape extinction (Zarcone, Iwata, Smith, Mazaleski, & Lerman, 1994); differential negative reinforcement of alternative behavior, including FCT, and of zero rates (Vollmer & Iwata, 1992; Vollmer, Roane, Ringdahl, & Marcus, 1999, who found DNRA held at reduced integrity when compliance was reinforced more densely than problem behavior; Roberts, Mace, & Daggett, 1995, who found DNRA more effective than DNRO); escape extinction (Iwata, Pace, Kalsher, Cowdery, & Cataldo, 1990: 5 of 6 children improved with guided compliance, the 6th needed blocking added); and noncontingent escape (Vollmer, Marcus, & Ringdahl, 1995: breaks thinned from 10 s to 2.5 and 10 min; faster reductions than DNRO). Geiger et al. ordered five questions by ethics, safety, practicality, and organization: curriculum and instruction appropriate; environment able to tolerate any problem behavior; any demands already complied with; time away from instruction tolerable; and the most important goal (communication, other curricular targets, or tolerance of a necessary aversive event). Goh, Iwata, and DeLeon (2000) found that a dense NCR schedule of the reinforcers maintaining self-injury interfered with acquisition of mands until it was thinned; this form applies the same caution to NCE and sequences NCE and FCT rather than combining them.
Applied guidanceThe selection sheet asks the five questions in Geiger et al.'s order and returns their options at each branch (curricular revision; demand fading and NCE; the same with extinction if viable; activity choice and extinction; FCT, DNRA, or DNRO by goal, each with extinction if viable). Combining components follows the model where it speaks (extinction where the model says to consider it if viable; activity choice with a consequence-based procedure such as differential reinforcement or extinction) and one rule of this form: never NCE with FCT while the NCE schedule is rich. Punishment is not in the model; it is considered only after the function-based options have been tried and is governed by district policy and the BACB code.
Applied guidance: physical guidance in escape extinctionEscape extinction keeps the task in place when the behavior happens. Iwata et al. (1990) carried it out with guided compliance, in which the adult guides the student’s hands through the task. The Responding sheet shows a block whenever the plan holds escape extinction, in any words (on the Responding sheet, or in a card’s steps or settings; for multiple control, until you record that escape is not one of the functions), and whenever the response to the behavior calls for hands-on help, whatever the function. It asks whether staff may guide the student’s hands at all. The final plan, Copy for the BIP, the staff flowchart and the integrity checklist carry its lines. Until they are in place, the plan lists what is missing, and Copy for the BIP, Build staff flowchart and Print final plan ask first.
  • The least help first. A fixed line: the instruction, a gesture, a model, then a light touch at the elbow or wrist, about 5 seconds apart; guiding the hands comes last, and only as the stop rule allows. Without guidance, help stops at a gesture or a model. The student’s own prompts are written below it. Prompts that start with hands-on help (most-to-least, full physical first) are named, unless the block records that they are for teaching the replacement behavior or a skill only. With the answer “No”, every step, setting or prompt order that still calls for hands-on help is named; the Extinction card’s own escape step changes to gestures and models with one tap.
  • A written stop rule. Staff let go at once when the student pulls away, pushes against the adult’s hand, or goes stiff, or at any sign of distress. The task stays in place. After a short calm pause the instruction is given again, starting with the least help. After a set number of stops on one instruction, staff do not guide it again that session, and they tell the BCBA. If distress goes on, or the behavior gets more dangerous, the task ends calmly and the BCBA is told the same day (after 5 minutes, the form’s default).
  • Why guidance. With guidance, the plan says why it is needed: what was tried first, with dates and data (BACB, 2020, Code 2.15).
  • Never force. Guidance is gentle: the adult’s hands help the student move, and never move the student against their pull. Holding a student still, or holding the student’s body to make them do the task, is restraint. In a Florida public school, restraint may not be used to correct noncompliance (§ 1003.573(3), F.S.; Form CR-1), and at home the plan follows the same rule. The crisis plan is used only for imminent danger. If a hold happens anyway, or the student is hurt, staff stop, make sure the student is safe, tell the BCBA and the school administrator at once, and report the hold as the crisis plan sets out; anyone who suspects abuse reports it right away to the Florida Abuse Hotline (§ 39.201, F.S.).
  • Assent. The plan names the student’s assent plan (Form SI-1) and says what staff do if the student withdraws assent (BACB, 2020, Code 2.11; Morris, Detrick, & Peterson, 2021; Breaux & Smith, 2023). It records whether the student’s ways of saying no include the target behavior; when they do, the plan says which rule staff follow. Withdrawals of assent bring a review after 2 sessions in a row, or at the assent plan’s own review point if that comes first.
  • Consent. Escape extinction is its own consent item, and the agreement names the guidance answer: with or without hands-on guidance. The parent or guardian is told what staff will do, that the behavior may get worse for a while before it gets better, what else could be done instead, and that they can stop their agreement at any time. If the guidance answer changes after they agreed, the plan asks for their agreement again (Code 2.11). Until the parent and the team agree, the plan says not to start it, and the final plan prints a line for the parent’s initials.
  • Staff who see only the brief. The brief for limited-contact staff (Form EB-1) reads “What staff do not do”, not this block, so the block offers to add the hands-off rule there.
The words the form fills in are its defaults: each says so until it is edited or ticked “Reviewed for this student”. Restraint-like items and protective equipment (the C_SR, SAFE and SEXT cards) carry a caution: staff never hold the student, and never put on or fasten an item that stops the student moving, to stop the behavior or to make the student do something. Equipment is used only as a doctor or therapist prescribed or recommended it for this student, and the crisis plan only when someone is about to be seriously hurt. These are practice rules of this form.

3. Attention- and Tangible-Maintained Behavior

EvidenceFCT (Carr & Durand, 1985) is the most published function-based treatment. Hagopian, Fisher, Sullivan, Acquisto, and LeBlanc (1998) summarized 21 inpatient cases: FCT without extinction produced a 90% reduction in 0 of 11 applications; with extinction, in 11 of 25; adding a punishment component to the 14 failures produced a 90% reduction in all 14. Worsdell, Iwata, Hanley, Thompson, and Kahng (2000) and Fisher et al. (1993) likewise found FCT without extinction often insufficient. When extinction cannot be arranged, differential reinforcement can still work if the alternative response produces reinforcement that is denser, longer, higher quality, or more immediate than the problem behavior produces (Horner & Day, 1991; Kelley, Lerman, & Van Camp, 2002; Athens & Vollmer, 2010). Noncontingent reinforcement reduced attention-maintained self-injury as quickly as DRO with fewer side effects (Vollmer, Iwata, Zarcone, Smith, & Mazaleski, 1993) and is best started at a dense schedule and thinned (Hagopian, Fisher, & Legacy, 1994); Carr, Severtson, and Lepper (2009) classified NCR as an empirically supported treatment. Hanley, Piazza, Fisher, Contrucci, and Maglieri (1997) found that participants preferred FCT to NCR and extinction when given a choice. Schedule thinning after FCT: an abrupt shift from continuous reinforcement to delays or intermittent reinforcement re-evoked problem behavior (Fisher, Thompson, Hagopian, Bowman, & Krug, 2000; Hanley, Iwata, & Thompson, 2001); a multiple schedule with signaled reinforcement and extinction periods maintained the mand with reinforcement available only 20% of the time (Hanley et al., 2001), and Greer, Fisher, Saini, Owen, and Jones (2016) summarized 25 consecutive applications of thinning that used discriminative stimuli to signal when the FCR would not be reinforced (multiple schedules, response restriction, or chained schedules) and concluded that the signals helped maintain the effectiveness of FCT while minimizing the need for punishment or other supplemental procedures. Hagopian, Boelter, and Jarmolowicz (2011) reviewed thinning tactics and recommended signaled schedules with alternative reinforcers available during extinction components.
Applied guidanceThe framework on the selection sheet asks, in order: can the reinforcer be withheld consistently and safely (extinction feasible); must behavior be suppressed at once; does the student already have a recognizable request; and is contingent delivery on every request tolerable in the setting. Extinction feasible and immediate suppression needed → NCR at a dense schedule with extinction, thinned, then FCT (Goh et al., 2000 caution applies to rich NCR). Extinction feasible and no request in repertoire → FCT with extinction on continuous reinforcement, then multiple-schedule thinning. Request already in repertoire → DRA of that response with extinction (Winborn, Wacker, Richman, Asmus, & Geier, 2002). Extinction not feasible → DRA or FCT with reinforcement parameters favoring the alternative, or NCR without extinction (Lalli, Casey, & Kates, 1997). DRO is reserved for cases with no alternative response to reinforce (Vollmer & Iwata, 1992).
Evidence: component functional analysisBann and Morris (2026) evaluated a component FA that introduces establishing operations sequentially and additively (attention restricted, tangibles restricted, instructions presented) and delivers the matching consequences in reverse order, against an extended multielement FA, in nine clinic-referred children (4–13 years) with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Component-FA evocative-effect probabilities predicted multielement FA outcomes with AUC .94–1.0 and, at the optimal cutoffs with four sessions, 100% sensitivity/specificity (evocative) and 87.5% sensitivity/100% specificity (reinforcement). The component FA averaged 20 min per session and 76 min in total versus 92 min for the multielement FA. FCT + DRA built on the identified function reduced challenging behavior by 95% (Axel) to 100% (seven participants) relative to the MEFA test condition. The component FA cannot detect automatic reinforcement or idiosyncratic functions; a no-interaction screener should precede it. The cutoffs were derived on that sample and have not been applied prospectively, and the sample held no isolated attention function, one escape function, two synthesized functions, and no automatic function, so the method is not validated for those outcomes; the authors state it should not be the sole basis for a function conclusion.
Applied guidanceWhen the function came from a component FA, the setup sheet records the sessions completed, the probabilities, whether a no-interaction screener preceded it, and the extended-MEFA confirmation (SVI quotient); the last question on the escape, attention, and tangible selection models adds the FCT_CFA card, which keys the communication response to the confirmed isolated or synthesized reinforcer (both reinforcers together for a synthesized function) and sets the outcome benchmark from the source. The procedural details of that study's FCT and DRA are in its Supporting Information and are not reproduced; the FCT card supplies the procedure. Schedule thinning, generalization, and maintenance were not evaluated there, so they are planned from the implementation-plan sheet.

4. Automatically Maintained Behavior and the Hagopian Subtypes

EvidenceHagopian, Rooker, and Zarcone (2015) subtyped automatically maintained self-injury from the functional-analysis pattern: Subtype 1, differentiated (higher in the no-interaction condition than in play); Subtype 2, undifferentiated (comparable across play and no-interaction); Subtype 3, co-occurring self-restraint. Reinforcement-based treatment (NCR, DRA, DRO) was effective for most Subtype 1 cases and comparatively ineffective for Subtype 2; Hagopian, Rooker, Zarcone, Bonner, and Arevalo (2017) replicated this across 49 published cases. Hagopian, Rooker, and Yenokyan (2018) found that the level of differentiation (LOD = 1 − mean play rate ÷ mean no-interaction rate, × 100) predicted response to reinforcement alone at a cutoff of 63.7%. Hagopian, Falligant, Frank-Crawford, Yenokyan, Piersma, and Kaur (2023) simplified subtyping: after visual analysis identifies the behavior as automatically maintained (97% accurate against structured criteria), classify Subtype 1 if LOD ≥ 62.5%, Subtype 2 if LOD < 62.5%, Subtype 3 if self-restraint occurs in more than 25% of intervals across three no-interaction series; the cutoff classified 98 of 99 cases correctly and requires a rigorous analysis with at least three series. Rooker, Hagopian, Haddock, Mezhoudi, and Arevalo (2019) found that three individuals with Subtype 2 self-injury, like three with socially reinforced self-injury, were sensitive to changing reinforcement schedules on a single-operant task, preliminary support that the insensitivity is a property of the self-injury response class rather than of the person. Contrucci Kuhn and Triggs (2009) showed that when self-injury is high across every condition, protective equipment can attenuate the automatic reinforcer so that social functions can be re-tested: after alone sessions under a helmet extinguished the behavior, contingent attention re-evoked it, revealing an attention function that the original analysis had masked; Borrero et al. (2002), Le and Smith (2002), and Moore, Fisher, and Pennington (2004) cautioned that protective equipment during assessment can suppress responding and hide functions. Matched stimulation competed with automatically maintained behavior better than unmatched preferred items (Piazza, Adelinis, Hanley, Goh, & Delia, 2000); environmental enrichment and competing-item access reduced hand mouthing and self-injury after inconclusive analyses (Vollmer, Marcus, & LeBlanc, 1994; Ringdahl, Vollmer, Marcus, & Roane, 1997; Shore, Iwata, DeLeon, Kahng, & Smith, 1997), with effects that varied across brief and extended applications (Lindberg et al., 2003). Hagopian et al. (2020) described an augmented competing-stimulus assessment (prompted engagement and blocking during the assessment) for cases in which the standard assessment identifies few or no competing items: in 6 cases with treatment-resistant subtypes of automatically maintained behavior, where it had identified 0 or 1, the number of competing items increased in all 6. Sensory extinction reduced stereotypy when the product could be identified (Rincover, 1978); response interruption and redirection reduced vocal stereotypy (Ahearn et al., 2007). Fisher and Iwata (1996) analyzed the relation between self-restraint and self-injury; Powers, Roane, and Kelley (2007) treated self-restraint that emerged with protective equipment.
Applied guidanceThe selection sheet computes the LOD and self-restraint level from the EA-1 play and no-interaction means and classifies the subtype with the 2023 cutoffs; the classification is confirmed by visual inspection of the EA-1 graph and is not applied to an analysis with fewer than three series. Subtype 1 → competing-stimulus assessment from PA-1 items, noncontingent matched stimulation, DRA of an alternative producing similar stimulation, DRO where needed. Subtype 2 → the same reinforcement components are still built, but the plan does not rely on them: an augmented competing-stimulus assessment, protective procedures with medical oversight, sensory extinction where the product is identifiable, RIRD or blocking, a re-test of social functions under protective equipment when the behavior was high across every condition (Contrucci Kuhn & Triggs, 2009), and consultation with the prescriber about the medical evaluation of self-injury; other skills in the plan are taught with ordinary reinforcement (Rooker et al., 2019). Subtype 3 → assessment of the self-restraint–self-injury relation and specialist consultation before restraint is faded or removed, with any alternative restraint-like items introduced on a plan. Mixed automatic and social control → treat each function; the helmet re-test identifies which social consequences to withhold.
Evidence: arranging and ordering (higher-level RRB)Fergus et al. (2026) replicated the functional-analysis-to-treatment sequence for arranging and ordering displayed by four males with autism (13–22 years) in a residential school and extended it with the augmented competing-stimulus assessment of Hagopian et al. (2020). An FA can distinguish automatically maintained from socially mediated arranging/ordering: four referrals showed automatic functions and three showed escape or attention functions. Providing three effective competing stimuli (ECS; an item associated with at least an 80% reduction in RRB relative to a no-stimulus control) identified via an A-CSA reduced arranging/ordering to zero or near-zero for all four participants, with the required support level (none, prompting, or prompting + blocking) determined by the A-CSA phase in which the stimuli competed. Response blocking was withdrawn for both participants who needed it, and RRB stayed at zero (Adam) or was re-reduced with contingent prompts alone (Mack); no emotional responses were recorded in any treatment session for Adam or Mack. The process-versus-product analysis classified three participants as product-maintained and one as process-maintained, but its results did not inform treatment, and the authors listed the feasibility limits of proactive placement (shared spaces, changing materials, time, unknown durability, single-location assessment). Not shown: maintenance over time, generalization across therapists (one participant), social validity, procedural fidelity, or effects on collateral behavior such as bolting or pica.
Applied guidanceWhen the target is arranging/ordering assessed with the EA-1 arranging/ordering variant, the selection sheet does not apply the self-injury subtype branches; it reads the A-CSA phase from the setup sheet and prescribes the level: ECS only, ECS + prompted engagement, or ECS + prompted engagement + response blocking (RRB_ECS, RRB_PE, RRB_RB on the build sheet). The Antecedents sheet planner holds the three ECS, the prompt and blocking rules, the step-down rule (withdraw blocking after stable zero or near-zero RRB; contingent prompts before reinstating it; the stable criterion is a working convention of this form), generalization probes with the least restrictive effective package, and the measures in the source's units (episodes per minute, functional item engagement, prompts and blocks per session, emotional responses). Preferred-product placement is listed as an antecedent option with the source's feasibility caveats, not as a demonstrated treatment.

5. Building, Teaching, Thinning, Generalizing, and Checking the Treatment

EvidenceTiger et al. (2008) set best-practice guidelines for FCT: select a response that is recognizable and quickly acquired and less effortful than the problem behavior (Horner & Day, 1991; Durand & Carr, 1992; Durand, 1999); begin with a trained implementer in a safe controlled setting; arrange many teaching opportunities with contrived establishing operations; prompt with most-to-least (Fisher et al., 1993) or least-to-most with progressive delay (Shirley, Iwata, Kahng, Mazaleski, & Lerman, 1997); reinforce every response initially; withhold reinforcement for problem behavior; then thin, generalize across trainers, settings, and common stimuli (Stokes & Baer, 1977; Durand & Carr, 1991), train caregivers, and increase response complexity. Harding et al. (2009) described the parent-implemented version: play before work so the break is valuable, a visual work area and a "play" card on a microswitch, a two-step chain (complete the task, then mand), work requirements raised from one to two to four blocks per trial as Lalli, Casey, and Kates (1995) had done, three returns to baseline over 7 months, and generalized improvement reported on a behavior rating scale; they recommended baseline probes in two or three additional settings before FCT and probes after. Wacker et al. (2005) reported average reductions of 93% in the training context, 81% across persons, 90% across settings, and 73% across tasks. St. Peter Pipkin, Vollmer, and Sloman (2010) showed that integrity failures that reinforce the problem behavior are more detrimental than failures to reinforce the alternative; Vollmer et al. (1999) found DRA effects largely robust to such challenges after full implementation, with their negative effects quickly reversible. Parsons, Rollyson, and Reid (2012) summarized behavioral skills training (instructions, modeling, rehearsal, feedback) as the evidence-based staff-training method. Reimers and Wacker (1988) developed the Treatment Acceptability Rating Form used by Harding et al. Lerman, Iwata, and Wallace (1999) found extinction bursts or increases in aggression in nearly half of 41 data sets, and substantially less often when extinction was part of a treatment package than when it was the sole intervention.
Applied guidanceEach component card on the build sheet carries its implementation steps and parameters; the implementation-plan sheet assembles the training checklist, the integrity checklist (one item per step), the thinning plan, the generalization probes, and the acceptability ratings; the monitoring sheet scores integrity per session and applies the thinning rule (advance when problem behavior is at or below the criterion and the alternative response is independent for three consecutive sessions at the current level; step back one level after two consecutive sessions above criterion at that level). These decision rules are practice rules of this form.

6. Reinforcers, Schedules, Prompts, and Errors

EvidenceDeLeon, Graff, Frank-Crawford, Rooker, and Bullock (2014) reviewed reinforcer identification and arrangement: paired-stimulus assessment produced more differentiated hierarchies than single-stimulus (Fisher et al., 1992); MSWO matched paired-stimulus in about half the time (DeLeon & Iwata, 1996); free-operant assessment was fastest and evoked the least problem behavior but was less likely to yield a hierarchy (Roane et al., 1998); tangible-maintained behavior was evoked by PS and MSWO but not free-operant, and attention-maintained behavior by free-operant (Kang et al., 2011); food displaced leisure items in mixed arrays (DeLeon et al., 1997b); access duration in the assessment changed which items won (Steinhilber & Johnson, 2007); preferences stayed stable for some individuals and shifted for others over months (Zhou et al., 2001), and when a daily brief MSWO and the pretreatment assessment disagreed, responding generally favored the daily top item (DeLeon et al., 2001); noncontingent delivery lowered later preference for the item, although its progressive-ratio break points still rose on average, the least of four conditions (DeLeon et al., 2011b); accumulated access matched or exceeded distributed access and was preferred (DeLeon et al., 2014; Fienup et al., 2011); and their flowchart orders social, then non-edible tangible, then edible reinforcers, each tested under stringent conditions and within a token system. Schedule definitions follow Catania (2007) and Ferster and Skinner (1957); multiple-schedule thinning after Hanley, Iwata, and Thompson (2001); lag schedules after Page and Neuringer (1985); progressive-ratio assessment after Hodos (1961) and Roane, Lerman, and Vorndran (2001). Most-to-least prompting produced fewer errors while least-to-most was sometimes faster (Libby, Weiss, Bancroft, & Ahearn, 2008); progressive delay transferred control with few errors (Touchette & Howard, 1984); prompt-type effectiveness varied by learner (Seaver & Bourret, 2014). Error-correction effectiveness and efficiency also varied by learner, and a brief assessment identified the best procedure (McGhan & Lerman, 2013; Carroll, Joachim, St. Peter, & Robinson, 2015).
Applied guidanceThe reinforcers-and-schedules sheet walks the DeLeon flowchart, chooses the preference-assessment method from the student's scanning, motor, matching, and position-bias status and the function of the behavior, lists the parameters to set (delay, rate, quality, magnitude, continuity, MOs, variation, re-assessment, validation, separate classes), and selects the schedule from the goal of the arrangement. The prompting-and-errors sheet chooses the hierarchy, the fading method, and the error-correction procedure, with an assessment recommended whenever the questions do not settle it. The responding sheet writes the consequence protocol matched to function with the crisis procedure kept separate. All selector rankings are practice rules of this form; each option names its source.

7. Skill-Based Treatment, Tolerance, Self-Control, Mands, and Correspondence

EvidenceHanley, Jin, Vanselow, and Hanratty (2014) treated three children after a synthesized analysis by teaching a simple functional communication response for the synthesized reinforcers, then a more complex, polite form, then a tolerance response to denial, and then contextually appropriate behaviors (relinquishing items, transitioning, completing instructions) in progressively longer chains with unpredictable reinforcement; problem behavior fell to near zero, and effects were extended to the children's homes and, for one child, to school. Ghaemmaghami, Hanley, and Jessel (2016) showed that progressively delaying reinforcement while teaching tolerance and reinforcing engagement in alternative activities during the delay produced delay tolerance with problem behavior near zero (terminal delays of 3 to 10 min). Jessel, Ingvarsson, Metras, Kirk, and Whipple (2018) reported socially significant reductions across 25 outpatient applications of the interview-informed analysis and functional communication training with contingency-based reinforcement thinning, and Santiago, Hanley, Moore, and Jin (2016) replicated the approach in school and home settings. Rajaraman et al. (2022) framed the enhanced-choice model, in which the learner can opt into or out of treatment sessions, as a trauma-informed variation. Self-control training: Schweitzer and Sulzer-Azaroff (1988) taught impulsive children to choose a larger delayed reinforcer by gradually increasing the delay; Dixon et al. (1998) and Binder, Dixon, and Ghezzi (2000) added a concurrent choice with progressive delay; Dixon, Rehfeldt, and Randich (2003) found that an intervening activity during the delay increased tolerance. Correspondence training: Risley and Hart (1968) reinforced correspondence between what preschoolers said they would do and what they did; Israel and O'Leary (1973) found say-then-do sequences more effective than do-then-say; Baer, Detrich, and Weninger (1988) analyzed the function of the verbalization; Lloyd (2002) reviewed the procedure. Mand training: Skinner (1957) defined the mand as verbal behavior under the control of a motivating operation and specific reinforcement; Michael (1988) tied mand training to establishing operations; Hall and Sundberg (1987) contrived establishing operations by interrupting behavior chains; Sundberg and Michael (2001) argued for a mand-first curriculum in autism; Sweeney-Kerwin et al. (2007) transferred control of the mand from prompts to the motivating operation; Albert, Carbone, Murray, Hagerty, and Sweeney-Kerwin (2012) increased mands with an interrupted-chain procedure; Carbone, Sweeney-Kerwin, Attanasio, and Kasper (2010) increased vocalizations with sign mand training and prompt delay. Curriculum-linked assessment: the VB-MAPP (Sundberg, 2008) organizes verbal operants, barriers, and transition skills, with content validity reported by Padilla and Akers (2021); Essential for Living (McGreevy, Fry, & Cornwall, 2012) organizes must-have skills (requesting, waiting, accepting removals and transitions, completing tasks, accepting "no," and health and safety routines) for learners with moderate to severe disabilities; the AFLS (Partington & Mueller, 2012) covers basic, home, community, school, vocational, and independent-living skills; PEAK (Dixon, 2014) extends verbal-behavior programming to derived relational responding, with reported relations to the VB-MAPP (Dixon et al., 2015) and controlled trials of its modules (McKeel, Dixon, Daar, Rowsey, & Szekely, 2015).
Applied guidanceThe skills sheet builds the skill-based treatment sequence when the synthesized analysis was used or when the plan needs tolerance for delay and denial: simple FCR, complex FCR, tolerance response, then contextually appropriate behaviors chained under gradually lengthened delays and probabilistic reinforcement, with the enhanced-choice option recorded. Self-control training is used when the goal is choosing larger delayed reinforcers; correspondence training when the student can state a plan and the goal is self-management; mand training with contrived establishing operations for acquisition and natural establishing operations for generalization, transferring control from prompts to the motivating operation. Curriculum instruments are used to select and sequence replacement and skill targets and to record barriers, not as treatments; the peer-reviewed support for each is stated on its card.

8. Antecedent Interventions

EvidenceSmith and Iwata (1997) reviewed antecedent influences on behavior disorders and the establishing-operation account of why removing or adding events before behavior changes it; Kern, Choutka, and Sokol (2002) found that assessment-based antecedent interventions in natural settings reduced challenging behavior in most published cases, and Kern and Clemens (2007) summarized classroom antecedent strategies. Proximity. Oliver, Oxener, Hearn, and Hall (2001) manipulated staff proximity for one individual and found aggression high when staff were close and near zero at a distance, with continuous attention in both conditions; proximity was itself the evocative event. Rapport and pairing. McLaughlin and Carr (2005) treated rapport as a setting event: poor-rapport staff evoked more problem behavior, and a rapport-building intervention reduced it; Kelly, Axe, Allen, and Maguire (2015) found less challenging behavior and better academic responding in sessions preceded by a 2- to 4-min pairing session; Lugo, King, Lamphere, and McArdle (2017) operationalized pairing behaviors (proximity, praise, imitation, reflecting, offering items, novel play); Shillingsburg, Hansen, and Wright (2019) described fading from child-led play to instruction. Attention through the day. Noncontingent attention reduced attention-maintained behavior (Vollmer et al., 1993; Hagopian, Fisher, & Legacy, 1994), and presession attention or access lowered the value of the reinforcer and the behavior it maintained (Berg et al., 2000; McGinnis, Houchins-Juárez, McDaniel, & Kennedy, 2010; Rispoli et al., 2011). Exercise. Kern, Koegel, Dyer, Blew, and Fenton (1982) and Kern, Koegel, and Dunlap (1984) found vigorous (not mild) exercise reduced stereotypy and increased appropriate responding afterward; Lang et al. (2010) reviewed 18 studies with generally positive effects; Neely, Rispoli, Gerow, and Ninci (2015) found the effect strongest when exercise continued until an individualized satiation indicator. Instruction. High-probability request sequences increased compliance with low-probability requests (Mace et al., 1988; Ducharme & Worling, 1994); interspersing easy requests reduced aggression and self-injury during instruction (Horner, Day, Sprague, O'Brien, & Heathfield, 1991) and improved acquisition (Neef, Iwata, & Page, 1980); faster teacher presentation reduced off-task behavior (Carnine, 1976). Predictability. Activity schedules built independent chains (MacDuff, Krantz, & McClannahan, 1993); predictable schedules reduced problem behavior (Flannery & Horner, 1994); advance notice of transitions reduced stereotypy (Tustin, 1995) and, with function-based components, transition tantrums (Wilder, Chen, Atwell, Pritchard, & Weinstein, 2006); priming reduced problem behavior and improved responding in class (Koegel, Koegel, Frea, & Green-Hopkins, 2003). Setting events. Sleep deprivation and allergy symptoms increased escape-maintained behavior (Kennedy & Meyer, 1996); preceding setting events were related to higher rates of problem behavior, and interventions that eliminated them were associated with low rates (Kennedy & Itkonen, 1993); neutralizing routines after a setting event reduced it (Horner, Day, & Day, 1997). Seating. Row seating produced less disruptive behavior than clusters during independent work (Bicard, Ervin, Bicard, & Baylot-Casey, 2012; Wannarka & Ruhl, 2008). High-probability sequences. Lemons and Wilder (2026) compared varied-order and constant-order three-instruction high-p sequences in three young children with autism: both forms increased cooperation with the low-p instruction for all three, the varied order was more effective for one and equally effective for the other two, and the authors recommend using varied high-p instructions or assessing the two formats with each learner; the Antecedents sheet carries a planner with that study's parameters.

High-Probability Sequence Planner (Lemons & Wilder, 2026)

The high-probability instructional sequence (HPIS) is an antecedent-based intervention used to increase cooperation with low-probability tasks: a series of instructions with a high probability of cooperation is delivered immediately before an instruction with a low probability of cooperation (Lemons & Wilder, 2026; Mace et al., 1988). In Lemons and Wilder (2026), high-p instructions were those with at least 80% cooperation and low-p instructions those with less than 40% cooperation across 10 randomly ordered probe trials, and cooperation was defined as initiating the task within 10 s of the instruction. Three single-step high-p instructions were delivered 1–3 s apart with brief praise for each; the low-p instruction was delivered within 3 s of cooperation with the third high-p instruction and only after cooperation with three consecutive high-p instructions, and noncooperation with a high-p instruction led to a 30-s pause and a new high-p sequence. Both varied-order and constant-order sequences increased cooperation with the low-p instruction for all three children with autism in that study; a varied order was more effective than a constant order for one of the three participants and equally effective for the other two, and across Lemons and Wilder (2026) and Davis and Reichle (1996), 5 of 7 participants cooperated more under varied than constant high-p sequences. The authors recommend either using varied high-p instructions or assessing varied versus constant formats with each learner, for example with a brief probe-trial preassessment. Longer intervals between the high-p instructions and the low-p instruction may reduce HPIS effectiveness (Pitts & Dymond, 2012, as cited in Lemons & Wilder, 2026). This planner therefore defaults to a varied order, and the study's timing values are offered as starting values, not as tested optima. The trial sheet under the planner records each session's five trials (high-p 1 to 3 and the low-p, cooperated or not, and the order used) and computes the low-p cooperation per session against the 40% identification figure and each high-p instruction's cooperation against the 80% re-verify rule.

Scope of the evidenceLemons and Wilder (2026) studied three clinic-based children with autism under age 7 with an adult experimenter and one brief motor or transition low-p instruction per child (jump, touch yogurt, get lunchbox); no functional assessment was conducted, noncooperation with the low-p instruction ended the trial, and maintenance, generalization, and social validity were not assessed. The HPIS has been reported to be ineffective in some cases (Rortvedt & Miltenberger, 1994; Silbaugh & Swinnea, 2019; Wilder et al., 2007, as cited in Lemons & Wilder, 2026). The 80% / 40% criteria are that study's operational definitions, and the consequence for low-p noncooperation in this plan is set from the function and the Responding sheet, not from the study.

For higher-level restricted and repetitive behavior (arranging and ordering) with an automatic function from the EA-1 arranging/ordering variant. Scope of the evidenceFergus et al.
