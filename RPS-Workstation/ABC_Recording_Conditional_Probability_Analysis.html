Brief Incident Description

Keep it to observable events in order. What was happening immediately before · what the student did · what adults and peers did next · what happened to the demand, item, or attention · how it ended. Avoid inferred states ("frustrated," "attention seeking") — those are hypotheses, and the analysis tab is where they get tested. Bijou, Peterson, and Ault (1968) set the standard the field still uses: even words like ignores and disappointed stay out of the record. Where the episode ran in a chain, number the steps — a consequence for one response is often the antecedent for the next.

Background (response-independent) samples

Optional, but required for contingency strength

A conditional probability on its own cannot tell you whether a consequence is contingent on behavior, only that it followed behavior. If staff attention follows 80% of incidents but is also delivered continuously all day, there is no differential contingency. To estimate one you need a base rate: how often the same consequence occurs when the target behavior has not just occurred.

Take a sample on a timer — every 5 or 10 minutes, or at a fixed number of momentary checks per period. The Observation timer on the Record tab runs this schedule for you: it alerts you at each check, skips a check when an incident was logged in its window, and fills in the date, time and window length below. At each check, if the target behavior has not occurred in the preceding window, record which of these events happened in that window. Skip the check if the behavior did occur; that window is already captured as an incident.

S. What Every Observation Has in Common

Read once; sections A to E build on it

Every observation with this form has the same frame: one observer, placed to see the student and the adults and taking no part; a target behavior defined before the first session and never changed; the Observation header; and the Observation timer running from start to finish. Inside that frame you make two kinds of record: incidents and background samples.

S. The Observer, Step by Step

    TeacherStudentPeerObserverThe observer’s screen, drawn large

    What You Say and Do

    • Before you start, ask staff to carry on exactly as they would without you. Then say nothing to the student or to staff until the observation ends.
    • Start the Observation timer when the observation starts, and let it run to the end.
    • Keep the definition, the consequence window and the check interval the same in every session.

    What Goes on the Sheet

    • The Observation header: Student / client, Observer, Setting / location, Observation start and end, Target behavior under assessment, Consequence window and Background check interval. It travels with the file and changes no calculation; the timer takes the check interval as its setting.
    • Recording format: Coded + narrative, unless a second observer is running narrative alongside you or the codes genuinely capture what happened.
    • At the end: Save data on the Data file tab. Handle the file as a protected student record.
    Decision. Fix three things before the first observation and hold them for every session and every observer: the target behavior’s definition, the consequence window, and the check interval. Two observers, a fixed target definition, and a fixed consequence window matter more to the quality of these numbers than the number of incidents collected.

    A. Logging an Incident

    Record tab · New incident · one record per discrete occurrence

    An incident is one discrete occurrence of the target behavior. The antecedent is what was in place immediately before it; lower-intensity responses in the seconds before go under Preceded by…; the consequence is what happened within a short, consistent window after it. The example: a math demand, a refusal, a hit, and the demand withdrawn.

    A. At the Table, Step by Step

      What You Say and Do

      • Nothing to the student or the teacher. Record what the adults do; do not step in.
      • Press Incident starting as the behavior begins and Incident over when it has ended.
      • Hold the consequence codes until the consequence window has closed, then code what happened inside it.

      What Goes on the Sheet

      • Antecedent: what was in place immediately before the behavior (Presented with academic demand), not what you believe the student wanted.
      • Behavior: the code the definition names (Physical aggression to staff), with the lower-intensity responses under Preceded by…: Verbal refusal (“no”, “I'm not doing it”), Pushing materials away.
      • Consequence: what happened within the window (Demand removed/avoided, Given staff attention), then Immediately after the consequence.
      • The narrative, Activity / routine, Adult within view and any setting events reported; then Save incident.
      Why. The codes carry the arithmetic; the narrative carries what the codes flatten: the exact wording of a demand, who was standing where, what the student said, and anything that did not fit a box. Where the episode ran in a chain, number the steps: a consequence for one response is often the antecedent for the next.

      B. A Background Check

      Background samples tab · at every alert of the Observation timer

      A background sample is a window in which the target behavior did not occur. Taken on the timer through the same periods as the incidents, the samples give the base rate: how often each condition and each consequence occurs when the behavior has not just happened. This is the step teams skip, and skipping it reduces an ABC record to a tally of what adults do all day.

      B. At the Check, Step by Step

        What You Say and Do

        • Nothing. The check is yours alone; with flash and vibration the room need not know it happened.
        • Take the check when the alert comes, and look back over the window that just ended.
        • If the target behavior occurred in the window, skip the check; if that incident is not logged yet, log it first.

        What Goes on the Sheet

        • Date, Time and Window length (min), filled by the timer.
        • Antecedent conditions in place during this window, and Events that occurred during this window, whatever they followed.
        • Lower-intensity responses seen during this window, Adult within view, and Setting events in effect today.
        • Save sample, even when every box is empty: an empty sample is still data.
        Why. A conditional probability on its own cannot tell you whether a consequence is contingent on behavior, only that it followed behavior. If staff attention follows 80% of incidents but is also delivered continuously all day, there is no differential contingency. The background samples supply the base rate, and the analysis holds out for at least 10 of them before it reports contingency strength.

        C. Coding Calls That Change the Result

        Antecedent, consequence and setting-event codes · the reasons are on the Methods tab

        Each of these codes was added beyond the original sheet because it marks a distinction the original collapsed, and collapsing it changes what the analysis can find. The drawings show the moment; the line under each one shows the code exactly as the form names it.

        C. Seven Calls, One at a Time

          What You Say and Do

          • Nothing different: these are calls you make while recording.
          • When a code fits, check it, even if it is not the one you expected. When none fits, use Other and describe the event.
          • Train the codes the referral question needs. An unchecked box costs nothing; a box checked inconsistently costs the analysis.

          What Goes on the Sheet

          • “In a minute”: Told to wait; access delayed. “No”: Denied access to something.
          • Attention going to someone else: Adult attention directed to a peer or another adult. Attention simply ending: Removal of staff attention.
          • Presented with transition demand, with each component in place: Transition: preferred activity ended, Transition: non-preferred activity beginning, Transition: movement between locations.
          • Behavior that starts before the instruction: Signal that a demand or transition is coming.
          • A specific noise: Aversive noise or sensory stimulation.
          • Sleep, meals, the bus, earlier incidents: the setting-event sections, never the antecedent column.
          • Demand kept and task guided: Demand remained in place, Prompted or guided through the task. A reprimand: Reprimand or correction delivered.
          Decision. If you disagree with one of these calls for a particular student, the code map on the Methods tab shows the class each code carries, and the reasoning is there to argue with. Whatever you decide, decide it before the first observation and train it: a sheet scored at 95% agreement beats a fuller sheet scored at 70%.

          D. A Second Observer

          At least a third of sessions · Methods tab, Interobserver agreement

          Compute interobserver agreement before you trust anything on the Analysis tab. A second observer records the same period independently and the two records are compared incident by incident. The form scores each unit as a match or a mismatch and divides agreements by agreements plus disagreements, separately for occurrence and for nonoccurrence, because a single overall figure hides the common failure.

          D. Two Observers, Step by Step

            What You Say and Do

            • Before the session, agree on the definition, the codes, the check interval, and how close two start times must be to count as one incident.
            • During it, no talking and no looking at the other screen.
            • After it, compare the two data files incident by incident and window by window.

            What Goes on the Sheet

            • Each observer saves a separate data file.
            • The occurrence units are the incidents either observer logged; the nonoccurrence units are the check windows either observer sampled.
            • The four counts under Interobserver agreement: Occurrence agreements, Occurrence disagreements, Nonoccurrence agreements, Nonoccurrence disagreements.
            • For each incident both observers logged, compare the codes too: nothing on the form corrects for a coding disagreement.
            Decision. Take a second observer for at least a third of sessions. Read occurrence agreement before overall agreement: when the behavior is rare, two observers can disagree about almost every occurrence and still post a high overall figure. If the occurrence figure is the weak one, the problem is almost always the response definition rather than the observer.

            E. Reading the Analysis

            Analysis tab · follow along with Load simulation on the Data file tab

            Load simulation on the Data file tab to follow along: 46 fabricated incidents and 60 background samples built around a known escape-maintained pattern. These are not real observations and must never be presented as student data. Every figure in this section is the simulated record’s.

            E. On the Analysis Tab, Step by Step

              What You Say and Do

              • Load simulation on the Data file tab to see these numbers; with your own file, read the same cards in the same order.
              • Narrow the record with the filters at the top of the Analysis tab (behavior, dates, setting); Reset returns to all of it.
              • Set Function established by FA only when a functional analysis has established it.

              What Goes on the Sheet

              • Print analysis prints the record with the analysis as it stands.
              • In the report: the ranking, as the conditions to arrange in a functional analysis and the reinforcer to program in the test condition.
              • Not a function statement in a behavior plan.
              Decision. Descriptive assessment suggests a function; it does not confirm one. A low number is not evidence of no relation either: a consequence delivered on a thin schedule can maintain the behavior with a low conditional probability. The descriptive finding that carries the most weight is a record with no correlation to any social event.

              Terms, as the Sheet Uses Them

              For whoever is holding the tablet
              Antecedent
              What was in place immediately before the behavior, scored from what was there rather than from what you believe the student wanted.
              Consequence window
              The short, consistent window after the behavior in which consequences are recorded: a few seconds, held constant. Several published descriptive analyses used 10 s; others examined longer lags.
              Background (response-independent) sample
              A window taken on the timer in which the target behavior did not occur: the conditions in place, the events that occurred, the lower-intensity responses seen. A window that held the behavior is skipped; it is already an incident.
              Base rate, p(C|noB)
              c / M: of the M sampled windows without the behavior, the proportion in which the event occurred anyway. Without it the conditional probability cannot be interpreted.
              p(C|B)
              a / N: of the N recorded occurrences of the target behavior, the proportion followed by consequence C. The core measure of published descriptive analyses.
              p(B|C)
              a / (a + c): of all the occasions on which the event occurred, the proportion that involved the behavior. It separates an event that tracks the behavior from one staff deliver constantly for their own reasons.
              CP:UP
              p(C|B) divided by the unconditional probability p(C) = (a + c) / (N + M), the share of every sampled unit that held the event. In Camp et al. (2009) a retrospective review of a panel of behavior analysts’ consensus decisions showed they agreed a descriptive record showed a relation only where p(C|B) exceeded .20 and CP:UP was at least 1.23; the consequence table applies both.
              Contingency space
              p(C|B) plotted against p(C|noB), the diagonal marking independence. Distance above the diagonal is the visual form of Δp; below it, the event occurs mostly without the behavior.
              Δp (contingency difference)
              p(C|B) − p(C|noB): how much more often the consequence follows the behavior than it occurs without it. Zero means independence.
              Yule’s Q
              (ad − bc) / (ad + bc), bounded at ±1. It uses all four cells, so a consequence with a high base rate cannot inflate it. The strength words apply |Q| thresholds of .20, .43 and .60 (Rosenthal, 1996; Yoder, Lloyd, & Symons, 2018): negligible, weak, moderate, strong.
              Thin
              A 2×2 table with an empty cell or fewer than five observations on one side (a faded bar, or “thin” after the strength word), or an adjusted bar resting on fewer than five incidents.
              Opportunity-adjusted probability
              The conditional probability computed only across incidents in which the matching establishing operation was in place: escape counted only where an escape-class antecedent, such as a demand, preceded the behavior.
              Precursor
              A lower-intensity response recorded under Preceded by… A response whose p(response | target) exceeds its own background probability is a candidate; a functional analysis of the precursor confirms response-class membership.
              Setting event
              A distal condition, often hours earlier, that changes how much the consequences are worth. Recorded on incidents and on background samples, reported in its own panel, and never merged into the antecedent analysis.
              Motivating condition
              The reinforcer class whose value an antecedent code establishes, as the sheet groups them: attention, tangible/access, escape/avoidance, or low social input. Presented with unwanted attention and Loud/busy environment are classed as escape.
              Withheld
              A consequence item that describes a reinforcer class not being delivered: Demand remained in place, No attention provided, Prompted or guided through the task. Counted, but never pooled with delivery.
              Occurrence and nonoccurrence agreement
              Agreements divided by agreements plus disagreements, computed separately for occurrences and nonoccurrences, then overall. Read the occurrence figure first.
