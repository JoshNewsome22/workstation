Guide: Briefing Staff Who See the Student Briefly

1. Why This Is Not a Shorter Behavior Plan

A bus attendant, a music teacher and a lunchroom monitor are not running the intervention. They are trying not to make the situation worse, to recognize the request when it comes, and to know when to call. Handing them the plan, or a condensed version of it, produces one of two failures: they read none of it, or they attempt procedures they cannot run in their setting and run them badly.

So this document is selected, not summarized. Most of the plan is deliberately absent.

2. What Must Never Be Left Out

Two blocks earn their place on every brief regardless of setting. The first is what not to do: limited-contact staff rarely cause harm by omitting a procedure, and regularly cause harm by doing the one thing that strengthens the behavior — arguing, or removing the demand at the wrong moment — or by holding a student. A hold that uses significant physical force to restrict a student’s movement is physical restraint, which Florida law permits only for authorized school personnel, only when all positive behavior interventions and supports have been exhausted and there is an imminent risk of serious injury; seclusion is prohibited outright (§ 1003.573(1)(d)2., (2) and (3)(b), Florida Statutes). What trained staff do is on the crisis plan (Form CR-1). Unless everyone receiving the brief is named there as trained, the brief tells them when and whom to call, not how to hold. The second is the threshold for calling: a person who does not know when to call will either call for everything or for nothing.

3. Honor the Request Every Time

If the plan teaches a request, the people who see the student occasionally are the ones most likely to ignore it — they were not there when it was taught and do not know what it cost to establish. A request that works in the classroom and not on the bus is on extinction on the bus, and when a request taught by functional communication training stops working, the problem behavior it replaced tends to return (Volkert et al., 2009). If that behavior then gets the student what the request would have, it is being reinforced again. Say so on the page, in plain words: every time, even the third time.

The earliest sign is the best moment to act. Staff who see the student only briefly do most good by prompting the request when they see the first sign, before the behavior: precursors reliably come before severe behavior, and treating them reduces it (Fritz, Iwata, Hammond, & Bloom, 2013). So the brief says what to do when you see it, not only what it looks like. And when safety means the behavior still gets some of what it wants (the other student has to be moved), make the request pay more: a longer or better break, given sooner, so asking stays the better deal (Athens & Vollmer, 2010). Do not offer the card straight after the behavior, which can teach the chain behavior, card, break; offer it again at the next earliest sign.

4. Write for the Setting, Not for the Chart

A driver cannot leave the seat. A music teacher has thirty other students and no adult support. A brief that asks for something the setting cannot deliver is not a brief, it is a liability. Where the plan's procedure cannot run here, say what to do instead rather than leaving the line in.

5. Keep It to Pages, Not Sections

One page is better than two, and two are better than three. The length line on The brief view estimates the printed length by laying the brief out as it will print; the acknowledgment page is not counted, because it stays with you rather than with the reader.

6. Who May Receive It

This is part of the student's education record. It goes to staff with a legitimate educational interest in working with this student, and no further. Name the setting and the roles on the brief so that scope is on the page, and collect the acknowledgment so you know who holds a copy.

7. Check That It Was Understood

Signing shows the brief was received. The questions and the tries show it was understood. With teach-back on, each signature block carries three questions built from the brief (what you do at the earliest sign, what you do when the student asks, when you call and whom); the reviewer asks each one, explains a wrong answer again in other words and asks again until it is right, then ticks it. A third try, prompting the request at the earliest sign, joins the two of Show and try, and a short repeat 1 to 2 weeks later can be dated. Reading the written steps alone gave 30.5% integrity on a preference assessment, teach-back raised it to 91.1%, and brief feedback brought every participant to 100% (Sleiman et al., 2023); staff given only the written methods were 34% and 46% accurate (Graff & Karsten, 2012); written instructions alone gave weak and short-lived changes (Alavosius & Sulzer-Azaroff, 1990); and a memo alone did nothing (Quilitch, 1975).

A brief clarifies and prompts. If an adult has it, has answered the questions, and still does not do a step, the gap is probably elsewhere: training, materials, time, competing tasks, or no one noticing. Run Form PD-1 for that step instead of reissuing the brief (This adult is not doing a step, on the Acknowledgment view). Posted reminders and a written prompt were the interventions the Performance Diagnostic Checklist–Human Services did not point to, and they did not work (Bowe & Sellers, 2018; Ditzian et al., 2015). The other way round, when Form PD-1 finds that a substitute, a bus driver, a specials teacher or lunchroom staff needs clarification or a prompt, this brief is the one written for them; task clarification was part of the intervention the checklist pointed to, and it worked (Merritt et al., 2019).

Pictures and plain words. Up to three pictures with a caption each (the request card, the earliest sign, where the timer is) do more than another sentence: written steps with little jargon, pictures and worked examples were enough for most students and in-home technicians to run a preference assessment (Shapiro et al., 2016). Setup gives an estimated reading grade for the brief (the Flesch–Kincaid grade, from the words a sentence and the syllables a word; a heading, a list line or a box counts as a sentence, and syllables are estimated from the spelling; the acknowledgment page is left out): aim at or below the reader’s level; a low score does not make a page right. Training manuals ran from 7th-grade to college-graduate reading level (Andrasik & Murphy, 1977).

How it is going. An optional line, written again each time the brief is reissued: “Since [date], the request has been used about [n] times a day; thank you for honoring it on the bus.” Bus drivers trained in one session got brief progress updates now and then, and the gains held over three school years with the school running the program (Putnam et al., 2003); posted feedback raised what staff did where a memo and a workshop had not (Quilitch, 1975).

Where the behavior was assessed. When the behavior was not assessed in this setting, the brief says: if what you see here looks different, tell the contact. For one student, a functional analysis on the playground found adult attention and one in the classroom found tangible items, and the treatment matched to each setting worked better there (Lang et al., 2009). Bus and lunchroom staff are the people most likely to see a different pattern.

Writing help

A multi-line text box on this form shows an Improve wording button at its corner when you are in it and whenever it holds text; from a keyboard, Alt+Enter in the box (Option+Return on a Mac or iPad) opens the same panel. Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print. Concise keeps every fact in fewer words, which suits a brief that has to fit a page.
