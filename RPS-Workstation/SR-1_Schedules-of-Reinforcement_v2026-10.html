The Catalogue

Every schedule the research names, in five families. Each entry gives the notation, the definition in the words of the source that defined it, how the schedule is programmed, what it does to behavior, where the applied literature has used it, what goes wrong, and a school example. The last line of each entry is a notes box for this program’s own use of the schedule; the notes save with the form. Open an entry by clicking its name; print opens them all.

What the Records Look Like

Stylized cumulative records: time runs left to right, each response steps the line up, and each short diagonal mark is a reinforcer. They are drawn by rule from the descriptions in Ferster and Skinner (1957) and the sources in the catalogue, not traced from data, and they show the shape to expect from each schedule once it has taken hold. Human performances with verbal rules can look flatter than these (Catania, Matthews & Shimoff, 1982).

Reading a Record

SlopeRate. A steep line is a high rate; a flat line is a pause. Compare slopes, not heights.
Pause after the markThe post-reinforcement pause of fixed schedules: the flat stretch right after each reinforcer, longer as the requirement grows.
Curve into the markThe scallop of fixed-interval schedules: a gradual steepening as the time approaches, then the reinforcer.
Straight line through the marksVariable schedules: no feature of the schedule predicts the next reinforcer, so nothing pauses.
Steps of equal height, widely spacedSpaced-responding DRL: single responses separated by at least the criterion time, each one paid.
Runs with growing gaps, then a flat lineA progressive ratio approaching its breakpoint, or a fixed ratio under strain.
Steep, then flattening to nothingExtinction: the burst, then the decline; the flattening is slower after an intermittent history.

The Schedule Card

One page for the people who run the schedule: the rule in plain words, the parameters, the data to keep, when to step and when to step back, and what never happens. It is built from the Design page; “Print the schedule card” prints it alone.

Reading the Notation

LettersF fixed, V variable, R ratio (responses), I interval (time), T time (no response required). FR 10: the tenth response pays. VI 60 s: the first response after an average of 60 seconds pays. FT 30 s: delivery every 30 seconds whatever happens.
Numbers and unitsRatios count responses; intervals and times are in seconds or minutes, always stated. Ferster and Skinner’s convention is minutes unless noted; the applied literature usually writes the unit.
DR-Differential reinforcement of: O other (absence), L low rates, H high rates, P paced responding, A alternative, I incompatible, D diminishing rates, C communication (FCT), V variability (lag). The letter after DR names what is paid, and what is paid is what grows.
Combined schedulesmult (signaled alternation), mix (unsignaled alternation), chain (sequence with a stimulus change), tand (sequence without one), conc (two responses at once), conjt (two schedules on one response), alt (either requirement), conj (both requirements), interlock (a requirement that changes with time), second order written as outer(inner), e.g., FI 5 (FR 10).
ModifiersLH limited hold (a window after set-up); resetting or non-resetting (DRO); whole-interval or momentary (DRO); signaled or unsignaled (delays); progressive (a parameter that changes across reinforcers).

Where the Catalogue Comes From

The definitions of the basic and combined schedules are Ferster and Skinner’s (1957), quoted from the B. F. Skinner Foundation e-book of the text, and Catania’s (2007) two tables of basic schedules and schedule combinations as reproduced in DeLeon, Bullock and Catania (2013). Zeiler (1977) is the classic analysis of what controls behavior under schedules; Lattal and Neef (1996) survey the schedule research that bears on applied work. The differential-reinforcement entries follow Vollmer and Iwata (1992) for the family and the Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis studies named in each entry for the variants. The applied entries draw on the thinning literature after functional communication training (Hanley et al., 2001; Hagopian et al., 2011; Greer et al., 2016), the token literature (Hackenberg, 2009, 2018; Ivy et al., 2017), and the two chapters on arranging reinforcement by DeLeon and colleagues (2013, 2014). Where an entry cites a study through one of those reviews rather than directly, it says so.

What the Chooser and the Designer Assume

DRO starting intervalThe mean inter-response time from baseline (minutes observed ÷ occurrences), times the share chosen (75% by default): a value the student already meets most of the time (Poling & Ryan, 1982; Vollmer & Iwata, 1992). Steps increase the interval by the chosen percentage until the terminal value; the criterion and the step-back rule are written by the user.
DRL limitsThe first full-session limit is the baseline count scaled to the session length, rounded down to a whole number; the limits then fall in equal steps to the target share of baseline. Spaced-responding criteria start at the baseline mean IRT and lengthen by the same factor each step.
Variable seriesFleshler and Hoffman (1962): the nth of N intervals with mean T is T[1 + ln N + (N − n) ln(N − n) − (N − n + 1) ln(N − n + 1)], which keeps the probability of reinforcement nearly constant through the interval (Catania & Reynolds, 1968). The arithmetic series is 2T/(N + 1), 4T/(N + 1) … 2NT/(N + 1), shuffled. Both average T. Random schedules state the probability per cycle (RI) or per response (RR) instead.
NCRThe first interval is the baseline mean IRT (Vollmer et al., 1993); thinning by fixed increments or by performance (Kahng et al., 2000); an omission rule guards against accidental reinforcement (Vollmer et al., 1997). The step table doubles the interval until the terminal value, which is a working convention.
Multiple-schedule thinningS+ held constant while S− is multiplied by the factor until the terminal value, after Hanley et al. (2001) and Greer et al. (2016); the criteria are the user’s, with the chooser’s defaults as working conventions.
Data sheetsThe card lists the data to keep; the sheets are the workstation’s: Form DD-1 for counts, intervals and sessions (DRO intervals run and earned, DRL counts against the limit, NCR deliveries, reinforcers per session, PR breakpoints, tokens and exchanges), Form SM-1 for the interlocking session sheet, and Form MT-1 for interval samples (momentary and variable-momentary DRO checks, the S+ / S− periods of a multiple schedule). The card’s Data sheet used line names them when it is left blank.
Token pricesResponses per back-up = tokens per back-up × responses per token. The designer warns when an exchange costs more than 50 responses (tokens per exchange × responses per token), a working convention after Bullock and Hackenberg (2006) as reported in DeLeon et al. (2013).
