How Assent Is Sought

Consent comes from the parent or guardian (Form IC-1). Assent is the student’s own agreement to take part, asked for in a way the student understands and checked throughout, not once (BACB, 2020, Code 2.11 and the glossary; Morris, Detrick & Peterson, 2021). This page writes down how assent is asked for, what this student’s “yes” and “no” look like in observable terms, what staff do when the student withdraws, and a log of every session.

What This Form Builds

A functional behavior assessment collects interviews from the adults, records and direct observation. This form adds the person the assessment is about. The student-assisted and student-directed interviews (Kern, Dunlap, Clarke & Childs, 1994; Reed, Thomas, Sprague & Horner, 1997; O’Neill, Albin, Storey, Horner & Sprague, 2015) ask the student the same questions the adult interview asks the teacher, in plain words: what is liked, what is hard and why, what comes before and after the behavior, and what the student would rather have. The answers are a hypothesis in the student’s own words. They are carried into Form FS-1 as the student’s report, beside the adult interviews and the observation data, and they often name an antecedent the adults did not (a task that is too long rather than too hard; a peer; the noise).

The assent page follows the two newer lines of work. Morris, Detrick and Peterson (2021) asked behavior analysts to define assent and withdrawal of assent in observable terms for each person, to seek assent throughout rather than once, and to say what happens when it is withdrawn. Rajaraman and colleagues (2022) set out a trauma-informed practice of behavior analysis with choice and shared governance at its center, and the enhanced choice model (Rajaraman, Hanley, Gover, Staubitz, Staubitz, Simcoe & Metras, 2022) treated dangerous problem behavior while the student could leave the treatment context at any time. The Ethics Code for Behavior Analysts (BACB, 2020) names assent in its consent standard and defines it in its glossary. The log on this form gives the practice something to count: the share of sessions in which the student withdrew, and what was changed in response.

The preference page is a treatment preference assessment. Hanley, Piazza, Fisher, Contrucci and Maglieri (1997) showed that people with developmental disabilities, given a concurrent-chains choice between function-based packages that worked about equally well, chose one of them consistently; Hanley, Piazza, Fisher and Maglieri (2005) found the same with packages that included and did not include a punishment component, and the preferred one was not always the least restrictive. The record here is a simplified version of that arrangement for a school: a sample of each component, a choice, and a count. Wolf (1978) made the case that the people a program serves should judge its goals, procedures and effects; Schwartz and Baer (1991) asked that the judgement be collected from the people who actually live with the program; Hanley (2010) asked that it be measured by what they choose, not only by what they say. The summary page puts the student’s words, the assent plan and the preference result on one sheet for the file.

What the Research Supports

The student can be interviewed about functionKern, Dunlap, Clarke and Childs (1994) published a student-assisted functional assessment interview for students with emotional and behavioral challenges: what the student likes about school, which classes are hard and why, what happens before and after the problem behavior, and what would help. Kern, Childs, Dunlap, Clarke and Falk (1994) used it with one student and built curricular changes from the answers; his behavior improved in the classes where the changes were made. The questions on the Interview page are of that kind.
The student’s answers add information, and disagree sometimesReed, Thomas, Sprague and Horner (1997) wrote a student-guided version of the functional assessment interview and compared students’ answers with their teachers’. The two sources agreed on some elements and not on others. That is the reason to collect both, and the reason this form labels the line it computes as the student’s report rather than the finding.
A standard handbook carries a student-directed interviewO’Neill, Albin, Storey, Horner and Sprague (2015) include a Student-Directed Functional Assessment Interview beside the adult interview and the observation form, with the same structure of routines, antecedents, consequences and preferred reinforcers. The pages here keep that structure so the answers line up with the adult interview (Form IN-1).
Choice itself reduces problem behaviorDunlap et al. (1994) gave students with emotional and behavioral challenges a choice among tasks and saw less disruption and more engagement than when the same tasks were assigned; Shogren, Faggella-Luby, Bae and Wehmeyer (2004) reviewed choice-making interventions and found a consistent effect on problem behavior. What the student would rather do (Interview page) feeds the choices the plan builds in (TD-1).
People choose between treatments, and the choice is measurableHanley et al. (1997) placed two function-based packages (functional communication training and noncontingent reinforcement, with extinction) in the terminal links of a concurrent-chains arrangement; both participants chose one package reliably once each had been sampled. Hanley et al. (2005) repeated the method with packages that did and did not include punishment. The Preference page records the same thing with a pair of samples and a choice, repeated.
Assent needs an observable definition and a response planMorris, Detrick and Peterson (2021) reviewed assent in behavior-analytic research with participants with autism and developmental disabilities and found it rarely described. They recommend individualized definitions of assent and of withdrawal of assent, repeated assent checks, and a stated procedure for what is done when assent is withdrawn. The Assent page is those three things.
Choice and shared governance in treatmentRajaraman, Austin, Gover, Cammilleri, Donnelly and Hanley (2022) describe trauma-informed behavior analysis with four commitments: acknowledge trauma and its effects, ensure safety and trust, promote choice and shared governance, and emphasize skill building. In the enhanced choice model (Rajaraman, Hanley, et al., 2022), students with dangerous problem behavior could at any moment choose the treatment context, a hangout area or leaving; problem behavior fell without the escalation that comes with blocked escape. The withdrawal procedure on this form (pause, offer a choice, end if needed) is the school-sized version.
Social validity from the person servedWolf (1978) proposed that the goals, procedures and effects of a program be judged by its consumers; Schwartz and Baer (1991) reviewed the practice and asked for the judgements to come from the people who live with the program; Hanley (2010) argued for measuring preference objectively, by choice, with the recipients of behavior change programs. The preference count and the student’s own words on the Summary page are the form’s social validity record; Form SV-1 carries the adult ratings.
The hypothesis belongs to the whole assessmentCooper, Heron and Heward (2020) place indirect assessment (interviews and rating scales) before descriptive and experimental analysis and warn that an interview alone does not establish function. The student’s hypothesis on this form is used the same way: to focus the observation (OB-1) and, where warranted, the analysis (EA-1), and to be checked against them.

How the Figures Are Calculated

The student’s hypothesis lineEach “right before” and “usually after” line the student endorses carries a tag (attention, escape, tangible, automatic). The counts per function are summed across both lists; the function or functions with the highest non-zero count are named first, the rest follow with their counts. Lines without a tag (being sent to the office, losing points) are listed but not counted. The line is labelled as the student’s report.
Share of sessions with withdrawalSessions are rows of the assent log with a date or a session name. A withdrawal is a row with a withdrawn-at minute, or a row where assent was not obtained. Share = withdrawals divided by sessions. The last ten sessions are shown beside the total.
Review thresholdWorking convention: a share above 25% (editable), or three withdrawals in a row, calls for a review with the student and the case BCBA and a repeat of the preference page. Any withdrawal is honored on the day; the threshold decides when the procedure itself is questioned.
Preference resultFor each component: times offered (as A or B), times chosen, percent chosen. The ranking by percent chosen is compared with the student’s stated rank; a difference is noted, since choice and report can disagree. A component chosen on two thirds or more of its offers, over at least three offers, is reported as preferred (working convention).

What Keeps the Interview Honest

SettingNot in front of peers; not right after an incident; the student chooses the room where the choice exists. Someone the student trusts asks, or sits in.
WordingThe student’s word for the behavior, not the plan’s. Short questions. Examples offered in threes, never one. The answer written as said, including “I don’t know”.
StoppingThe interview is itself a session: assent is asked for at the start, a break is offered, and the student can end it. A stopped interview is recorded as such and tried again another day, not pressed.
What is done with itThe student is told what will change and what cannot change, and why. A preference the plan cannot honor is written on the Preference page with the reason, so the student’s request is not silently dropped.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated record is a sixth-grader interviewed in the counseling office (reading version, with faces marked for the younger view), an assent plan with twelve logged sessions and three withdrawals (one declined at the start, two during a session), and nine paired choices among four plan components.
