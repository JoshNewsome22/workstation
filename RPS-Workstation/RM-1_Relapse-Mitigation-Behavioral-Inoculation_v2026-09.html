Treatment Challenge Log

One row per challenge. Enter the rates from the last five sessions before the change (condition A) and the first three sessions after it (condition B), comma separated. Relapse is scored when any condition-B session exceeds every condition-A session — the criterion Briggs et al. (2018) adapted from Lerman and Iwata (1995). If more rates are entered, only the last five before and the first three after are scored, and the row says so; when fewer sessions were run, all of them are scored.

Proportion of baseline is the highest rate among the sessions scored in that condition divided by the baseline mean, which is the mean of the last five baseline sessions (the definition in Briggs et al., 2018, and Mitteer et al., 2026). The Four Relapse Types

ResurgenceA target response that was extinguished during treatment returns when reinforcement for the alternative response is discontinued or worsened. In practice this is what schedule thinning does, every time an extinction component is introduced or lengthened.
RenewalThe target response returns when the context changes while the contingencies stay the same — a new aide, a new room, a move from clinic to classroom. ABA renewal is a return to the original context; ABC renewal is a move to a third one; AAB renewal is a move to a new context after treatment in the context where the behavior was reinforced. All three have been shown with free-operant behavior in rats, and ABA renewal was stronger than AAB (Bouton et al., 2011).
ReinstatementThe target response returns when the reinforcer withheld during treatment is delivered again, either independently of behavior or as a commission error following the behavior itself; the second can occur during lapses in treatment integrity, which makes it especially relevant to clinicians (Kimball et al., 2023).
Spontaneous recoveryThe target response returns after time away from the treatment context — a long weekend, a holiday, a school break — when extinction is resumed.

The definitions follow Kimball et al. (2023). These are conceptually distinct but often inseparable in practice. A substitute aide unfamiliar with the plan presents a context change and an alternative-extinction challenge at the same time, and may then commit the commission error that reinstates the behavior. Mitigation aimed at one type need not carry over to the others: multiple-context training addresses renewal and is unlikely to mitigate reinstatement or resurgence (Kimball et al., 2023).

Where the Detection Rule Comes From

The criterion on the challenge log is the one Briggs et al. (2018) used, adapted from the definition of an extinction burst in Lerman and Iwata (1995): relapse is scored when responding during any of the first three sessions after the change exceeds responding during every one of the last five sessions before it. Magnitude is expressed as a proportion of the mean baseline rate so that challenges and cases can be compared.

Note what this criterion is relative to. It compares against the previous treatment step, not against baseline, which Briggs et al. (2018) give as one possible reason why relapse detected this way is far more common than an extinction burst as Lerman and Iwata defined it. A low proportion of baseline can still meet the criterion. Read the proportion columns alongside the relapse column, not instead of it.

What the Theories Say, and Where They Fail

Behavioral momentum theory. Shahan and Sweeney's (2011) model treats alternative reinforcement as both a disruptor of target behavior and a contributor to behavioral mass, and predicts that resurgence rises with both baseline and alternative reinforcement rates. Craig and Shahan (2016) tested its reinforcement-rate predictions directly and concluded that the model mischaracterizes the effects of reinforcer rates on persistence and resurgence. With no alternative reinforcement, persistence was negatively related to baseline reinforcement rate — the opposite of the momentum prediction, and a well-established finding under single schedules. Low-rate alternative reinforcement made target responding more persistent than extinction alone, which the model cannot produce. Resurgence occurred only after high-rate alternative reinforcement, while the fitted model predicted it for every group that received alternative reinforcement. Fitting the model required free parameters differing from the prescribed values by orders of magnitude (k = 0.0061 against 0.05; c = 0.00064 against 1; d = 0.012 against 0.001), with R² = 0.41.

Mitteer, Fisher, Greer, and Helvey (2026) pooled the four clinical tests of the momentum predictions from their own group (21 analyzed cases). Leaner reinforcement for the target behavior in baseline reduced resurgence in all six cases on proportion of baseline but in only three on raw rates; leaner reinforcement for the communication response reduced it in one of three; three times the treatment sessions reduced it in four of six and increased it in two. The authors conclude that the alternative-reinforcement result is inconsistent with momentum theory, that treatment-duration predictions “do not appear overly helpful,” that the theory is “an imperfect framework for understanding and mitigating resurgence,” and they point to the temporally weighted matching law (resurgence as choice) instead. The strategy table on the inoculation plan carries the figures.

Context theory. Bouton's account holds that extinction learning is relatively specific to the context in which it occurs (Bouton et al., 2011), which is why renewal happens and why increasing similarity between treatment and non-treatment settings helps. It is not quantitatively formalized, contextual change tends to be inferred from behavior change, and Craig and Shahan (2016) found patterns it also does not predict.

Resurgence as choice. Shahan and Craig (2017) recast resurgence as a choice phenomenon: it occurs when the relative value of a historically effective target option rises because a more recently effective alternative has been devalued, with recent reinforcement weighted more heavily than remote reinforcement. The model accounts for the reinforcement-rate effects that defeated momentum theory, and extends in principle to magnitude, quality, punishment, satiation, and DRO. Shahan, Browning, and Nall (2020) added a contextual layer to explain why cycling alternative reinforcement on and off during treatment reduces later resurgence.

Resurgence as an instance of behavioral contrast. Lattal and Miles (2024) conclude that, with certain caveats, resurgence appears to be an instance of behavioral contrast measured against an extinction baseline, and they report considerable overlap in the controlling variables of the two. One practical consequence is worth carrying into a plan: the contrast literature shows that target responding need not be near zero to change when conditions elsewhere worsen. Increases during thinning are therefore not confined to behavior that has been fully eliminated.

These are the empirical and theoretical sources. The exposure indicators, risk bands, and evidence tags on this form are a working convention for organizing that literature into a plan; they are not themselves findings, and no model listed above currently accounts for the full pattern of results.
