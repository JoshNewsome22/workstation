Delay Discounting Probe

How the titration is run at the table → Walkthrough

Optional. It converts "he can't wait" into a quantity you can re-measure after treatment. Enter the indifference point at each delay (the immediate amount the student treats as equal to the delayed amount) and the sheet computes area under the curve and a hyperbolic k.

Getting indifference points from school-age students. (a) Adjusting amount: hold delay fixed and raise or lower the immediate amount across trials until preference switches; the switch value is the indifference point. (b) Adjusting delay (Mazur, 1987): hold amounts fixed and adjust the delay until choice is roughly 50/50. Both produce similar estimates. (c) Fixed-block format (Evenden & Ryan): a set list of choices with the delay increasing across blocks; this yields percent larger-later choice, not indifference points, and belongs in Tab 2B. Use experienced outcomes. Real and hypothetical money rewards produced similar discounting in adults (no systematic difference for 5 of 6 participants in Johnson & Bickel, 2002; no detectable difference among 20 college students in Madden et al., 2003, who caution that this rests on failing to detect an effect), but that has not been shown for students with intellectual disability, and Finch et al. excluded hypothetical-choice studies from the SCT literature.

Points are entered indifference points; the curve is the best-fit hyperbola V = A/(1 + kD) found by least squares on a log-spaced grid of k. Half-value delay = 1/k, the delay at which value has fallen to half of A.

Reading the output

  • AUC (Myerson, Green, & Warusawitharana, 2001) normalizes delays and values to proportions and sums the trapezoids under the points. It ranges from 1 (no discounting) to 0 (maximum discounting), assumes no model, and is less sensitive to non-systematic data than a curve fit.
  • k scales how much delay degrades value. A larger k means steeper discounting. Because k is heavily skewed, compare phases or students on log k.
  • Mazur's hyperbola out-performs the exponential model across species and reward types, and it predicts preference reversal directly. Expect it to over-predict value at short delays and under-predict it at long ones, a systematic deviation that Odum (2011) describes. Applied implication: that pattern alone is not grounds to discard the fit.
  • Magnitude effect: larger delayed amounts are discounted less steeply. Re-probe with the same amount or the comparison is not interpretable.
  • Money is discounted less steeply than consumable rewards (food, cigarettes, alcohol). If you probe with an edible and treat with tokens, do not expect the two curves to line up.

What a steep function does not tell you. Yeh, Myerson, and Green (2021) examined 1,206 Human Connectome Project adults. With income and education controlled, discounting correlated with only four of eleven cognitive measures, all with r < .20, loaded on its own factor apart from every cognitive factor, and was uncorrelated with Neuroticism and Conscientiousness, the Big Five traits tied to psychometric impulsiveness. Delay discounting is an individual-difference characteristic in its own right, not a proxy for intelligence or a personality label. Report what the choice data show; do not write "impulsive" into an IEP on the strength of this probe.

Probability Discounting Probe

Optional, and a separate question from the one above. Here the larger outcome is uncertain rather than delayed: the student chooses between a smaller certain amount and a larger amount delivered with probability p. Enter the indifference point at each probability and the sheet computes area under the curve and a hyperbolic h against odds against.

These are not the same process. Green and Myerson (2004) reviewed the evidence that delay and probability discounting are distinct: the magnitude effect runs in opposite directions — larger delayed rewards are discounted less steeply, while larger probabilistic rewards are discounted more steeply — and the two dissociate across groups and manipulations. Do not read a steep delay curve as predicting a steep probability curve, or treat one as a substitute for the other. Both are well described by a hyperboloid with an exponent, and the exponent differs between them.

Odds against. Probability is converted to odds against, Θ = (1 − p) / p, which plays the role delay plays in the delay equation. A probability of .5 is odds against of 1; .25 is 3; .1 is 9. Rachlin, Raineri, and Cross (1991) showed the hyperbolic form fits value as a function of odds against.

For waiting after a request: contingency-based, not time-based. Ghaemmaghami, Hanley, and Jessel (2016) compared time-based progressive delay (TBPD) with contingency-based progressive delay (CBPD) in four individuals aged 21 months to 30 years. TBPD failed for all four; problem behavior resurged within the first 16 s of delay for three of them, even though immediate reinforcement was programmed on two of five trials and an alternative activity was freely available, with prompts to engage in it for three of the four. The presence of an activity was not enough; the response contingency during the delay was the active component. CBPD (delay cue → tolerance response "okay" → DRA or DRO requirement → reinforcer) produced near-zero problem and collateral behavior, optimal communication rates, and delays of several minutes (about 10 to 15 min, with roughly 50 demands completed, for one participant), within 2 to 8 hr of treatment over 4 to 24 weeks. A 2022 multilevel meta-analysis (Muharib et al.) found contingency-based delays effective for autistic individuals and most effective when the contingency included positive reinforcement; DRA-based thinning also cut the prevalence of resurgence roughly in half relative to time-based thinning (Drifke et al., 2020).

Delay arrangement

Iannaccone and Jessel (2021) found tolerance under both DRA- and DRO-based CBPD, with more contextually appropriate behavior under DRA. Prefer DRA when a specific skill is expected during the wait; use DRO when the natural expectation is simply "do something else" with little supervision. (In Ghaemmaghami et al., Jack's requirement combined the two: engagement in any activity, without problem behavior or collateral responses.)

Generalization, Maintenance, and Social Validity

This is the weakest part of the published literature, so it has to be the strongest part of your file. In the 25 experiments reviewed by Finch et al. (2024), generalization was assessed in 3 and maintenance in 2; neither was assessed in any negative-reinforcement study, and the longest follow-up was 6 months (Benedick & Dixon, 2009). No study measured social validity. The review's authors were direct about it: there is little evidence that tolerating a 400-s delay with a programmed activity and a clinician nearby transfers to the delays of days or weeks a student meets outside the session. Plan the probes before training starts and put them on the calendar.

Evidence Guide

What the source literature does and does not support, in the order you will need it. Findings are stated as reported; applied implications are marked as such.

Decision Path

1. Is there a functional communication response already, and is the problem waiting after a request?
Yes: delay tolerance after FCT. Use contingency-based progressive delay with a tolerance response and DRA or DRO requirement. Skip the choice arrangement. Evidence: Ghaemmaghami et al. (2016); Hanley et al. (2014); Drifke et al. (2020); Iannaccone & Jessel (2021); Muharib et al. (2022 meta-analysis).
No: continue.
2. Is the referral behavior escape-maintained?
Yes: negative-reinforcement task-choice arrangement. Verify aversiveness or difficulty per student. Altered delay plus task magnitude or aversiveness worked in every application; add a commitment response if allocation does not shift. Evidence: Perrin & Neef (2012); Porter & Sy (2020); Lerman et al. (2006).
No: positive-reinforcement concurrent choice.
3. Is the goal only to shift allocation, or also to lengthen the tolerated delay?
Allocation only: intervening activity alone (11/11). Allocation plus delay: progressive delay plus intervening activity (35/37 allocation; 28/31 terminal delay).
4. Does the student engage in challenging behavior during the delay?
Yes: make the intervening activity incompatible with it, deliver the reinforcer contingent on the delay requirement rather than independent of behavior, and score challenging behavior as a dependent variable. Dixon & Cummings (2001) saw reductions in self-injury and aggression with an intervening activity; Gokey et al. (2013) saw no increase from low baseline levels.
5. Is a token economy in place?
Yes: deliver tokens immediately and delay the exchange, not the token. Leon et al. (2016) found that delayed tokens reduced responding fastest for all three participants (by 43% and 66% at 3 s for two of them); a delayed exchange was tolerated to 20 s and 6 s, and delayed food to 20 s and 60 s.

What the Discounting Literature Contributes

  • Value declines hyperbolically with delay across pigeons, rats, and humans and across reward types; the hyperbola predicts preference reversal and fits better than the exponential model (Odum, 2011; Mazur, 1987).
  • The degree of discounting is stable: people retested up to a year later with the same assessment discount to a similar degree (as reviewed by Odum, 2011), and the fixed-sequence and titrating formats correlated at r = .81 (Rodzon, Berry, & Odum, 2011), so a re-probe after treatment is a defensible outcome measure.
  • Discounting is heritable (twin estimates rise from roughly 25% in mid-childhood to 30–55% by late adolescence; chip heritability 12–22% in adults) and is nonetheless modifiable; heritability is a probabilistic liability, not a fixed trait (Deng et al., 2026). The genetics review explicitly names delay fading and delay exposure among the behavioral approaches and notes that long-term maintenance and generalization of such training are inconsistently reported (Rung & Madden, 2018), which matches the applied review's conclusion.
  • Steep discounting is not reducible to low cognitive ability or to psychometric impulsiveness (Yeh, Myerson, & Green, 2021). Socioeconomic variables account for a share of its correlations with other measures, so interpret a student's discount rate in context.
  • Domain generality is real but has limits: discounting for one reward predicts discounting for another, except that small-reward and consumable-versus-money comparisons can dissociate (Odum, 2011). A probe with an edible tells you about edibles first.

What the Applied Review Contributes

  • Twenty-five experiments, 79 participants, 1988 through mid-2021; 20 of 24 articles in JABA. Most participants were children (70%), most common diagnoses were autism or developmental disability, intellectual disability, ADHD, and TBI, and school was the most common setting (49%). Race and ethnicity were unreported for 92% of participants and socioeconomic status for all.
  • All studies measured response allocation; 12 also measured terminal delay, 1 task engagement, 2 challenging behavior.
  • The review did not evaluate experimental rigor, did not include grey literature, and was not preregistered; its efficacy patterns are tentative and explicitly call for component analyses and quantitative synthesis.

What the Delay-Tolerance Literature Contributes

  • Time-based delay after FCT often loses the communication response and brings problem behavior back: in Ghaemmaghami et al. (2016) it failed for all four participants, with problem behavior back within the first 16 s of delay for three, even with an alternative activity available (and prompts to it for three of the four); in Hanley et al. (2001), increasing delays extinguished the communication response of the one participant exposed to them. It is not universal: in Fisher et al. (2000), delay fading alone kept destructive behavior low for one of three participants, and punishment, or prompting and praise for work during the delay, was added for the other two.
  • Contingency-based delay with a tolerance response produces near-zero problem behavior, near-optimal communication, and practical delays (about 10 to 15 min, and roughly 50 demands, for one participant) in a few hours of treatment, with generality shown across a small but heterogeneous group of participants, reinforcement contingencies, and contexts, including a context in which problem behavior still produced the reinforcers during the delay (Ghaemmaghami et al., 2016).
  • DRA-based requirements produce more contextually appropriate behavior than DRO-based ones, though both yield tolerance (Iannaccone & Jessel, 2021); DRA-based thinning reduces the prevalence and magnitude of resurgence relative to time-based thinning (Drifke et al., 2020).
  • Signals can help a student wait: both participants in Vollmer et al. (1999) chose the larger, delayed reinforcer when the delay was signaled (with a hand gesture or a timer), and signals helped maintain the communication response during delay fading for 2 of 3 participants in Kelley et al. (2011). A student who counts aloud has turned an unsignaled delay into a signaled one (Leon et al., 2016, Alex).

What every trial has in common

Read once; the sections below only change what is on the cards and what is written down.

A choice trial is short and always the same shape. Two options sit on the table, side by side, in the same positions for a whole session (swap sides between sessions, not within one). The adult names both options in a fixed sentence, then waits. The student chooses by pointing, touching, or handing over a card; the adult delivers exactly what was chosen, with any delay run on a visible timer; then the row is written and the next trial starts. Nothing is negotiated after the choice, and neither option is ever praised or discouraged.

Two adults are best: one runs the trial and the other scores. On the sessions sampled for the interobserver agreement the toolkit's Tab 7 asks for, both adults keep their own record, independently. One adult can do both if the sheet is on a clipboard beside the timer.

Materials: the two outcomes, ready to deliver at once (the tablet already unlocked to the video; the break area set up); a timer the student can see, a visual timer where possible; a delay signal if the plan uses one (the toolkit's example is a green card); the intervening activity for training trials; the printed sheet.

    A. Dimension sensitivity check

    Both options immediate · Tab 2, table A · before anything else

    The question is whether the student wants the larger option at all when no waiting is involved. If they do not choose 2 minutes over 30 seconds when both are handed over at once, delay is not what you are measuring and the reinforcer or the dimension needs changing before you go on.

    A. At the table, step by step

      What you say, every trial

      • Two choices. This one: thirty seconds of tablet, right now. This one: two minutes of tablet, right now.
      • Point to the one you want. Then silence. No prompts, no are you sure?
      • Deliver the chosen amount at once, in the same way each time. Start the tablet timer where the student can see it.

      What you watch for

      • A side bias: nine choices of the left card whichever option is on it means the student is choosing a side, not an amount. Swap sides next session and compare.
      • Hesitation is not data here; only the choice is.
      • No delay is programmed anywhere in this check. If a delay creeps in (the tablet takes 20 s to load), fix the materials.
      Decision. ≥ 80% for the larger option across two consecutive sessions: the dimension works, go to B. Below that: change the reinforcer or the dimension (magnitude, quality, or duration) and run this again. The toolkit's Tab 2 conclusion field records which.

      B. Choice baseline

      Delay on the larger option only · Tab 2, table B · required in every study the toolkit reviews

      Now the larger option costs a wait. Same two cards, same sentence, but the 2-minute break comes after a timer runs. Run a session at 0 s first to anchor, then raise the delay across sessions. The delay at which the student's choices switch from the larger option to the smaller-now option is the switch point; training starts below it. At least one delayed session should also score challenging behavior, because that is often what the delay produces.

      B. At the table, step by step

        What you say, every trial

        • Two choices. This one: thirty seconds of tablet, right now. This one: two minutes of tablet after the timer. Point to the timer as you say it.
        • If the student chooses the delayed option, start the timer in view and say nothing else. When it sounds: Timer's done. Two minutes of tablet.
        • If the student chooses the immediate option, hand it over at once. Thirty seconds later the trial is over and the next one starts.

        What you score during the wait

        • Any challenging behavior during the delay, counted, with a note of what it was. In the example, aggression appears the first time a 30 s delay is introduced.
        • If the plan's stop rule is met (the example: second instance of aggression), end the session, deliver nothing extra, and write the row as it stands.
        • Do not shorten a delay because the wait is going badly; that teaches that behavior ends waits.
        Decision. Raise the delay session by session until allocation to the larger option falls; the delay where it fell is the switch point (the example: 30 s) and it goes into Baseline delay at which allocation switched. Then return to 0 s once: if allocation and behavior recover, delay is the controlling variable and the conclusion is Yes, proceed to training.

        C. Waiting assessment

        Optional · Tab 2, table C · a duration, not a choice

        No choice here. The reinforcer is on the table within reach, the adult gives one instruction, and the stopwatch runs until the student takes it or until something ends the wait. Three or four of these give you an unassisted waiting tolerance in seconds, which the toolkit uses to sanity-check the starting delay.

        C. Step by step

          How it runs

          • Put the tablet on the table where the student can reach it. Say the instruction once, exactly as written on the sheet: Wait, please. Start the stopwatch on the last word.
          • Say nothing more. No countdown, no almost, no blocking.
          • Stop the clock when the student takes the item or when a behavior ends the wait (aggression, leaving the seat, sweeping the table). Deliver the item either way; the assessment is over.

          Why it is optional

          • It answers how long can he wait with nothing to do, which matters if the routine really does require clock-waiting.
          • If the plan will teach waiting with an intervening activity, D is the better baseline.
          • Used in 6 of the 21 experiments the toolkit reviews.

          D. Naturalistic baseline

          Optional · Tab 2, table D · engagement duration with no programmed reinforcement

          How long does the student stay with a task when nothing is arranged for staying? Set the task out, give the instruction, and time engagement with the 3-second onset and offset rule the toolkit uses in training (engaged means manipulating, responding to, or orienting toward the materials). This is the baseline the later generalization probes are compared to, so the procedure must be identical then.

          D. Step by step

            How it runs

            • Set out the task. Stay with this as long as you can. Start timing on the first touch.
            • Pause the clock at 3 s of looking away or hands off; resume on re-engagement. Stop when disengagement lasts, or at your session limit.
            • No praise, no prompts unless you are willing to write the prompt in the notes and count it against the score.

            What the numbers are for

            • An activity the student stays with for minutes (the tiles, 240 s) can carry a training delay; one that lasts 40 s cannot.
            • Run the same task, the same way, as the generalization probe later; the comparison is only fair if nothing but the training changed.

            E. Aversiveness verification

            Escape arrangements only · Tab 2, table E · which tasks the student avoids

            When the reinforcer is escape from work, the “smaller” and “larger” options are tasks, so you need to know which tasks the student finds aversive before you can build a choice out of them. Lay the tasks out as an array, ask the student to pick the one to do first, remove it, and repeat until the array is empty. The order is the data.

            E. Step by step

              How it runs

              • Four task cards, or the real materials, in a row. Pick the one you want to do first. Give a minute of that task, then remove it and ask again with what is left.
              • Rotate the positions every round. Five rounds is enough to see a stable order.
              • Porter and Sy (2020) did this for each participant before any training, which is where the toolkit takes it from.

              What it feeds

              • High-aversive tasks become the delayed (impulsive) side of an escape choice; low-aversive ones the immediate self-control side, exactly as the toolkit's example assigns them.
              • If the order is flat, no task is aversive enough to build a choice on, and escape is probably not the function to train around.

              Delay discounting probe

              Optional · Tab 3 · turns “he can’t wait” into a curve you can re-measure after training

              At each of several delays you find the indifference point: the amount available now that the student treats as equal to the full amount later. The adjusting-amount method does it in five trials per delay. The immediate amount starts at half the delayed amount; every time the student takes the delayed option the immediate amount goes up, every time they take the immediate one it goes down, and each step is half the previous one. Where it settles is the indifference point. Do that at six delays and the sheet draws the curve and computes AUC and k.

              The titration, step by step

                What you say, every trial

                • Would you rather have sixty seconds of tablet right now, or two minutes of tablet after thirty seconds? Update the first number every trial; the second sentence never changes within a delay.
                • With a verbal student this can be asked as a question and nothing delivered until the end (the example's classroom adaptation). With a student who needs the outcome to be real, deliver the chosen outcome on some trials, drawn at random, so every choice can count.
                • Five trials per delay, then the next delay. Randomize the order of delays across the session if the student can tolerate it; if not, run them ascending and say so in the notes.

                Reading the titration

                • The row of numbers under the cards is the immediate amount trial by trial: 60, then 90 or 30, then a step of 15, then 8, then 4. Where it ends is the indifference point.
                • At 0 s the point should be close to the full amount; a low value there means the student is not choosing by amount and the probe stops.
                • Johnson and Bickel (2008) treat the data as nonsystematic if a point is more than 20% of the delayed amount above the one before it, or if the last point is not at least 10% of the delayed amount below the first. Note it with the probe; the toolkit then leans on AUC rather than k.
                What the numbers mean. AUC runs from 1 (no discounting) to 0. k scales how fast value falls; 1/k is the delay at which value has halved. The example's baseline probe gives AUC 0.29 and a half-value delay near 44 s; after four weeks of training the re-probe gives AUC 0.58. Compare phases on log k, and only with the same reward and amount.

                Probability discounting probe

                Optional and separate · Tab 3 · the larger outcome is uncertain, not delayed

                Same table, same titration, one change: the larger option comes now but only sometimes. The adult shows the odds with something the student can see, a spinner or a bag of chips with the right mix, and the outcome is drawn after the choice. The immediate option is certain. The indifference point at each probability goes on the sheet, which converts probability to odds against and fits its own curve.

                With the spinner, step by step

                  What you say

                  • Sixty seconds of tablet for sure, or two minutes of tablet if the spinner lands on green. Green is half of it. Show the spinner; let the student spin it after a maybe choice.
                  • Keep the delivery immediate on both sides. If the uncertain option also involves waiting, you are measuring two things at once.

                  Why it is a different question

                  • Green and Myerson (2004): delay and probability discounting dissociate, and the magnitude effect runs the opposite way for each. A steep delay curve says nothing about how the student handles maybe.
                  • In the example both are steep, which is why the plan keeps every break-card request honored (probability of delivery stays 1) while the delay is faded.

                  A training trial

                  Tab 5, session data sheet · contingency-based progressive delay with an intervening activity · fading step 20 s

                  Training is not a two-card choice. The student is in the routine that produced the problem (independent math), makes the request the plan taught (hands the break card), and the delay begins. Under contingency-based progressive delay the delay ends when the student meets the response requirement, here two maintenance problems, with the timer as the visible signal; the break then follows at once. Some requests are honored immediately (the plan's example: 2 of 5), which keeps the request worth making.

                  One delay trial, step by step

                    What you say

                    • When the card is handed over: You asked for a break. Finish these two problems and the timer, then break. Put the green card up, start the timer in view, put the two problems in front of the student. Say it the same way every time; it is written verbatim on Tab 4.
                    • When the requirement is met and the timer is done: Timer's done. Break time. Deliver the break at the desk, 2 minutes, with the same phrase.
                    • On an immediate trial: Sure, break now. No activity, no timer.

                    What you score

                    • Latency: seconds from the card being handed over to the student starting the activity.
                    • TR independent: the tolerance response (hands the card, says okay, starts) with no prompt within 10 s.
                    • Engaged: on the activity for the delay, 3-s onset/offset rule. Under the cumulative reset rule the timer pauses on interruption and does not restart, so the obtained delay grows.
                    • PB: problem behavior during the delay, counted. The example's stop rule ends the session at the second instance of aggression and steps back a level.
                    Advancing. The example advances one fading step (3, 6, 10, 20, 45, 90, 180 s) after 80% self-control choices across two consecutive sessions with problem behavior at or below 0.2 per minute, and steps back after two sessions below 50%. Tab 6 graphs % SC and the delay achieved together so both are visible.

                    Terms, as the sheets use them

                    For whoever is holding the clipboard
                    Self-control (SC) option
                    The larger or better outcome that comes later. Choosing it is a self-control choice. On the escape side, it is the less aversive task, immediate.
                    Impulsive (IMP) option
                    The smaller or lower-quality outcome that comes now. Choosing it is an impulsive choice. Neither word is a judgment of the student; they name the options.
                    Dimension
                    What the two options differ in: duration, magnitude, quality, or (for escape) task difficulty or aversiveness. The dimension check confirms the student’s choices track it.
                    Allocation
                    The share of choices that went to one option in a session: 8 of 10 to the SC option is 80% allocation.
                    Switch point
                    The delay at which allocation moved from the larger option to the smaller-now option. Training starts below it and works up past it.
                    Indifference point
                    The amount available now that the student treats as equal to the full amount later (or the uncertain amount). One per delay or probability.
                    AUC
                    Area under the curve of indifference points, 0 to 1. Near 1: delay barely matters. Near 0: almost any delay empties the larger option of value. Needs no model.
                    k and half-value delay
                    How fast value falls with delay in Mazur’s hyperbola; 1/k is the delay at which value has halved. Compare on log k, same reward, same amount.
                    Programmed vs obtained delay
                    What the sheet said the delay would be, and what actually ran. The difference is your integrity measure; a few seconds of drift makes a fading step meaningless.
                    Contingency-based progressive delay (CBPD)
                    The delay ends when the student meets a response requirement (two problems, an activity). Time-based delay ends on the clock alone, and it failed for every participant in the study the toolkit cites.
                    Reset rule
                    Cumulative: the timer pauses on interruption and resumes. Consecutive: the delay restarts. Written on Tab 4; the observer scores whichever is written.
                    Tolerance response (TR)
                    What the student does when the delay is announced: hands the card, says okay, starts the activity. Independent if no part of it was prompted within 10 s.
                    Engaged
                    Manipulating, responding to, or oriented toward the assigned materials, scored with a 3-second onset and offset rule.
                    Immediate-reinforcement (IMM) trial
                    A request honored at once, on purpose, on about two of five trials. It is scored, not skipped, and it is what keeps the request worth making.
                    Interobserver agreement (IOA)
                    Two observers score the same session independently; the toolkit computes block-by-block agreement. Collect it on at least a third of sessions.
