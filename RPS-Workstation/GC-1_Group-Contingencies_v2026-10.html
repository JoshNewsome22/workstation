Design a class-wide behavior system, print the poster the class sees, and keep the daily record beside it. In the original game the class was divided into two teams, a mark went against a team each time one of its members broke a rule, and both teams won if both stayed under the criterion (Barrish et al., 1969). Later studies ran the game with a criterion as low as five marks in a class period, with a winning team announced each day, and found it works across grades from kindergarten to high school (Tingstrom et al., 2006). Donaldson et al. (2011) showed teachers could run it in kindergarten after a brief training. The rule-following version (teams earn a point for following the rules) is the one to offer first: Tanol et al. (2010) found it gave the same or fewer rule violations as marking fouls, and the teachers preferred it; choose it under What the record counts.

CW-FIT teaches the three skills as lessons with examples and practice, then runs the game with a timer: when it sounds, every team whose members are all showing the skills gets a point and a specific praise statement; teams meeting the goal earn the reward. Attention to inappropriate behavior is removed by the ignoring skill. On-task behavior rose and disruptions fell in the urban classrooms of Kamps et al. (2011), and the group-level effects held in a randomized trial (Kamps et al., 2015).

What This Form Builds

A group contingency makes a consequence for the group depend on behavior, and the three kinds differ in whose behavior. Litow and Pumroy (1975) named them: in an independent group-oriented contingency the same rule applies to every student but each earns on their own behavior; in a dependent one the whole group earns on the behavior of one student or a few; in an interdependent one the group earns on the group’s performance, judged by every member meeting the criterion, by the group’s total or mean, or by the score of a member drawn at random after the period (a reward that rests on members picked in advance is a dependent arrangement). Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020) keep the same three terms and the same cautions. The programs with names, the Good Behavior Game, CW-FIT, the mystery motivator, randomized interdependent contingencies and tootling, are interdependent arrangements with particular rules, and the form holds each with its own fields so the rule can be written out, posted, and recorded against.

The Good Behavior Game is the oldest and best studied of them. Barrish, Saunders and Wolf (1969) divided a fourth-grade class into two teams, put a mark on the board against a team whenever a member was out of seat or talking out, and let both teams win privileges when both stayed under the criterion; out-of-seat and talking-out fell from most of the intervals to a few, in math and in reading, in a reversal design. Medland and Stachnik (1972) and Harris and Sherman (1973) replicated the effect and examined which parts of the game carried it. Tingstrom, Sterling-Turner and Wilczynski (2006) reviewed the game’s first 33 years and found it effective across ages, settings and behaviors; Bowman-Perrott et al. (2016) pooled 21 single-case studies and reported a large effect (TauU = .82), largest for students with or at risk for emotional and behavioral disorders; Joslyn, Donaldson, Austin and Vollmer (2019) summarized the behavior-analytic studies and what is known about its components. Kellam and colleagues (1994, 2008) ran the game in first-grade classrooms in a randomized prevention trial and followed the children into middle school and young adulthood, with lower aggression and lower rates of later problems among the boys who had been most aggressive at the start.

CW-FIT (Wills et al., 2010; Kamps et al., 2011, 2015) adds to the game what a behavior analyst would add: the skills are taught first (getting the teacher’s attention appropriately, following directions, ignoring inappropriate behavior), points are given with praise to every team meeting the expectation when a timer sounds, attention to inappropriate behavior is withheld, and students who need more get self-management cards or help cards. Randomizing the criterion, the behavior, the students or the reward (Kelshaw-Levering, Sterling-Turner, Henry & Skinner, 2000; Theodore, Bray, Kehle & Jenson, 2001) and keeping the reward in a sealed envelope (Moore, Waguespack, Wickstrom, Witt & Gaydos, 1994) are ways to keep every expectation in force all period and to keep a reward from losing its pull. Tootling (Skinner, Cashwell & Skinner, 2000) is a peer-reporting arrangement in which the class earns on the count of classmates’ prosocial behaviors written down, and the later studies found the disruptive behavior of the class fell while it ran (Cihak, Kirk & Boon, 2009; Lambert, Tingstrom, Sterling, Dufrene & Lynne, 2015).

What the Research Supports

The three kinds, and which to chooseLitow and Pumroy (1975) reviewed the classroom studies to that date and found support for all three arrangements. Gresham and Gresham (1982) compared them directly in a special class and found the interdependent and dependent arrangements reduced disruption more than the independent one. The reviews since (Maggin, Johnson, Chafouleas, Ruberto & Berggren, 2012; Little, Akin-Little & O’Neill, 2015) find group contingencies effective across grades and behaviors, with interdependent arrangements the most studied. The choice on this form is by risk as much as effect: independent where peer pressure would harm a student, dependent only with the safeguards, interdependent with a saboteur rule.
The Good Behavior GameBarrish et al. (1969): two teams, marks for fouls, both teams win under the criterion, privileges as prizes; disruptive behavior fell in a reversal design in a general-education fourth grade. Tingstrom et al. (2006): effective from preschool through high school, in general and special education, with teachers as the implementers in most studies. Bowman-Perrott et al. (2016): across 21 single-case studies the effect was large (TauU = .82) and was largest for students with or at risk for emotional and behavioral disorders. Lannie and McCurdy (2007) ran it in an urban first grade and measured the teacher’s behavior too: student on-task behavior rose and disruptions fell while teacher praise barely changed, which is why this form pairs the game with praise rather than assuming it. Pennington and McComas (2017) found the reduction in disruptive behavior was specific to the classroom context in which the game was played, which is why the fading table starts the game in one period and adds the others on purpose.
Kindergarten and brief teacher trainingDonaldson, Vollmer, Krous, Downs and Berard (2011) ran the game in five kindergarten classrooms after a brief teacher training; disruptive behavior fell in every class while the game was on. Donaldson, Wiskow and Soto (2015) found that the reduction held while the game was played and did not carry over to the class period after it, which is one more reason the fading table adds periods on purpose.
Rule following or rule violationTanol, Johnson, McComas and Cote (2010) compared a version of the game that marks rule violations with one that gives points for rule following in kindergarten; both reduced rule violations, the reinforcement version gave comparable or lower levels, and the teachers preferred the positive version. Wright and McCurdy (2012) compared the game with the Caught Being Good Game and found both effective. The form lets the team choose: a GBG with fouls (lower is better) or a point version (higher is better); the direction is set on the Design sheet and the record follows it.
CW-FITWills et al. (2010) describe the program and its first results; Kamps et al. (2011) ran it in six urban elementary classrooms with a multiple-baseline design and found on-task behavior rose and disruptions fell for the class and for the students at risk; Kamps et al. (2015) reported a randomized trial across schools with the same direction of effects. The skills, the timer, the praise and the ignoring are the parts; the form holds each.
Randomized componentsKelshaw-Levering et al. (2000) randomized the criterion, the target behaviors and the reward in a second-grade class and found disruption fell under randomized interdependent contingencies, and that randomizing the behavior and criterion kept students from working to a known minimum. Theodore et al. (2001) randomized the contingency and the reward with adolescents in a self-contained class and found disruptive behavior fell. Moore et al. (1994) used the mystery motivator, a reward sealed in an envelope with a chart marking the days it is available, and reduced disruption in a general-education class with little teacher time.
TootlingSkinner, Cashwell and Skinner (2000) taught fourth-graders to record classmates’ prosocial behavior and set a cumulative class goal with a group reward; reports rose when the contingency was in place. Cihak et al. (2009) and Lambert et al. (2015) extended it to classroom behavior and found disruption fell and appropriate behavior rose for elementary students with and without disabilities.
Long-term outcomesKellam et al. (1994) ran the Good Behavior Game in first-grade classrooms in Baltimore with random assignment and found lower aggressive behavior in sixth grade for the boys who had been most aggressive in first grade. Kellam et al. (2008) followed the same cohort to ages 19 to 21 and reported lower rates of drug and alcohol disorders, antisocial behavior and other outcomes among the young men who had been in game classrooms. Embry (2002) summarized the evidence and called the game a universal behavioral vaccine; the form treats it as a class-wide system and does not claim the long-term outcomes for any one classroom.
What goes wrongThe cautions in the literature are the ones on the Design sheet: a student who cannot meet the criterion, or who finds it reinforcing to cost the group, can spoil an interdependent arrangement, and the fix used from the first study on is a team of one (Barrish et al., 1969; Tingstrom et al., 2006); a dependent arrangement can make a student a target rather than a hero unless peers are taught what to do and the criterion is within reach (Litow & Pumroy, 1975; Cooper et al., 2020); a criterion set from a wish rather than a baseline produces a class that never wins; and a game run without praise reduces fouls without teaching anything (Lannie & McCurdy, 2007). Stage and Quiroz (1997) found group contingencies among the most effective interventions for disruptive classroom behavior in their meta-analysis, but the effect depends on the game being run as written, which is what the Fidelity sheet checks.

How the Figures Are Calculated

MetWhat a cell holds, and when it is met, follows the arrangement (Litow & Pumroy, 1975). Most arrangements: the team’s or the class’s count, met at or under the criterion (fouls, disruptions) or at or over it (points, tootles, percent), by the direction set on the Design sheet; a team with its own criterion (a team of one, or a student playing under the criterion in their plan) is scored against that. Interdependent, every student meets the criterion: the number of students over the criterion, met at 0. Independent: the students present and the students who earned, met when the share earning reaches the share set on the Design sheet (80% unless another is written). Dependent: the target student’s or students’ score. A day is met when every column with a value is met. A row with no criterion is baseline and is never met.
Baseline and suggested criterionThe baseline is the first run of phase B days; a later run of B days (a return to baseline) and probe days without the game are not pooled into it. The suggested starting criterion is a level the class already reaches on its better baseline days: the best quarter of the baseline team-days (for a ceiling, the count a quarter of them were at or under; for a floor, at or over), so the class wins often from the start and contacts the reward quickly; beside it, how many baseline team-days would have met it. No suggestion is given from fewer than three baseline days, and a baseline that moves by a quarter of its mean or more across its days is flagged as trending. The Design sheet warns when the starting criterion was met on no baseline team-day, or on every one. The criterion then steps on the decision rules. All of this is a working convention; TD-1’s group-contingency component gives the same rule.
Decision rules (working conventions)Only the game days played at the current criterion and in the current phase count, from the day that criterion began, so a step is held until the class performs steadily at it (Hartmann & Hall, 1976). No step is taken until three of them have been played. Met on three of the last four of them (or on all of the first three), tighten the criterion by the step toward the terminal value; met on fewer than two of the last four, relax it by one step and check fidelity and the reward; otherwise hold. At the terminal value the game moves to the next fading phase when the criterion is met on 5 game days in a row in phase 1, and on 6 in a row in phases 2 and 3, as the fading table says. Any step waits for a fidelity check at 80% or more since the criterion began. The numbers come from the Design sheet: the days before a change, and the “N of the last M” in the tighten and relax rows when they are written that way. Each verdict reports the ones it used.
Winner (fewest fouls wins)With a Good Behavior Game won by the fewest fouls, the team or teams with the lowest score that day win (ties both win); under the option that also pays any team at or under the criterion, those teams win as well. An interdependent arrangement in teams where the best team wins is scored the same way. Met still reads every team against the criterion, which is what the criterion rules use. The scoreboard’s winning line follows the same choice.
Tootling totalThe game days’ counts are added in order; the day the total reaches the cumulative goal is met and the count starts again at zero. The decision reads progress to the goal: the goal reached in the current count, raise it by the step toward the terminal value; the mean tootles per game day and the days still needed at that rate are shown; no goal reached in ten game days, lower it or revisit the reward (working conventions).
The graphOne series per team (or one for the class), with a different color and marker for each; the criterion as a stepped line; phase changes as dashed vertical lines. The y-axis scales to the data and the criterion.
Agreement on the talliesTotal-count agreement for one team (or the class) on one game period: the smaller count over the larger, times 100; both zero is 100%. 80% or more is the working convention; the Record lists the latest check and notes a step taken without a check since the criterion began.
Fidelity percentYes divided by (Yes plus No) over the common steps and the chosen arrangement’s own; N/A is excluded. Praise and a neutral voice for fouls are scored as two steps, because the game does not bring praise on its own (Lannie & McCurdy, 2007). At or over 90 the game ran as written; 80 to 89 is a note to the teacher; under 80 the record from that period is not a test of the system, and the Record takes no step while the latest check since the criterion began is missing or under 80 (working conventions).

Ethical Cautions

Dependent contingenciesOne student carries the class. The arrangement is used only with the student’s agreement, with a criterion the student already meets on a good day, with peers taught to encourage and never to pressure, and with an exit rule the teacher applies the same day if the student is blamed. The poster names the student, or shows the student’s goal, only when the student agreed and the parent agreed in writing (Yes, with its date, on the Design sheet); until then it says “our class goal” and names no one. Where the student’s plan already has its own reinforcement, the two should not conflict: the student is not made to lose twice.
Interdependent contingenciesPeer pressure is the mechanism and the hazard. The teacher keeps the score, peers do not report on each other, and a student who repeatedly costs the group, whether from an inability to meet the criterion or because the group’s reaction is itself reinforcing, is moved to a team of one with the same rule and the same prize rather than left to be the reason the class lost. A student with a BIP in the room competes under the criterion in their plan, not the class criterion, when the two differ.
Fouls and marksMarking fouls is a response-cost arrangement: Tanol et al. (2010) call that version of the game GBG-response cost, and found the rule-following version (points for following the rules) gave the same or fewer rule violations and was the one the teachers preferred, so the points version is offered first and reinforcement comes before response cost. When fouls are used, a mark against a team is recorded in a neutral voice without a lecture, and marks are never removed as a reward or added as a punishment for something outside the rules. The game does not take away anything the class already had. A losing team gets no consequence beyond not earning.
RewardsActivities and privileges the class chose; edibles only with family agreement and as the last choice. Nothing on the menu is something students are entitled to anyway (recess, lunch, a bathroom break).
FadingA game that runs every period forever is a sign that the expectations were never taught; the fading table moves the game to intermittent and the reward to delayed on a rule, with the data saying when.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated class is a second-grade Good Behavior Game with two teams, four baseline days, nineteen game days in phase 1 starting at the level the class reached on its better baseline days (9 fouls per team) and stepped by 2 to 7, 5, 3 and the terminal value of 2, each step taken after the rule was met and a fidelity check, then three game days in phase 2, the poster, six fidelity observations, four agreement checks on the tallies and the decision rules applied.
