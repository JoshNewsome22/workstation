Who This Quick Start Is For

A quick start is four pages the adults who run the plan read before the first day and keep at hand: a teacher, a paraeducator, a transition team, a new classroom. It is not the plan and does not replace it; it is the plan in the words those adults use, laid out so that what matters most is found first. Staff who only see the student briefly get Form EB-1 instead.

What Comes From the Case

Nothing from the case yet. Open this form in the workstation with the case's other forms open (DM-1, TB-1, FS-1, TD-1, CR-1, GB-1, PA-1), or open a case file, and what they hold is placed here as a starting point. You can also write everything yourself.

What the case holds is placed into empty fields only and marked from the plan — rewrite. The mark stays while the field still holds the plan’s words and goes when you have rewritten them. A quick start in the plan’s own words is a plan nobody reads at 7:50 in the morning; the rewrite is the work.

Page 1 · Who the Student Is, and Why the Behavior Happens

The first page is read first and remembered best. It carries who the student is as a person, where things stand right now, the few things every adult must get right, the chain behind the behavior in three boxes, and the health and safety facts that cannot wait for page three.

Page 2 · Every Day: Prevent and Teach

What a good day looks like, step by step: the routines that prevent most hard moments, how to talk to the student (the exact words that work and the ones that do not), and the skills the student already has, so adults build on them instead of starting over.

Page 3 · When It Gets Hard: Respond

Tier by tier: what you see, what you do, what you say. The same from every adult. The crisis procedures themselves stay on Form CR-1; this page says what each adult does up to the point of handing over, and names the plan it hands over to.

Page 4 · One Plan, One Voice, One Record

The non-negotiables, side by side with what they replace; what is recorded every school day and how; how the first weeks advance on the data, not the calendar; and who to call for what.

The Flow Sheet

A fifth page, optional: one glance from what do you see? to what to do. It is drawn from what the other pages already hold — the calm-day routines, the request to honor, and the tiers — so there is nothing to keep in step by hand. Three branches: the student is working or just flickering; the student uses the request; the behavior is happening and climbing. Every branch ends in the same recovery, and the last tier ends in a call and the crisis plan.

The Quick Start, As It Prints

Letter pages, portrait. Every page is rebuilt from the fields as they change and again at the moment of printing, so what prints is always what the fields hold. The letterhead prints once at the top of the first page, as on every form.

Guide

1. What a Quick Start Is, and Is Not

A behavior intervention plan is written to be complete and defensible. The adults who run it need something else on the first morning: what this student is like, the three or four things that matter most, what to say, what to do when it gets hard, and who to call. A quick start is that document. It is a selection and a translation, never the plan itself: where the two differ, the plan governs, and every copy says so, under the title of page one and at the foot of each sheet where the browser prints one.

Form EB-1 is for people who see the student briefly and are not running the plan; it leaves most of the plan out on purpose. This form is for the people who are running it. Both carry the confidentiality line and are kept out of students’ sight.

A quick start clarifies and prompts. If an adult has it, has answered the questions, and still does not do a step, the gap is probably elsewhere: training, materials, time, competing tasks, or no one noticing. Run Form PD-1 for that step instead of reissuing the quick start (This adult is not doing a step, under This issue on Setup). Posted reminders and a written prompt were the interventions the Performance Diagnostic Checklist–Human Services did not point to, and they did not work (Bowe & Sellers, 2018; Ditzian et al., 2015); a memo instructing staff changed nothing (Quilitch, 1975); and written instructions alone gave weak and short-lived changes (Alavosius & Sulzer-Azaroff, 1990).

2. What Comes From the Case

Inside the workstation, with the case’s other forms open, their facts are placed here into empty fields: the person-centred profile and the safety precautions from DM-1; the target behaviors and their definitions from TB-1, which become the record table and the replacement skills; the function from FS-1, which becomes the chain on page one; the plan from TD-1, which seeds the five things, the daily routines and the response rows; the crisis plan’s stages from CR-1, which become the tiers; the record on DD-1, read within its current phase (the level before it in brackets), which becomes the where things stand tiles and the record table in the data sheet’s own words, with GB-1’s levels when there is no record; the health record from DM-1 (the conditions, the allergies, an emergency medicine by name, never a dose) and Who to call (the administrator or front office as the crisis call, the nurse when a condition is listed, the parent, the case BCBA); the assent plan from SI-1, which becomes the line for when the student says stop; the people trained on the plan from ST-1, which start each adult’s part; and the ranked menu from PA-1, which goes to who the student is. The same happens when a case file is opened. From the case on the toolbar lets you pick any of it into a form that already has entries.

Every line placed this way is marked from the plan — rewrite. The form does not translate the plan into plain language; turning “differential reinforcement of alternative behavior” into words a paraeducator can act on is a clinical judgement, and getting it subtly wrong would be worse than leaving it to you. The mark stays until the words change, or until you have read them and press Keep as is; the Setup view counts what is still marked, and the technical terms still on the pages are listed back to you.

While anything is still marked, the quick start is a draft: every page prints Draft across it and Draft: not yet reviewed by the case BCBA under the title, Print asks first, and the rows of rules and tiers that still hold the plan’s words go to Forms TI-1 and TV-1 marked as a draft, so TI-1 does not score them as steps and TV-1 does not script or quiz on them.

3. Writing It

  • One sentence a line. Each line is read once, standing up. If it needs a second reading it is two lines or it is the plan’s business, not this page’s.
  • The five things are the ones that, done wrong, undo everything else. Not the five most interesting things. Three is fine; six is a list nobody keeps. Give each one how often or when: “every time he asks”, “at least 6 praise statements in 15 minutes”, “before each demand”. Goal statements changed teachers’ praise only once they said how often (Cohrs et al., 2016). The My day card (Flow sheet view) lists them with a tick for the morning and the afternoon; self-monitoring checklists raised staff integrity on a classroom token economy (Plavnick et al., 2010), and prompts with self-monitoring, faded to self-monitoring alone, held for classroom assistants (Petscher & Bailey, 2006). Compare the card with an observation now and then: self-checking holds up better when someone checks its accuracy.
  • Pictures and worked examples (up to three pictures with a caption each, and an example for each tier) make the written page work harder: with the written methods alone, teachers ran two preference assessments at a mean of 34% and 46% accuracy, and with steps that had little jargon, pictures and worked examples, five of six met mastery in two sessions (Graff & Karsten, 2012); and were enough for most students and in-home technicians in a replication (Shapiro et al., 2016). Setup gives an estimated reading grade for each page (the Flesch–Kincaid grade, from the words a sentence and the syllables a word; a heading, a list line or a table cell counts as a sentence, and syllables are estimated from the spelling): aim at or below the reader’s level; a low score does not make a page right. Thirty-nine behavior training manuals ran from 7th-grade to college-graduate reading level (Andrasik & Murphy, 1977).
  • Say this, not that. Write the exact words. Staff copy wording far more readily than they apply principles.
  • Tier by tier is a description of what the adult sees, not a diagnosis. The response at each tier is the one every adult can run; anything that needs training belongs on Form ST-1 and anything that needs two adults and a protocol belongs on Form CR-1, which the last tier hands over to.
  • What you record names the measure, what counts, and how, in the words of the data sheet the staff actually hold (Forms DD-1, HD-1, SM-1). The first day under a new team is the next row of the same record, not a new baseline.
  • The first weeks advance on the data. Each step names what has to be true before the next begins.

4. The Flow Sheet

The fifth page draws the same content as a decision: at the top what do you see?, then three branches. The calm branch carries the daily routines and the row marked earliest sign; the request branch carries the request you pick and what honoring it gives (from the plan: how much, and any wait the plan builds in); the behavior branch carries the tiers in order, each with what to do and say, ending in the crisis call and the crisis plan. Only the row marked recovery runs under all three; with none marked, a short general line does. The crisis call is the card you choose under Rules & calls, someone on site who runs the school’s crisis response; with none chosen the sheet prints a line to write a name, never the first card or the consulting BCBA. It is rebuilt from the pages, so it cannot drift from them.

5. Who May Receive It

The quick start comes from the student’s education record, which FERPA keeps confidential (34 CFR §99.3; disclosure needs the parent’s written consent, §99.30, unless an exception applies), and it goes only to school officials who work with this student and have a legitimate educational interest (§99.31(a)(1)(i)(A)). IDEA asks the school to protect the confidentiality of the student’s personally identifiable information wherever it is collected, stored, disclosed or destroyed (34 CFR §300.623). The handling line printed under the title of page one (keep it out of students’ sight; do not copy it or pass it on; return it when you no longer work with the student) is the school’s practice, not FERPA’s wording. Record who holds a copy under This issue on Setup.

5a. Issues, Each Adult’s Part, and the Reading Check

Each issue has a number and a date, printed on every copy. Start a new issue raises the number, dates it today and starts an empty What changed, which prints on page one, so staff read the change instead of rereading four pages; collect the old copies from the people listed under Who holds a copy. Each adult’s part says who does what (IDEA asks that each person who carries out the IEP be told his or her specific responsibilities, 34 CFR §300.323(d)(2)) and whether they are trained on this plan (Form ST-1); tiers and rules marked Trained staff only carry that badge on the pages. The reading check is a last page of up to six questions from these pages, answered in each adult’s own words after reading and before training; the answers are on the Flow sheet view, on screen only. It shows the handout was read and catches a misreading; it does not show the adult can run the plan, which takes training with practice and feedback (Parsons, Rollyson, & Reid, 2012). It is scored as teach-back on the Flow sheet view: for each adult and each question, Right first time, Corrected and re-asked or Not yet; a wrong answer is explained again in other words and the question asked again until it is right. With every question answered and none left at Not yet, the check is complete on the date recorded, and Form ST-1 shows it beside that adult’s training record. Questions with correction and re-asking took the integrity of a preference assessment from 30.5% after reading to 91.1%, and brief feedback then brought every participant to 100% (Sleiman et al., 2023).

The first check. Each adult’s part has a First check, within the first 3 school days the adult runs the plan, and Then, the schedule after it (Form TI-1, or Form RS-1 for an RBT). Both go on the due list. Once a check is booked, page four prints it and the line “In your first week, the BCBA (or the person named) will watch once and talk with you the same day”. Setup names any adult with no check booked. Integrity fell soon after training without feedback (Witt et al., 1997; Noell et al., 2000); in-service training changed recording for only 2 of 8 staff, and all 8 improved once a supervisor was there and gave feedback (Mozingo et al., 2006).
