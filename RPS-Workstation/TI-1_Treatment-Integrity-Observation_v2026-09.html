Plan type and main procedure set how the Summary reads the 80–89% fidelity band: 80% kept DRA with every alternative response reinforced working, but not NCR, DRA on an interval schedule or DRO for some people, and not a schedule being thinned (Jones & St. Peter, 2022; Hronek & Kestner, 2025). Program phase is carried to the retraining list, because consequence errors matter most while a task is first being learned (Burlison et al., 2026). The start date marks a new plan or a changed step: until a check reaches the mastery criterion, errors do more harm than later (St. Peter Pipkin et al., 2010).

How to Score

CorrectThe step was implemented as written.
CommissionError of commission. On a consequence step: a consequence the plan withholds was delivered (the reinforcer after the target behavior, the demand or the delay ended early on protest, the wrong consequence). On any other step: the step was done incorrectly, or something the plan does not call for was added.
OmissionError of omission. On a consequence step: an earned consequence was not delivered. On any other step: the step was not done when it came due.
N/AThe step did not apply during this observation, because the condition that calls for it never arose. N/A is excluded from every integrity denominator; the agreement check (IOA) compares N/A codes like any other code.
ComponentOn the Protocol Steps table, tag each step Antecedent (what is arranged, presented or prompted before the behavior) or Consequence (what follows the target behavior, the alternative behavior or a correct response, including withholding a consequence). Tagging a step Consequence marks it critical by default; untick the mark if the plan says otherwise. Consequence steps get their own figures on the Summary: delivering a reinforcer after the behavior that should be on extinction, or after a wrong response, has been linked to loss of treatment effect in several DRA, DRO and discrimination-training studies, though not in all (St. Peter Pipkin et al., 2010; Hronek & Kestner, 2025, as summarized by O’Neill et al., 2026; Burlison et al., 2026). Antecedent and prompting errors have been tested too: in discrete-trial teaching, repeated instructions, a missing controlling prompt, and missing reinforcement each slowed or prevented learning (Carroll et al., 2013). So the tag says where an error sits, not that it matters less. For a skill-acquisition plan, or one that does both, a step tagged Antecedent whose words name a prompt or an instruction is marked critical as it is tagged; untick the mark if the plan says otherwise.
OpportunitiesA step can come due more than once in an observation. The sheet holds one code per step per observation (Commission if any time was a commission error, otherwise Omission, otherwise Correct). For consequence steps the Scoring sheet also takes the counts — correct, commission, omission — for every time the step came due, so that fidelity can be stated per opportunity and N/A when there was none, as O’Neill et al. (2026) report it. The live runner writes the counts; by hand, count them from the session.

Which code? Did the step come due? No: N/A. Yes: was it done as written? Yes: Correct. No, on a consequence step: was something the plan withholds given (a reinforcer, an end to the demand or the delay)? Commission. Was something earned held back? Omission. No, on any other step: not done, Omission; done wrongly, or something added, Commission.

Run both announced and unannounced observations, mixed across the same weeks and activities. A difference between the two may reflect the observer’s presence, a change over time, or different activities; the summary reads it as reactivity only when an announced check falls after an unannounced one.

Protocol Steps

Mark a step critical when an error on it defeats the purpose of the protocol — typically the reinforcement contingency, an extinction component, or a safety step. Critical steps are scored separately, because an overall figure in the nineties can hide a failure on the one step that carries the contingency. Tag the component of each step; a step tagged Consequence is marked critical as it is tagged.

Opportunities on Consequence Steps

For each consequence step and observation: how many times the step came due and was done correctly (C), done as a commission error (Com — the consequence delivered when the plan withholds it, or the wrong consequence delivered) or omitted (Om — the earned consequence withheld). Opportunity fidelity is correct ÷ (correct + commission + omission), N/A when the step never came due. A step with a code but no counts counts as one opportunity. A figure from fewer than 5 opportunities is flagged: with one or two opportunities a single outcome gives 100% or 0% whatever the interventionist usually does (O’Neill et al., 2026). The number 5 is this form’s convention; the studies set none.

Integrity Summary

Where the Errors Were

Errors by Component and Class

Three figures, never one: the overall percentage of steps correct, the share of consequence-step opportunities with a commission error, and the share with an omission error. Opportunities are the counts entered for consequence steps; a step with a code but no counts is one opportunity. Global percentages can hide the one error that matters (Cook et al., 2015).

Announced Against Unannounced

A large drop from announced to unannounced may mean the score reflects the observer’s presence rather than the interventionist’s usual practice, but only when the two kinds of check were mixed across the same weeks and activities; otherwise it may be a change over time. Treat the unannounced figure as the estimate of what the student actually receives: an observer who arrives unannounced is still in the room, so it reduces reactivity rather than removing it.

With and Without Distractions

Tick the conditions under each observation on the Scoring sheet: other students needing help, whole-group instruction, staff short, interruptions. In a classroom analog, integrity fell for all 5 teachers when students created distractions, most of all for delivering the reinforcer in DNRA, differential negative reinforcement of an alternative behavior (Berdeaux et al., 2022). A lower figure with distractions points to the conditions in the room (Form PD-1), not only to a skill.

Agreement on the Integrity Scoring Itself

Tick IOA on an observation column in the scoring sheet, then enter the second observer's codes below. Integrity data need their own reliability check before they are used to judge a plan.

Retraining List

Generated from the scoring sheet, in priority order: first any consequence step with a commission error, then any critical step below the criterion, then any consequence step with an omission, then every other step below the criterion or with a commission error. The order follows the direction of the evidence (commission on consequence steps was the more damaging class in Burlison et al., 2026, and in the DRO and DRA work they and O’Neill et al., 2026, review); no study tested a retraining order. Carry these step numbers into Form ST-1 as the training targets.

Before retraining, tick what the check found. The four questions come from the Performance Diagnostic Checklist–Human Services (Form PD-1 runs the full checklist). Only the first leads to retraining on Form ST-1; for the others, change the setting or the feedback, not the training.

Feedback Given After Each Check

For each scored observation: when the feedback was given, how, and what it held. Feedback works best given within a school day, on the interventionist’s own integrity graphed across checks, with the missed steps named and rehearsed. After consultation alone, teachers ran a plan on 50% of days; with performance feedback on their own integrity, on 93% of days (Noell et al., 2000). Written feedback on the student’s progress alone did not keep integrity up; daily feedback on the teacher’s own integrity, with directed rehearsal of missed steps, raised it the most (DiGennaro et al., 2007). Feedback by email with a graph worked, and by video call reached mastery faster and held better (Zhu et al., 2021).

The fading rule (a default; the BCBA may set another). Check and give feedback at least weekly until the mastery criterion is met. Then check every 2 weeks. Return to weekly after any check below the criterion, or after any commission error on a critical step. Feedback every other week kept integrity up once it was high (Codding et al., 2005; Codding et al., 2008).

Integrity Beside the Student’s Data

How the Figures Are Calculated

Individual trial integrityWithin one observation: correct steps ÷ scored steps. N/A steps are removed from the denominator.
All-or-nothing by trialThe proportion of observations in which every scored step was correct. A strict measure: it asks how often the protocol ran perfectly start to finish.
Global integrityAll correct steps ÷ all scored steps, pooled across every observation.
All-or-nothing by stepThe proportion of steps that were correct on every observation where they applied. This locates the unreliable steps rather than the unreliable sessions.
Critical-step integrityThe same pooled calculation, restricted to steps marked critical.
Mastery criterionMet when each of the last unannounced observations with scored steps, taken in date order (column order breaks a tie; an undated observation counts as the oldest), is at or above the percentage set in the toolbar; the toolbar also sets how many. When it is met, critical-step integrity on those same observations is checked against the same percentage, and below it the verdict says the plan is not yet running as written. A commission error on a critical step, or on a consequence step, on those observations fails the criterion whatever the percentages; with no critical step come due on them, the verdict stays open.
ReactivityIntegrity pooled across the announced observations (their correct steps ÷ their scored steps) minus the same figure for the unannounced observations, in percentage points. Shown only when at least one announced check falls after an unannounced one; otherwise the difference is confounded with time.
TrendLeast-squares slope of individual trial integrity across the scored observations, taken in date order (an observation with no scored step is skipped), in percentage points per observation.
IOAPoint-by-point agreement on the integrity codes themselves: steps coded identically by both observers ÷ steps coded by both, × 100, with N/A counted as a code. Shown for each IOA observation and pooled across them.
KappaCohen’s kappa on the same pairs of codes: (observed agreement − chance agreement) ÷ (1 − chance agreement). Chance agreement is the sum, over the four codes (Correct, Commission, Omission, N/A), of the share of steps each observer gave that code, multiplied together. Because most steps are coded Correct, two observers agree often by chance alone, so kappa is the fairer figure. Below .60 is flagged; .60 is Hartmann’s (1977) suggestion, not a validated cut-off. Not calculable when both observers gave every step the same one code.
IOA coverage and driftFor each observation type (announced, unannounced): the observations with scored steps that have IOA, out of all of them. A type below 20%, or with none, is flagged. Agreement is shown for each second observer (the observer named on the Summary’s IOA table). Possible drift is flagged when the latest IOA check is 10 or more points below the first, when a later type’s mean IOA is 10 or more points below the first type’s, or when one second observer’s mean is 10 or more points above the others’. The 20% and 10-point marks are this form’s conventions.
With and without distractionsIntegrity pooled across the observations with any distraction ticked (correct steps ÷ scored steps), beside the same figure for the observations with none ticked.
Abrupt dropIndividual trial integrity on consecutive scored observations, in date order: a fall of 20 points or more from one to the next is flagged. The 20 points are this form’s convention.
Feedback delaySchool days (Monday to Friday) from the observation’s date to the date the feedback was given; over 1 school day is flagged. Holidays are not known to the form, so count them out by eye.
Opportunity fidelity (consequence steps)For a step tagged Consequence: correct opportunities ÷ (correct + commission + omission) across the observations, from the counts on the Scoring sheet; a scored code with no counts is one opportunity; N/A when the step never came due. Flagged when fewer than 5 opportunities stand behind it (a form convention).
Commission rate, omission rateOn consequence steps: commission opportunities ÷ all opportunities, and omission opportunities ÷ all opportunities. These are reported beside the overall figure, never in place of it. The case is also told the commission share for each observation (the commission opportunities on its consequence steps ÷ all its consequence-step opportunities), so Forms RM-1 and BC-1 can show it beside the overall percentage (v25.65): commission errors on DRA hurt when integrity fell to 40% or below, and omission errors alone did not (St. Peter Pipkin et al., 2010).
Weighted integrityCorrect ÷ (correct + omission + commission + commission on consequence steps), over every step’s opportunities: a commission error on a consequence step counts twice. This is this form’s convention; the studies tested consequence errors only, and none quantifies the weight.
Lowest step, rangeThe lowest per-step percentage among scored steps, and the lowest and highest individual trial integrity across the scored observations. Fidelity varies between observations and implementers, and performance after a drop in fidelity depended on the preceding level in Burlison et al. (2026), so the spread is recorded, not only the mean.
Fidelity bandPooled integrity on the observations the verdict is judged on (all scored observations when no unannounced one is scored), placed in one of four bands: 90% and above; 80–89%; 60–79%; below 60%. The 80–89% band is read from the main procedure, the schedule-thinning tick and the plan type (see below). The bands are this form’s working convention, informed by the parametric studies but not set by them.

The Live Observation Runner

On the Scoring sheet, Set up an observation offers the next empty column and fills in the date and start time from this device’s calendar and clock; choose announced or unannounced. The form has no time field, so the start time and the minutes observed are written after the activity in the column’s context. Code each step as it comes due with Correct, Commission, Omission or N/A, one step at a time or with all steps listed (keys C, M, O and N; the arrow keys move between steps, Z undoes the last code, and Space or P pauses), with this observation’s integrity and critical-step integrity shown against the toolbar’s percentage.

A step that comes due more than once is coded each time. The sheet holds one code per step per observation, so the runner writes Commission if any of those times was a commission error, otherwise Omission if any was an omission error, otherwise Correct (N/A only for a step that never came due): a step counts as correct only when it was implemented as written every time. Like the other decision rules on this form, this is a working convention, not a finding. End observation lists the steps not coded and the critical steps not scored. Save to observation writes the column through the sheet’s own fields, so every figure is recalculated as for hand coding, and asks before it replaces a column that holds codes; Discard asks first too. The runner never prints.

The Retraining List for Form ST-1

Save retraining list for ST-1, on the Retraining sheet, writes a file for Form ST-1 from the observations the verdict is judged on: every step below the criterion or with an error on them, with its codes, its commission and omission counts and its percentage correct, and each of those observations’ integrity and commission share on consequence steps (v25.65). The Retraining sheet’s own list pools every observation, so the file can list fewer steps, or different ones.

Why did it drop? For an interventionist already signed off as competent on Form ST-1, low integrity is often not a skill problem. Before retraining, the Retraining sheet asks four questions from the Performance Diagnostic Checklist–Human Services: does the person know the steps (rehearsal shows it now); have the materials and the time; is the task set up to be done; does someone notice when it is done. Only the first leads to retraining on Form ST-1; for the others the sheet says to change the setting or the feedback, not the training, with a line for what was changed. The answers go into the retraining file (as why) and to the case. When the checklist pointed to missing consequences, the graphed feedback it indicated raised performance and an intervention it did not indicate did not (Ditzian et al., 2015); performance feedback every other week raised teachers’ integrity on consequence steps for all 5 and on antecedent steps for 4 of 5 (Codding et al., 2005).

Why These Distinctions Matter

Commission and omission errors are not interchangeable. An omitted step usually means the interventionist did not know it was due, or was taken up with something else when it came due (a step whose condition never arose is N/A, not an omission); a commission error means something was done that the plan does not call for. Which remedy fits depends on why it happened (a skill not yet fluent, an unclear cue, or the conditions in the room, such as staffing or competing demands), and Form PD-1 sorts these out before retraining. Recording only a single integrity percentage discards the information that decides which.

A single aggregate figure also hides where the failure sits. Ninety-three percent integrity on a fourteen-step protocol is a different clinical situation depending on whether the missed step was a greeting or the reinforcement contingency, which is why critical steps are scored on their own.

Error Classes, Opportunities and Fidelity Bands

Score every protocol step on each opportunity. Record whether an error was an omission (a prescribed step not done) or a commission (on a consequence step, a consequence the plan withholds delivered, e.g., the reinforcer after the target behavior; on another step, the step done wrongly or something added). Mark consequence-delivery steps as critical by default. Parametric studies of DRO and of discrimination training show that reinforcing the behavior that should be on extinction has been linked to loss of treatment effect, though not in every study, that effects of combined consequence errors were comparable to full fidelity at 80% and for most participants at 60%, were degraded at 40%, and were absent at 20% (O’Neill et al., 2026), and that acquisition of a new discrimination was blocked for 5 of 6 learners when consequences averaged about 67% fidelity (Burlison et al., 2026). These were analog studies with adult volunteers; treat the bands on this form as clinical conventions, not validated cut-offs.

The bands the Summary applies: 90% and above, acceptable, routine monitoring. 80–89% depends on the procedure. With schedule thinning ticked, whatever the procedure: a caution, aim for near-perfect integrity until the new schedule is stable, as Jones and St. Peter (2022) recommend when moving from every-response reinforcement to an intermittent schedule. For NCR, DRA on an intermittent or interval schedule, or DRO: a caution, because 80% integrity did not keep NCR or interval DRA consistently effective in Jones and St. Peter (2022), who conclude that 80% “is not a uniformly acceptable minimal level”, and in DRO commission errors weakened the effect for 4 of 6 people (Hronek & Kestner, 2025). For discrete-trial or mand teaching: a caution, as for any skill-acquisition program (in mand training, 70% integrity slowed acquisition and no one acquired the mand at 40%: Pence & St. Peter, 2015; in discrete-trial training, 50% commission errors harmed learning as much as 100% for 2 of 3 learners: DiGennaro Reed et al., 2011). For DRA with every alternative response reinforced, on a behavior-reduction plan: acceptable, with a re-check scheduled, since it stayed effective at 80% (Jones & St. Peter, 2022; St. Peter Pipkin et al., 2010). Otherwise, as before: acceptable for a behavior-reduction plan in this evidence base, with a re-check scheduled; for a skill-acquisition program, or one that does both, a caution. (Response cost still suppressed responding at 50% integrity but not at 20%: St. Peter et al., 2016; RIRD worked at 50% implementation or higher: Colón & Ahearn, 2019. These are small analog studies, so the form reads their band by the plan type.) 60–79%, caution: effects were degraded for some learners at these levels, so any consequence step in error is retrained. Below 60%, not acceptable: the student’s data are not read as a test of the plan, and retraining comes before the plan continues. No band is an empirically validated safe threshold, and 80% is not a standard the research sets: in earlier DRO work commission errors were harmful for some participants even at 80% fidelity (Hronek & Kestner, 2025, as summarized by O’Neill et al., 2026), and at 60% three of eleven participants in O’Neill et al. (2026) lost the treatment effect, which is why those authors call their findings tentative.

Why the split by error class: in Burlison et al. (2026) the probability of a correct response on the next trial was 0.29 (in 33% blocks) and 0.24 (in 67% blocks) after a wrong response had been reinforced, against 0.46 and 0.63 after earned reinforcement had been withheld, and the authors conclude that error reinforcement was the more detrimental type of error. Omission errors are the less damaging class, not a harmless one: the condition in which every consequence was wrong blocked learning entirely, and omission-only errors raised responding for some participants in the DRO work summarized by O’Neill et al. (2026). The weighted figure counts a commission error twice as a convention of this form; neither study quantifies the weight. Opportunity counts follow O’Neill et al. (2026), who report obtained fidelity separately for each error type as correct opportunities over all opportunities, with N/A when a phase offered none, and who note that with a single opportunity one outcome yields 100% or 0% regardless of the implementer’s typical behavior.

What this form does not claim: that antecedent or prompting errors matter less (they slowed or prevented learning in discrete-trial teaching: Carroll et al., 2013); that variable fidelity is worse than steady fidelity at the same mean (not compared); that these results were obtained with staff or parents implementing plans with clients (both studies used computer-delivered errors with adult volunteers, 11 and 6 of them); or that any of its bands, weights or minimum counts were derived from the studies. Once errors are corrected, learners with a history of them can still master the task (all six did in Burlison et al., 2026), so detected low fidelity is a reason to retrain, not to abandon the plan.
