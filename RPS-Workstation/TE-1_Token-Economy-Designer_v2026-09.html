Thinning Plan

Thin one schedule at a time and record which. Response rates vary inversely with both the token-production and the exchange-production ratio, and the two interact (Bullock & Hackenberg, 2006), so raising both at once makes the effect uninterpretable. Each step shows its responses per exchange, the token-production ratio times the exchange-production ratio, and, where the backups per exchange are entered, its unit price (responses per backup delivery). Write when to move on and when to step back before the step starts, and the result (the date, and the rate or percentage on Form DD-1) once it is known; the form works out from the numbers which schedule each step changed.

Backups, and Whether the Token Is Generalized

A token exchangeable for one thing is a conditioned reinforcer for that thing, and it fails when the student is satiated on it or simply does not want it today. A generalized conditioned reinforcer is exchangeable across several backups tied to different establishing operations. In a pigeon token economy, tokens exchangeable for either food or water were produced more than tokens exchangeable for only one, suggesting enhanced reinforcing efficacy (DeFulio et al., 2014). The form counts what the student has to want for each backup to work (food, a break, adult time, a preferred activity), not the type of item: a tablet and a set of tiles depend on the same want. Left blank, it is read from the class.

A Token System Is a Chain, Not a Schedule

Hackenberg (2009) analyses token systems into three schedules. The token-production schedule specifies what the student does to earn a token. The exchange-production schedule specifies when an exchange opportunity is made available: in Bullock and Hackenberg (2006), and on this form, after a fixed number of tokens has been earned. The token-exchange schedule specifies how tokens are exchanged for backup reinforcers during that opportunity; in that study it was held at FR 1, and each token bought one food delivery (Hackenberg, 2009). In most classroom systems all three are collapsed and only one is ever adjusted, which is why thinning a token board so often produces an effect nobody can explain.

Bullock and Hackenberg (2006) manipulated the first two independently under second-order schedules. Response rates varied inversely with the token-production ratio at every exchange-production ratio, and inversely with the exchange-production ratio at every token-production ratio, most clearly at the higher token-production values. At higher values of both, responding increased in the segments closer to the exchange period — the chained character of the arrangement showing through, with rate rising as the backup came nearer.

Unit Price

Unit price expresses the cost of a reinforcer in responses: total responses divided by the reinforcers obtained. In a token system it is the token-production ratio multiplied by the exchange-production ratio, divided by the number of backup deliveries an exchange yields, each counted in one fixed unit, since unit price is responses per unit of reinforcer (Hackenberg, 2009): five stars exchanged at one minute of tablet per star are five one-minute deliveries. Two systems with very different-looking boards can impose the same unit price, and two that look similar can differ several-fold. This form computes it so the comparison is available before a thinning step rather than after. Bullock and Hackenberg (2006), however, found only some of their results consistent with unit price; most were well accounted for by the number of responses required to produce an exchange period, without regard to the amount of reinforcement available during it. The form shows that figure, responses per exchange, first, beside the unit price and for each step of the thinning record; the unit price of a past step is shown when the record holds the number of backup deliveries its exchange yielded (the Backups per exchange column). Unit price also turns on how the deliveries are counted (five 1-minute deliveries or one 5-minute one), and a menu priced in different numbers of tokens has a price for each backup (its token cost times the responses per token), which the form lists as a range. The form draws no band and gives no advice from either number: the sources give no threshold for a student, so a step is judged by the student’s own rate at it, recorded as the step’s result, against the step-back rule written before it started.

Generalized Conditioned Reinforcement

DeFulio, Yankelevitz, Bullock, and Hackenberg (2014) ran pigeons in a token economy where tokens bought food on one side of the chamber and water on the other, under separate food and water restriction. A third, generalized token type could be exchanged for either. The number of tokens accumulated before exchange rose as the exchange-production schedule was raised, and pigeons produced more generalized than specific tokens, suggesting enhanced reinforcing efficacy of generalized tokens. When the token-production price of each type was changed, production of specific and generalized tokens changed in opposite directions, indicating substitutability. The demonstration matters for practice because it separates a token that works for one backup from a token that holds its value across establishing operations — which is what a classroom system needs if it is to survive a student who is not hungry today.
