Where Baselines and Criteria Come From

Every baseline and criterion on this form can be typed in directly. Where the treatment plan (TD-1) already carries the number, Apply to empty fields copies it into the first reduction objective and the first acquisition objective on the form, so the goal starts from the plan's numbers. A copied value is an ordinary entry: it is not updated if the plan changes later, and editing it is an override — say why in that objective's note.

The Format Used Here

Each reduction objective is built from six components — who, the direction of change, the response measure, the target behavior, the current level and the target level — and rendered into the template paragraph, which adds the context, the success criterion and the target date.

(Consumer name) will (direction of change) their (response measure) of (target behavior) from (current level) to no (more/less) than (target level) in/at (context/environment) over (success criterion) # measurements by (target date).

Source: short-term objective components and template paragraph, Florida Institute of Technology ABA instructional series (2019).

The sentence on this form adds three things to the template. It writes “in” before the context unless the context begins with its own word (at, on, during, across…). It counts the success criterion in consecutive measurements, the rule the treatment plan uses. And it ends with the measurement method, which the IEP must describe (quoted under Acquisition Objectives).

A Check line appears under an objective whose parts contradict each other: a direction the response measure does not take when the behavior is reduced (latency to first occurrence rises; every other measure here falls), “no less than” with a decrease or “no more than” with an increase, or a target level that is not below the current level (not above it, for an increase) when both are a number in the same unit.

Acquisition Objectives

One row per skill being taught, including the replacement behavior the plan depends on. These follow the condition – behavior – criterion format: Florida Department of Education guidance lists behavior, conditions and criterion as the elements of a measurable annual goal, and short-term objectives are generally written the same way (Technical Assistance Paper FY 2005-2, 2004).

The Format Used Here

By (target date), given (condition), (student) will (observable behavior) (criterion) across (number) consecutive (unit), as measured by (measurement method).

The condition states what is present when the behavior should occur: the materials, the prompt level, the setting or the establishing operation. The criterion states how well and how independently. A goal without a stated condition is not measurable, because two people will arrange the opportunity differently.

The IEP must include “A statement of measurable annual goals, including academic and functional goals” and, “For children with disabilities who take alternate assessments aligned to alternate academic achievement standards, a description of benchmarks or short-term objectives” (34 CFR 300.320(a)(2)). Florida also requires benchmarks or short-term objectives for “Any other student with a disability, at the discretion of the IEP Team” (Rule 6A-6.03028(3)(h)3., F.A.C.). The IEP must also contain a description of “How the child's progress toward meeting the annual goals described in paragraph (2) of this section will be measured” and “When periodic reports on the progress the child is making toward meeting the annual goals (such as through the use of quarterly or other periodic reports, concurrent with the issuance of report cards) will be provided” (34 CFR 300.320(a)(3); Rule 6A-6.03028(3)(h)7., F.A.C.), which is why every objective here ends with its measurement method and the goal sheet prints the progress-report schedule entered on Setup. The finished objectives, numbered, ready to transfer.

Progress Report on Annual Goals

The periodic report on progress toward each annual goal that the IEP describes (34 CFR 300.320(a)(3)(ii)). For the period chosen, each objective's current level comes from the case (Form DD-1 for a behavior, Form SA-1 for a skill) and is set against what the objective asks; the progress code is suggested from those figures and can be changed, and each goal has one plain sentence for the family.

Guide: Writing a Goal That Can Be Measured

1. What Makes an Objective Measurable

An objective is measurable when two people, given the same definition and the same data sheet, would score the same episode the same way and would agree on whether the criterion has been met. That requires a response measure rather than an impression, a current level to move from, a target level to move to, the context in which it counts, how many measurements have to meet the criterion before the goal is considered met, and the method that produces the measurements. What the IEP must say about measuring progress and reporting it is quoted under Acquisition Objectives.

A criterion of “80%” with no statement of how many measurements, or “will reduce aggression” with no current level, cannot be scored. Nor can a goal whose measure does not match the data actually being collected: if the data sheet records rate per minute, the goal cannot be written in percentage of intervals.

2. Reduction and Acquisition Are Written Differently

A reduction objective moves a measure of an existing behavior down from where it is now (latency to first occurrence moves up: the behavior starts later). It states both levels, because a reduction from an unstated baseline cannot be evaluated.

An acquisition objective describes a behavior that may not yet occur at all, so a “from” level is often zero and carries little information. What it needs instead is the condition under which the behavior should occur and the level of independence expected, which is why it takes the condition – behavior – criterion form rather than the from/to form.

3. Pair Them

A reduction objective written on its own commits the team to suppressing a behavior without committing it to teaching anything. Each reduction objective on this form carries a field naming the acquisition objective it is paired with, each acquisition objective names the reduction objective it replaces, and the goal sheet prints that pairing under each objective, numbered as on the form (R1, A1).

When several behaviors share one replacement skill, the form writes one acquisition objective for it, naming every behavior it replaces. The replacements named on Form TB-1 are read without their staff notes (“(see target 4)”, “(replacement)”), and a paired replacement that points to a replacement target defined on TB-1, or reads exactly as one that does, takes that target’s name. Wording that only means the same thing is not merged. A behavior added later from From the case that shares a skill already on the form joins that skill’s list of the behaviors it replaces, when the list is the form’s own. In the sentence the skill follows “will”: an action named in the third person takes its plain form (“Hands a break card and waits”: will hand a break card and wait; “Participates in group activities”: will participate in group activities; “Using a quiet voice”: will use a quiet voice), a name followed by what is done with it starts with that action (“Tasks completed independently”: will complete tasks independently), a subject at the start is left out (“The student requests a break”: will request a break), an opening phrase moves after the action (“When frustrated, requests a break”: will request a break when frustrated), a name is introduced by a verb (“Break request”: will make a break request; “On-task behavior”: will demonstrate on-task behavior), and a staff note is left out. A context or a condition starts in lower case when its first word is an ordinary one (“In Room 12”, “Independent work”); a name stays as typed (“Jordan at recess”). The fields keep the words as they were typed; if a sentence still reads awkwardly, retype the skill in plain form (“ask for help”).

4. Where the Numbers Should Come From

The baseline belongs to the data, not to the goal: take it from the measurement that will also be used to judge the goal, over enough sessions to be stable. The reduction criterion and the number of consecutive sessions are already decided in the treatment plan as TD-1's rule for advancing a thinning level; this form reads them from there rather than inviting a second, different number.

Where a goal deliberately differs from the plan — a more conservative IEP criterion than the plan's thinning criterion, for instance — override it here and say so in the note field, so the difference is on the record as a decision rather than as a discrepancy.

Current level from DD-1, at the top of Reduction Objectives, takes the baseline from Form DD-1, the data the goal will be judged by. For each reduction objective it finds DD-1’s behavior of the same name and works out its level in DD-1’s first condition, the baseline, as DD-1’s own Results do: the mean of the days with data, their range and their number. It writes that level only into an empty Current level, for example “10 per school day (DD-1 baseline: mean of 6 days, range 8–12)”, with where it came from shown in full under the field, and only where the objective’s response measure is the one DD-1 records: rate per hour (DD-1’s rate), frequency per school day (a count over the entire school day), total duration per session in minutes (a duration recorded per session or over a single period) or percentage of intervals. An objective with no response measure yet takes DD-1’s. A level in another unit is never converted or written: a rate per minute is not DD-1’s rate per hour, and a latency in seconds is not DD-1’s latency in minutes. Each objective left empty is named with the reason (a different unit, no days in the baseline, or no behavior of that name on DD-1). TD-1’s Apply to empty fields still fills the first reduction objective on the form from the plan, its current level as a rate per minute, when that one was left empty. Inside the workstation the button reads the DD-1 open there; opened on its own, or with DD-1 closed, it reads a file DD-1 saved with Save data, or a case file that holds DD-1.

Writing help

The Progress reports box shows an Improve wording button at its corner when you are in it and whenever it holds text; from a keyboard, Alt+Enter in the box (Option+Return on a Mac or iPad) opens the same panel. Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. Rewrite with Claude works only in the forms opened from the website: a single-use passcode from your BCBA unlocks the tab (in the workstation, every form in that tab) until the session’s time is up or the tab is closed; press Lock when you finish on a shared iPad. Before anything is sent, the student’s name and ID become placeholders such as [Student], other names can be added under Also hide, and the panel shows exactly what will be sent; the names are put back in the answer. Use it for real cases only with your district’s or agency’s approval. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print.
