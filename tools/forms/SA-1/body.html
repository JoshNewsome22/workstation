Maintenance and Generalization Probes

A probe is a session run under baseline conditions: no prompts, no error correction, reinforcement as it will be in the natural setting. A maintenance probe repeats the trained conditions after a delay. A generalization probe changes one dimension: the person, the setting, the materials or the time. Record the dimension changed so that the untested ones stand out. A probe passes at the mastery level set on the Setup page, a working convention.

What This Form Builds

A skill-acquisition program is the teaching side of the plan. Form GB-1 writes the goal, Form TD-1 names the replacement skill the plan depends on, and this form records how the skill is taught and whether it is being learned. Its first page states what every instructor needs in order to run the same trial: the discriminative stimulus, the definition of a correct response, the time allowed, the prompt hierarchy and its codes, the error-correction procedure, the reinforcement schedule (Form SR-1), and the mastery criterion the data will be judged against. The pages after it are the data: a trial-by-trial grid for discrete-trial or natural-environment teaching, a step-by-step grid for a task analysis, a graph of percent independent per session with the mastery line and the phase changes, and the maintenance and generalization probes that decide when the program is finished.

Discrete-trial teaching presents learning opportunities as a series of trials, each with a clear instruction, a response, a consequence and a short pause before the next trial; it is well suited to establishing new responses and discriminations that the student does not pick up from the environment, and it must be joined to teaching in the natural setting if the skill is to be used there (Smith, 2001). Green (2001) sets out the stimulus-control side: a skill is learned when the student’s response comes under the control of the instruction or the natural cue rather than the prompt, which is why every prompt hierarchy on this form ends with a plan to fade. The task-analysis page follows the chaining literature summarized in Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020): forward, backward and total-task presentation, with the training step moving as steps are mastered.

The decision rules are the form’s working conventions and they are printed beside the graph so they can be changed: at criterion for the set number of consecutive sessions and instructors, the skill is mastered and moves to maintenance and generalization probes; no change over five teaching sessions means the procedure is changed (the prompt level, the delay, the error correction or the reinforcer) rather than continued; a maintenance probe below criterion calls for booster sessions. None of these numbers is a published threshold.

What the Research Supports

Discrete-trial teaching: what it is for and what it is notSmith (2001) describes the trial as a unit (instruction, response, consequence, inter-trial interval) and the method’s strengths: a high rate of learning opportunities, clear data, and the ability to teach discriminations and new response forms that a student does not acquire from the environment. He also lists its limits: skills taught only at the table may not be used elsewhere, students may wait for prompts, and the method must be combined with teaching in natural settings. The format field, the generalization plan and the probes page follow that advice.
Transfer of stimulus control and delayed promptingTouchette and Howard (1984) taught discriminations with a prompt that was delayed a little more over trials, so that the response could occur before the prompt. Control moved from the prompt to the task stimulus, and the transfer was quicker when the reinforcement contingencies favored responses made before the prompt over responses made after it. The form’s separate reinforcement lines for independent and prompted responses, and the time-delay field, come from this.
Most-to-least against least-to-mostLibby, Weiss, Bancroft and Ahearn (2008) compared the two hierarchies while teaching play chains to children with autism. Least-to-most produced acquisition in fewer sessions for some learners at the cost of many more errors; most-to-least produced few errors but was slower for some; most-to-least with a short delay before the prompt combined accuracy with speed. The hierarchy field, the delay field and the fading rule let the team write the arrangement they chose and change it when the error data say so.
Forward and backward chainingSlocum and Tiger (2011) taught chains with forward and backward chaining and found both efficient, with no consistent advantage for either across the children; preference for one method, measured directly, varied by child. The chaining type is therefore a choice the team records, not a rule; total-task presentation is included because it is the common classroom arrangement for a short chain.
Trial-by-trial against first-trial dataLerman, Dittlinger, Fentress and Lanagan (2011) compared data collected on every trial with data collected on the first trial only, and found that first-trial data gave a rough estimate of session performance for most children and skills, but frequently indicated mastery prematurely and were insensitive to early changes in performance. Cummings and Carr (2009) compared continuous and discontinuous (first-trial) measurement in a program for children with autism: skills measured by first trial reached the mastery decision in fewer sessions, and skills measured on every trial were somewhat better maintained. The grid records every trial and flags the first, so a team that must sample can see what the sample would have said.
The mastery criterion predicts maintenanceFuller and Fienup (2018) taught sets to criteria of 50%, 80% and 90% and found that the sets taught to the higher criterion maintained better. Richling, Williams and Carr (2019) compared criteria of 60%, 80% and 100% accuracy, each held across three consecutive sessions, and found that only 100% across three sessions reliably produced maintenance; 80% across three sessions, the criterion practitioners most often reported using, was often insufficient. The default criterion on the Setup page follows these findings and the reason line records any departure.
Error correction is chosen per learnerCarroll, Joachim, St. Peter and Robinson (2015) compared four error-correction procedures during discrete-trial instruction: an error statement, a model of the correct response, a single prompted correct response, and several prompted correct responses. The most efficient procedure differed across the five children, and for most of them a procedure that required an active correct response was more efficient than an error statement or a model alone. The form lists these procedures and asks for the one the team chose and the reason.
Generalization is programmed, not hoped forStokes and Baer (1977) reviewed the generalization literature and named the tactics that worked: train sufficient exemplars, train loosely, program common stimuli, use indiscriminable contingencies, introduce the behavior to natural maintaining contingencies, mediate generalization, and train to generalize, against the common practice of “train and hope”. The generalization plan on the Setup page and the dimension column on the Probes page are the record of which tactics were used and which dimensions were tested.
Probes instead of a continuous baselineHorner and Baer (1978) describe the multiple-probe technique: baseline measured by intermittent probes rather than every session, for skills where a long untreated baseline would be pointless or aversive. The phase code B on the grids marks a probe or baseline session; the phase line on the graph separates it from teaching.
Receptive and discrimination programs need their own careGrow and LeBlanc (2013) give recommendations for receptive-language instruction: balanced arrays with the target in each position equally, rotation of the comparison stimuli, a prompt-fading procedure chosen in advance, interspersal of mastered targets, and a data system that catches position and other faulty stimulus control. The materials, interspersal and error-correction lines are there so these choices are written down.
The prompting procedures by nameThe system of least prompts, constant and progressive time delay, graduated guidance and most-to-least prompting are defined and compared in Wolery, Ault and Doyle (1992), and the response prompts (verbal, model, physical), stimulus control transfer and the three chaining formats are defined in Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020). The code table on the Setup page uses those names and lets the team change the codes to the ones their sheets already use.

How the Figures Are Calculated

Percent independent (trials)Trials coded with an independent code ÷ trials scored in the session × 100. Empty cells are not scored. The number of trials per session sets how many rows the grid reads.
Percent correct (prompted included)Trials coded independent or prompted ÷ trials scored × 100. The gap between the two lines on the graph is the prompted share; a wide gap that does not close is a fading problem.
First trialThe code in trial 1 of each session. The metric reports how many of the last sessions began with an independent response; with the box ticked on the Setup page, the mastery rule also requires it.
Percent of steps independent (task analysis)Steps coded independent ÷ steps scored in the session × 100, over the steps listed.
Step status and current training stepA step is mastered when it was independent on the last two sessions in which it was scored (working convention). Forward chaining: the current training step is the first step not mastered; backward chaining: the last; total-task: every step. An entry in the override field replaces the computed step.
Mastery ruleThe last N teaching-phase sessions (N from the criterion) all at or above the criterion percent, with at least the set number of different instructor initials among them (and, if ticked, an independent first trial in each). Maintenance-phase and baseline-phase sessions are not counted.
No-change ruleAt least five teaching sessions, none of the last five at criterion, and the last of the five fewer than 10 percentage points above the first of the five: the form says to change the procedure. The five-session window and the 10-point margin are working conventions.
Error ruleWhen the last three teaching sessions average under 50% correct (prompted included) under a least-to-most hierarchy, the form suggests a most-to-least or delayed-prompt arrangement (Libby et al., 2008; Touchette & Howard, 1984). The 50% figure is a working convention.
Time-delay ruleUnder constant or progressive time delay the Delay row under the trial grid says the delay each session ran, and the form reads the latest teaching session: a 0-s session with every trial correct moves to the delay, a delayed session with more than 20% of trials in error returns to 0 s for a session, and under progressive delay a session at or above the criterion percent correct with no error lengthens the delay by its next step (working conventions after Wolery, Ault, & Doyle, 1992).
Maintenance ruleA maintenance-phase session or a maintenance probe below the criterion percent calls for booster sessions.
Probe resultIndependent ÷ trials × 100, passed at or above the criterion percent (working convention). The metrics count maintenance probes passed, the generalization dimensions probed and passed, and the settings or material sets passed against the number on the Setup page: a passed generalization probe whose dimension is Setting or Materials counts once per distinct condition.
Instructor countDistinct non-blank initials in the instructor row of the sessions in the mastery window. Blank initials count as no instructor, so the mastery verdict says when initials are missing.

Running the Sheet

Score the response, not the correctionThe code in the cell is what happened after the SD, before any correction. A trial that is corrected keeps its original code; the correction is procedure, not data.
Baseline before teachingTwo probe sessions under the probe rule (working convention) before any prompting, so the graph shows what teaching added. A baseline already at criterion means a harder target.
Two instructors earlyThe criterion asks for two instructors; the second one should start running sessions before the end, not after it, so that generalization across people is being taught rather than tested.
Fade on the dataThe fading rule on the Setup page is applied after each session; the session note records the level used. A prompt level that stays for many sessions is itself a reason to look at the reinforcer.
Keep the sheet with the programSave the data file with the program name in it; the CSV export carries every session, step and probe into a spreadsheet; the PDF print is the record for the file.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated program is a break-request (FCT) program taught by discrete trials with most-to-least prompting, two baseline probes, nine teaching sessions rising to criterion with two instructors, one maintenance session, an eight-step hand-washing task analysis in forward chaining, and four probes, one of them below criterion.
