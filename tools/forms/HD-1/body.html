Student, Family, and the Plan the Sheets Serve

Home data are kept by people who are busy and who did not choose this job. Everything the family is asked to write is decided here, in plain words, and printed on the sheet itself. Keep the sheets to the fewest behaviors, routines and marks that will answer the question the team has.

The Behaviors, in Plain Words

One to four behaviors from Forms TB-1 and TD-1, written as the family would say them. One example and one non-example each, so two people watching the same thing would mark it the same way. The measure decides what the sheet asks for: a tally mark each time, a yes or no for each routine, how many minutes, or a 0 to 3 rating. Match the school’s measure (Form TB-1). The rule for marking it says exactly what one mark is: what one time is for a count (three quick hits: one or three?), when a timed behavior starts and when it ends, what 0 and 3 mean on a rating; it prints in the sheet’s instructions. The last column is the one line from the plan that tells the family what to do when it happens; it prints on every sheet (not on baseline sheets).

Typing the Returned Sheets In

One week at a time, exactly as the family marked it: a count, a Y or N, minutes, or a 0 to 3 rating for each behavior in each routine on each day. Type a 0 where the family wrote 0, and a dash (-) where they marked that the routine did not happen that day (or press Routine did not happen (—) and tap the boxes). Leave a cell empty when nothing was written: an empty cell is a gap, not a zero, and a dash is neither (it is not asked for). The totals, the graph and the completeness figure are computed from what is here.

Routine Checklist, This Week

Each step as the family circled it: Y, N, or a dash where the routine did not happen. The day’s figure is the steps marked Y ÷ the steps marked Y or N. It is the home side of the treatment-integrity record (Form TI-1): a tally that improves while these stay at N says the plan is not what changed. It is the family’s own report of what they did, so read it beside an observed check, not in place of one.

ABC Notes, This Week

One row per note the family wrote: the day, the time, the routine, the boxes ticked before, what the child did and after, and their line in their own words. The counts below show what came before and after across every week typed in.

Sleep Log, This Week

Each row is a night and the morning after it (“Sun night → Mon” is filled in on Monday morning). Hours asleep: from falling asleep to waking for the day, less the time awake in the night. Beside it, the next morning’s marks from the morning routine.

Note Back to the Family, This Week

Fixed sentences from the numbers typed in for this week, with the week before beside them. Read it over and change what you like before it goes home; nothing is sent from here.

By Day, Across the Weeks Entered

One panel per behavior: the day total (count, yes-count, minutes or mean rating) on each day a value was entered, then the checklist’s steps done and the hours asleep when they were typed in. Weeks are laid end to end in the order of their start dates; a day with no entry is a gap, not a zero. A solid line marks a change of condition (baseline, plan, plan changed); an open point is a day on which not every routine on the sheet was marked.

Agreement Check

When a staff member (or the BCBA on a home visit) watched the same routine the family was marking, enter both values. Each person writes down their own mark before seeing the other’s. For a count or minutes, agreement is the smaller value divided by the larger; for a 0 to 3 rating, the two agree only when they are the same number (within one point is shown as well); a Y/N pair agrees or it does not. It is reported for each behavior, and the verdict rests on the lowest one and on every behavior having a check. A check by phone hears the behavior but does not see it, so it is shown and not counted. At or above 80% is treated as acceptable, as a working convention; below it, re-teach the definitions with the family and hold that behavior’s home data from decisions until a check reaches 80%.

What This Form Builds

A home data sheet is a measurement system handed to people who are not trained observers, who are tired by bedtime, and who care more about the child than about the data. The form takes that seriously. The BCBA makes every decision in advance (what to watch, in which routines, with what mark, how often it comes back and how), and the sheet carries those decisions on its face in large plain type, with an example row already filled in, the one line from the plan that says what to do, and the name and number to call. The family’s job is reduced to a mark at the end of a routine.

The four sheets answer four different questions. The weekly tally sheet asks how often, how long, or how hard a behavior was in each routine of the day; it is the sheet for a baseline at home and for watching a plan work. The routine checklist asks whether the agreed plan steps and the child’s replacement skills happened; it is the home version of the treatment-integrity record (Form TI-1), and a tally that improves while the checklist stays empty tells the team the plan is not what changed. The ABC note sheet turns a confusing moment into three tick boxes and one sentence; a week of them is the home side of the functional assessment (Forms OB-1 and IA-1). The sleep log records what the school cannot see, and a hard morning is often explained on the previous night’s row (Form MS-1 keeps the medical side).

The Entry page is where the paper becomes data. The BCBA types the sheets in as they came, with no cleaning: the behavior grid, the checklist’s Y and N marks, each ABC note and each night of the sleep log. The form computes the day totals, the steps done each day, the before-and-after counts and the hours asleep beside the next morning, draws the graph with the condition of each week (baseline, plan, plan changed), scores agreement for each behavior when a staff observation overlapped a home one, writes the week’s note back to the family, and keeps a record of which sheets came back and how complete they were. Each week keeps the design its sheets went home with, so a shorter sheet next month does not change a baseline already typed in. The completeness figure is a result in its own right: a family that returns full sheets for three weeks has told the team something about contextual fit (Form CF-1), and a family that does not has told the team to shorten the sheet, not to try harder.

What the Research Supports

Parents can be taught to run behavioral procedures, and the format mattersLerman, Swiezy, Perkins-Parks and Roane (2000) taught parents of children with developmental disabilities several skills and compared instructional formats. Written instructions alone were enough for some skills and not for others; skills with more components needed modeling, rehearsal and feedback. The lesson for this form: a sheet is written instructions. The row “How the family was taught the sheet” is there so the first week is practiced together, and Form CT-1 carries the training when the plan steps on the checklist need more than a sentence.
Structured parent training beats information aloneIn a randomized trial of 180 children with autism spectrum disorder and disruptive behavior, 24 weeks of parent training (a manualized program of behavioral strategies with practice and feedback) reduced parent-rated irritability and noncompliance more than a parent education program that gave information without strategies (Bearss et al., 2015). The sheets on this form are the data arm of that kind of program: they tell the trainer what is happening between sessions and give the family something to bring to each one.
Parent management training rests on parent-collected dataKazdin’s (2005) account of parent management training describes the program’s reliance on home records: parents define the behavior, count it, and bring the count to the session, where praise and points are adjusted on what the record shows. The tally sheet and the return schedule reproduce that cycle at the scale a school-based team can run.
Positive behavior support in families is built around routinesLucyshyn, Dunlap and Albin (2002) and Hieneman, Childs and Sergay (2006) organize family-centered positive behavior support around the routines of the day: a plan is written for a routine, run in that routine, and judged by how that routine goes. The routine rows on every sheet, and the step list grouped by routine on the checklist, follow that structure, so the data say not just how much but when.
Conjoint consultation makes the family a partner in the dataConjoint behavioral consultation (Sheridan, Kratochwill & Bergan, 1996) brings parents and teachers into one problem-solving sequence (problem identification, analysis, plan implementation, evaluation) with data collected in both settings. Home sheets that match the school’s measures are what make the cross-setting comparison possible; the agreement check on the Entry page is the point where the two records meet.
The family’s view of the sheet is a resultWolf (1978) argued that the goals, procedures and effects of a program should be judged acceptable by the people who live with them. A data sheet is a procedure. The row for what the family said about it, and the completeness figure, are the social-validity record for the sheets themselves; a sheet the family dislikes or does not return has failed on Wolf’s terms whatever it would have measured.
Measurement at home is still measurementThe definitions, the choice of a count, a duration or a rating, the interobserver agreement check and the rule that a missing day is a gap and not a zero follow the measurement chapters of Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020). A rating of 0 to 3 is the least precise of the four measures and is meant only for behaviors that resist counting (bedtime difficulty, agitation); the setup check warns when a rating is chosen for a behavior that can be counted, and asks for what 0 and 3 mean. For the family to follow the gap-not-zero rule, the sheet tells them to write 0 when the behavior did not happen and a dash when the routine did not happen, so a blank means only that nothing was marked.

How the Figures Are Calculated

Day total, tallyThe sum of the counts across the routines marked that day, with the number of routines marked when it is fewer than the routines held that day (a routine marked with a dash did not happen and is not counted).
Day total, yes or noThe number of routines marked Y, shown as Y of the routines marked (N counts as marked; an empty cell does not).
Day total, minutesThe sum of the minutes across the routines marked that day, with the routines marked as for a tally.
Day total, 0 to 3 ratingThe mean of the ratings given that day, to one decimal.
Week totalThe sum of the day totals (for a rating, the mean of the day means). Days with nothing written are left out, not counted as zero.
CompletenessCells with something written ÷ cells the sheet asked for (behaviors × routines on the sheet × days on the sheet, less the cells marked with a dash because the routine did not happen) × 100, per week, from the design that week’s sheets went home with; and the number of weeks for which each sheet came back, out of the weeks it was sent.
AgreementFor a count or minutes: the smaller value ÷ the larger × 100 (two zeros agree at 100%), the total count and total duration comparisons of Cooper, Heron and Heward (2020). For a 0 to 3 rating: 100% when the two ratings are the same, else 0%; agreement within one point is shown beside it. For Y/N: 100% when both say the same, else 0%. Reported as the mean for each behavior with the number of checks; checks by phone are shown and not counted. The verdict rests on the lowest behavior and names any behavior with no check. 80% is the working convention for acceptable; it is not a published threshold for home data.
Checklist, steps doneFor each day: the steps marked Y ÷ the steps marked Y or N × 100 (a dash, the routine did not happen, and an empty box are left out).
Sleep, hours asleepFrom the time the child fell asleep (or lights out, when that is all that is written) to the time they woke for the day, less the minutes awake in the night. A night is flagged as short when it is an hour or more under the child’s own middle (median) night on the log, and as broken with two or more wakings or 30 minutes or more awake; both are working conventions, not sleep standards.
GraphWeeks are ordered by their start date (weeks without a readable date are placed after the dated ones in the order entered); a start that is not a Monday is moved to that week’s Monday (a Sunday to the next day), the same date the case receives. Each day with a value is a point; consecutive days are joined; a day with no entry breaks the line. An open point is a day on which fewer routines were marked than were held. A dashed line marks a new week, a solid labeled line a change of condition, and “sheet changed” a week whose sheets had other days, routines or behaviors.

What Keeps the Sheets Coming Back

Ask for less than you wantOne behavior, five routines and seven days is 35 marks a week. Two behaviors double it. Start with the fewest marks that answer the question and add a sheet only when the first one comes back full for two weeks. The Home sheets page counts the marks the sheets ask for each week and the pages they print, and warns above 70 marks (a working convention).
Put the sheet where the routine endsOn the fridge for meals, by the bed for bedtime, in the car for outings. A sheet in a folder is filled in from memory on Friday.
Something comes back the other wayA thank-you at every return and the graph with two sentences every week. A family that sees its data used keeps sending it. Write the note from this week on the Entry page drafts those sentences from the numbers, to read over and change before it goes home.
Keeping the sheets is the family’s choiceRecord when and how the family agreed to keep them (BACB Ethics Code 2.09 and 2.11, involving the family and their informed consent), and every sheet says that keeping it is their choice and that stopping will not change the child’s services.
The sheet is not a report card for the parentThe checklist asks whether the step happened, not whether the parent was good. A week of No is information about the step, the routine or the training, and the conversation says so.
An empty week is dataRecord it on the Entry page as returned-but-empty or not returned, with the reason the family gave. Three such weeks mean the sheet changes, not the family.
Match the school’s measureIf the school counts, the home counts; if the school rates, the home rates on the same scale. Only then can the two records be compared or graphed together.

Writing help

A multi-line text box on this form shows an Improve wording button at its corner when you are in it and whenever it holds text; from a keyboard, Alt+Enter in the box (Option+Return on a Mac or iPad) opens the same panel. Check wording works on this device and sends nothing: it marks words that name a feeling, guess at intent, function or a diagnosis, label the behavior or the person, or leave a count, time or intensity vague, and suggests observable wording; words in quotation marks are left alone. Rewrite with Claude works only in the forms opened from the website: a single-use passcode from your BCBA unlocks the tab (in the workstation, every form in that tab) until the session’s time is up or the tab is closed; press Lock when you finish on a shared iPad. Before anything is sent, the student’s name and ID become placeholders such as [Student], other names can be added under Also hide, and the panel shows exactly what will be sent; the names are put back in the answer. Use it for real cases only with your district’s or agency’s approval. On an iPad with Apple Intelligence, Writing Tools (select the text) can also proofread and rewrite; it is Apple’s own and works on the text as written, names included, on the iPad or on Apple’s servers, so follow your agency’s rules for it and do not use its ChatGPT options for student text. The button and the panel are not saved with the form and never print.

About the simulation

Load simulation (toolbar) replaces every entry with a worked example; nothing in it is real data. The simulated family is a second-grader with three behaviors (a count, minutes, and a 0 to 3 rating) across five routines, four home sheets, three weeks typed in (the behavior grid, two weeks of the checklist, four ABC notes and twenty nights of the sleep log; the checklist was not sent in week 3), a plan change in week 2, four agreement checks (one by phone, so not counted), and the sheets-returned record.
